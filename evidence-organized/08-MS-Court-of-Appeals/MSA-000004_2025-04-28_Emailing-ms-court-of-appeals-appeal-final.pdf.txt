Statement of the Issues
On June 28, 2024, the Circuit Court Judge dismissed Appellant Matthew O. Reardon’s appeal of
his Justice Court convictions, based upon his finding that Appellant never timely perfected the
appeal by posting the required bonds or filing a proper Affidavit of Poverty within the thirty-day
period. As a result, the Circuit Court Judge concluded that the court was without jurisdiction and
dismissed the appeal. See C.P. 76–78. This ruling is deeply troubling to Appellant because the
dispositive facts were clearly reflected in the record and brought to the court’s attention multiple
times, yet were ignored. The following issues are presented to this Court for review:
Issue I:
Whether the Circuit Court Judge erred in dismissing the appeal despite the fact that Appellant was
expressly excused from the requirement to pay costs, post a cost bond, or post an appearance bond
to appeal his Justice Court convictions, by order of the Justice Court (lower court) Judge filed
December 3, 2021, which found Appellant indigent by reason of poverty. See C.P. 38.
Issue II:
Whether the Justice Court and the Circuit Court erred by improperly dismissing Appellant’s court-
appointed counsel prior to the appeal being perfected, in contravention of Miss. Code Ann. § 99-
15-15 and Mississippi Rule of Criminal Procedure 7.2(c).
Issue III:
Whether the Circuit Court’s dismissal of this appeal has resulted in a manifest injustice to
Appellant through a denial of due process.
Statement of Assignment
It is appropriate to assign this case to the Mississippi Court of Appeals. This appeal does not
involve any issue within the Supreme Court’s exclusive jurisdiction. See M.R.A.P. 16(b). Nor does
it involve “a major question of first impression” (M.R.A.P. 16(d)(1)) or any other issue that would
ordinarily cause the Supreme Court to retain the case. See M.R.A.P. 16(d)(2)–(4). Indeed, the
primary issue in this appeal could not be clearer from the Record. Mississippi law is unambiguous
that the justice court (lower court) judge determines the amount of any cash deposit or bond
required for an appeal from justice court. See M.R.Cr.P. 29.3, 29.4. In this case, the Justice Court
Judge expressly waived all such bond requirements by finding Appellant indigent in the order filed
December 3, 2021 (C.P. 38). That indigency determination rendered any cost bond or appearance
bond requirement inapplicable. Consequently, the sole basis relied upon by the Circuit Court Judge
in the June 28, 2024 order dismissing the justice court appeal is without merit. See C.P. 78. This
case involves the straightforward application of settled law to the facts, making it suitable for
decision by the Court of Appeals.
Statement Regarding Oral Argument
Given the clarity of the controlling facts and law, this appeal should be resolved in Appellant’s
favor on the briefs. However, out of an abundance of caution, Appellant respectfully requests oral
argument. Oral argument would allow Appellant to address any questions the Court may have and
to further emphasize the profound due process concerns raised by the lower courts’ actions in this
case. If the Court believes oral argument would aid its decision, Appellant welcomes the
opportunity to elaborate on the issues presented.
Statement of the Case
This case arises from the Circuit Court of Lafayette County’s dismissal of Appellant Matthew O.
Reardon’s appeal of several misdemeanor convictions from Lafayette County Justice Court. The
Justice Court convictions were entered on November 24, 2021, following a bench trial. Appellant
timely initiated his appeal to the Circuit Court on December 3, 2021, just nine days after the
judgment. Importantly, Appellant filed the notice of appeal pro se, without the assistance of any
attorney, because the Justice Court Judge had terminated Appellant’s court-appointed trial counsel
in an order signed December 2, 2021 (the day after the convictions). See C.P. 38. In that same
December 2, 2021 order (filed in the Circuit Court record on December 3, 2021), Justice Court
Judge Chris Childers expressly found that Appellant “is indigent and by reason of poverty cannot
offer security required by law and is unable to give bond to appeal the judgment against him. Mr.
Reardon is allowed to appeal if he desires to do so, and execution of judgment will be stayed
without the requirement of supersedeas bond.” Id. (emphasis added). This lower court order plainly
granted Appellant in forma pauperis status for purposes of appeal and waived any appeal bond
requirements.
Consistent with that order, Appellant’s initial filing in the Circuit Court on December 3, 2021
included a cover page noting the appeal from Justice Court (C.P. 8) and an accompanying Motion
for Relief (C.P. 9–20) detailing the fatal flaws and injustices that had occurred during the Justice
Court trial. Crucially, the final sentence of that motion explicitly stated: “In the interest of judicial
economy, please take judicial notice of this motion to serve as Defendant’s Notice of Intent to
Appeal the Judgment/Order.” See C.P. 20 (emphasis added). In other words, Appellant made clear,
in writing and at the outset, that this motion was to be treated as his notice of appeal.
On December 27, 2021, to eliminate any doubt about the notice of appeal, Appellant prepared a
separate formal Notice of Appeal for a trial de novo in Circuit Court. He filed this notice in Justice
Court (where it was stamped as received) and attempted to file the stamped notice in the Circuit
Court case. However, the Lafayette County Circuit Clerk refused to accept or docket any further
filings in the open appeal case unless Appellant paid a filing fee of approximately $150. Appellant
showed the clerk the Justice Court’s order declaring him indigent and allowing an appeal in forma
pauperis, but to no avail. The clerk insisted that no additional documents could be filed in the case
unless the fee was paid. Lacking the funds, Appellant was effectively barred from filing the formal
notice of appeal on that date. The clerk indicated that apart from paying the fee, Appellant would
have to wait until a hearing was held on the pending motion (C.P. 9–20) before he could file
anything further.
On January 7, 2022, still acting pro se, Appellant returned to the clerk and was informed that filing
a new Affidavit of Poverty would satisfy the “filing fee” requirement and allow him to file
additional documents. This was also presented as a prerequisite for appointment of appellate
counsel. Accordingly, Appellant filed an Affidavit of Poverty on January 7, 2022 (C.P. 33). He
also filed a Motion for Hearing Date for Decision on Motion (C.P. 35) regarding his initial post-
conviction motion, and a Motion to Schedule Hearing for De Novo Appeal (C.P. 36). In the
latter motion, Appellant notified the Circuit Court of the clerk’s December 27, 2021 refusal to
accept his formal Notice of Appeal and of the Justice Court’s order allowing an in forma pauperis
appeal. See C.P. 37. Attached to that motion were copies of the Justice Court’s indigency order
(C.P. 38) and the formal stamped Notice of Appeal that Appellant had attempted to file on
December 27, 2021 (C.P. 39). Thus, by early January 2022, the Circuit Court record contained
multiple documents making it unmistakable that (1) the Justice Court had waived any appeal bond
or costs due to Appellant’s indigency, and (2) Appellant had provided notice of appeal within the
time allowed, both through his initial motion and the attempted filing of the formal notice.
On January 10, 2022, Appellant filed a Motion to Seal IFP Affidavit (C.P. 40) after discovering
that his Affidavit of Poverty had been made publicly accessible in the court file, exposing his
sensitive personal information (including his social security number and his minor children’s
names). In this motion, Appellant again reminded the court that he had already been granted in
forma pauperis status by the Justice Court’s order, and he expressed concern over the clerk’s
careless handling of his confidential information.
On March 30, 2022, Appellant (still pro se) filed a Petition to Appoint Counsel (C.P. 42) to
ensure he had representation for the appeal. When no immediate action was taken, on April 7,
2022, Appellant filed a Notice of Hearing on Entered Motions and Petitions (C.P. 49) and
served it on the county prosecutor, seeking to have the court address the outstanding motions. A
hearing on these matters was scheduled for April 12, 2022. Five days before the hearing, on April
7, 2022, the County Attorney (prosecutor) filed a written response (C.P. 51–59) arguing inter alia
that Appellant’s initial notice of appeal did not comply with the formal requirements of M.R.Cr.P.
29.1. See C.P. 52 at ¶7.
A hearing was conducted on April 12, 2022, before Circuit Judge John Kelly Luther. The transcript
of this hearing is in the record at C.P. 105–137. During this hearing, the issue of the appeal bonds
was definitively settled on the record: Judge Chris Childers of the Justice Court had approved
Appellant’s appeal without the payment of any costs, bonds, or security by virtue of the finding
of indigency. Judge Luther acknowledged this fact on the record, stating “on December the 2nd
Judge Childers says that you can appeal without a bond.” See C.P. 115–116 (emphasis added).
Judge Luther reiterated moments later that “Judge Childers has already addressed the issue of
whether or not you can proceed without paying any money.” See C.P. 127. With that issue
resolved, two matters remained to be addressed at the hearing: (1) whether there were any
deficiencies in Appellant’s notice of appeal that needed to be cured (with notice given under
M.R.Cr.P. 29.1(c)), and (2) the appointment of appellate counsel for Appellant. During the hearing,
Judge Luther held that Appellant was entitled to counsel under Miss. Code Ann. § 99-15-15, and
re-appointed attorney Mitchell Driskell (Appellant’s trial counsel in Justice Court) to represent
Appellant on the appeal. See C.P. 124–130.
Notwithstanding these proper rulings, Judge Luther made a startling remark during the hearing,
stating to Appellant: “I would be lying if I told you I wanted your case. Because I don’t — you,
just being honest with you, you have a reputation of being difficult to deal with. And if I got half a
chance to dodge you, I would dodge you like a speeding bullet.” See C.P. 131. This gratuitous
comment was a cause for great concern. Appellant has at all times simply sought a fair trial and
fair treatment by the courts. This April 12, 2022 hearing was Appellant’s first substantive
appearance before Judge Luther, which makes the judge’s negative characterization of Appellant’s
“reputation” puzzling and troubling. Appellant is left to wonder on what basis the judge formed
this preconceived opinion, and it raised questions about the judge’s impartiality going forward.
On April 18, 2022, shortly after the hearing, Appellant’s re-appointed counsel Mr. Driskell filed a
Corrected Notice of Appeal and Demand for Jury Trial (C.P. 61–68). This filing was explicitly
made “pursuant to M.R.Cr.P. 29.1(c) to preemptively correct any deficiencies in [Mr. Reardon’s
pro se] Notice of Appeal and to alleviate the necessity of the court directing the clerk to issue a
written notice of deficiency.” See C.P. 61–62.* In other words, counsel proactively cured any
arguable technical defects in Appellant’s earlier pro se notice of appeal, as contemplated by the
Rules.
On May 20, 2022, the County Attorney filed a response to the Corrected Notice of Appeal and
Jury Trial Demand (C.P. 69–71). The County Attorney contended that the notice improperly
sought to appeal “two separate matters” (specifically, charges arising from incidents on December
28, 2020 and February 21, 2021). In reality, however, all of those misdemeanor charges were
consolidated and tried together in a single proceeding in Justice Court on November 4, 2021. The
prosecutor had voiced no objection to trying them together at that time. The County Attorney
further argued that the judgment on the Justice Court convictions was entered November 24, 2021
and that Appellant did not perfect an appeal within 30 days for one set of charges. Essentially, the
State was attempting to parse the appeal timeline even though the cases had been merged for trial.
Regardless, any issue about timeliness had been resolved by Judge Luther’s allowance at the April
hearing for the notice of appeal to be corrected, and by the State’s own acknowledgment that it
had no objection to reinstating the appeal (as noted below).
On May 31, 2024 – after a lengthy period of inactivity by the court – the Circuit Court Clerk (not
the prosecutor) filed a Motion to Dismiss for Want of Prosecution. See C.P. 72. In response, on
June 11, 2024, Appellant’s appointed counsel filed a Motion to Reinstate Case (C.P. 73–75). In
that motion, counsel pointed out that the issues before the court remained: (1) whether the original
pro se filings had perfected an appeal; if not, (2) whether the Corrected Notice of Appeal perfected
the appeal; and if an appeal was perfected, then (3) whether Appellant is entitled to a trial by jury
on the appeal. Counsel explained that no further action had been taken because those issues were
awaiting the court’s rulings. Counsel also moved to withdraw from representation and for a new
public defender to be appointed, noting that he was no longer with the public defender’s office and
had a potential conflict (his new employment was with a law firm that had been adverse to
Appellant in other matters). Counsel noted in the motion that the State (through the county
prosecutor) had no objection to reinstating the appeal and no objection to his withdrawal and
substitution of new counsel.
Despite the State’s lack of objection and the clear evidence that Appellant had diligently pursued
his appeal, on June 28, 2024 the Circuit Court entered an order dismissing Appellant’s appeal. See
C.P. 76–78. The court’s order reasoned that “an appeal from the Justice Court to Circuit Court is
perfected by simultaneously filing a written notice of appeal, a cost bond, and an appearance bond
(or cash deposit) with the clerk of the Circuit Court having jurisdiction within thirty (30) days of
the judgment,” quoting M.R.Cr.P. 29.1(a). The court found that “Mr. Reardon never timely
perfected an appeal.” The order further stated that even if counsel’s corrected notice of appeal
“cured” any deficiencies in the initial pro se notice, Appellant failed to “timely submit either: (1)
an Affidavit of Poverty; or (2) a Cost Bond or Appearance Bond.” See C.P. 77–78. The court noted
that the Affidavit of Poverty filed on January 7, 2022 was outside the thirty-day window. On that
basis, the Circuit Court concluded it was “without jurisdiction” to hear the appeal. Id. (It should
be noted that the Circuit Court’s order made no mention of the Justice Court’s indigency ruling at
C.P. 38 or Judge Luther’s own on-record acknowledgment that no bond was required.)
Compounding matters, in the same June 28, 2024 order, Judge Luther granted attorney Driskell’s
motion to withdraw and terminated Appellant’s representation, without appointing substitute
counsel for the purposes of further proceedings. As a result, for the second time stemming from
the same underlying case, Appellant was left to “navigate the waters” of the appellate process pro
se, without the assistance of counsel, at a critical juncture. This occurred despite the fact that
Appellant had never waived his right to counsel and indeed had been actively seeking counsel’s
appointment. After the dismissal, on July 18, 2024, Appellant (acting pro se once more) took all
necessary steps to appeal the dismissal: he filed a Notice of Appeal to the Mississippi Supreme
Court (C.P. 81), a Designation of the Record (C.P. 82), a Motion to Proceed In Forma Pauperis on
appeal (C.P. 83), and a Motion for Appointment of Counsel for the appeal (C.P. 85). These filings
perfected the present appeal to this Court.
On September 17, 2024, the Circuit Court Judge entered an order denying Appellant’s request for
appointment of counsel on this appeal (C.P. 102), stating that Appellant is not entitled to further
court-appointed counsel in this matter. Id. Appellant has therefore prosecuted this appeal on his
own behalf. In doing so, he seeks relief from this Court for the multiple violations of his rights that
occurred below.
Summary of the Argument
The Circuit Court of Lafayette County dismissed Appellant’s de novo appeal from his Justice
Court convictions on the ground that Appellant failed to timely submit either an Affidavit of
Poverty or the required appeal bonds (cost bond and appearance bond). The record, however,
makes abundantly clear that the usual requirements of posting such bonds did not apply in this
case because the Justice Court had formally excused Appellant from any requirement to pay costs
or provide security for his appeal. The lower court’s December 2, 2021 order expressly found
Appellant indigent and explicitly allowed him to appeal without posting a bond. See C.P. 38. Thus,
there were no appeal bonds for Appellant to post in the first place.
Mississippi appellate courts have consistently held that once a defendant is properly determined to
be indigent, that status and its accompanying protections should be honored throughout the
proceedings unless there is evidence of a change in circumstances. In Jones v. State, 912 So.2d
973 (Miss. 2005), the Mississippi Supreme Court emphasized that once a defendant has been
declared indigent, that determination should be respected through all stages of the case absent
evidence of a material improvement in the defendant’s financial situation. This principle was
recently reaffirmed in Jefferson v. State, 304 So.3d 1139, 1144 (Miss. 2020). Here, there was no
evidence (or even an allegation) of any change in Appellant’s financial circumstances after the
Justice Court’s indigency finding. In fact, Judge Luther himself acknowledged Appellant’s
indigency and bond waiver on the record during the April 12, 2022 hearing. Despite this, the
Circuit Court later inexplicably dismissed the appeal for lack of bond—an issue that had already
been conclusively resolved in Appellant’s favor.
Furthermore, it is undisputed that the Justice Court Judge never set any cash deposit or bond
amount for the appeal. He couldn’t have, given that he explicitly waived those requirements. The
absence of any bond amount set by the lower court underscores that a cost bond and appearance
bond were never necessary to perfect this appeal; they were effectively waived by the lower court’s
indigency order. Appellant’s filing of an Affidavit of Poverty on January 7, 2022 was done out of
an abundance of caution (to satisfy the clerk and to facilitate the appointment of counsel) – it was
not a jurisdictional prerequisite for the appeal under these circumstances. The Circuit Court’s
reasoning in dismissing the appeal is therefore without legal or factual merit and should be reversed
on this issue alone.
In addition to this primary issue, Appellant argues that the lower courts erred by depriving him of
his right to counsel at a critical stage. Specifically, the Justice Court and later the Circuit Court
improperly terminated Appellant’s court-appointed counsel before his appeal was perfected. This
occurred not once but twice in the course of this case: first when the Justice Court terminated
counsel immediately after trial, and again when the Circuit Court allowed counsel to withdraw
upon dismissing the appeal (without appointing new counsel). Appellant will show that a
defendant who is entitled to appointed counsel is entitled to continuous representation through all
stages of the proceedings, including the preparation and filing of a notice of appeal. This principle
is supported by the plain language of Miss. Code Ann. § 99-15-15 and M.R.Cr.P. 7, as well as
Article 3, Section 26 of the Mississippi Constitution. The right to assistance of counsel is also
guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution (made applicable to the states
through the Fourteenth Amendment) and by Mississippi Constitution Article 3, Section 26. This
Court is asked to reaffirm and enforce that right in the context of this case, where the lack of
counsel contributed to procedural pitfalls.
Finally, Appellant contends that the dismissal of his appeal, combined with the extraordinary
delays and procedural obstacles he has faced, has resulted in a manifest injustice and a denial of
due process. The original misdemeanor charges are now over four years old (and will be
approximately five years old by the time this appeal is decided). Such an excessive delay—largely
attributable to court inaction—undermines the integrity of any eventual trial de novo and has
prejudiced Appellant’s ability to defend himself. The lengthy delay, the denial of counsel at critical
times, and the erroneous dismissal of the appeal together amount to a fundamental unfairness that
this Court cannot countenance. For these reasons, Appellant respectfully asks this Court to reverse
the Circuit Court’s June 28, 2024 dismissal order. Moreover, given the clear record and the
multiple violations of Appellant’s rights, Appellant urges this Court to take the further step of
rendering a judgment of acquittal on the misdemeanor charges (which would terminate this
matter). Alternatively, at a minimum, the case should be reversed and remanded for a proper de
novo appeal trial in the Circuit Court, with all due process rights safeguarded.
ARGUMENT
I. THE CIRCUIT COURT JUDGE ERRED IN DISMISSING THIS APPEAL
BASED ON FAILURE TO POST BONDS THAT WERE EXPLICITLY
WAIVED BY THE JUSTICE COURT
In dismissing Appellant’s appeal on June 28, 2024, the Circuit Court reasoned that Appellant failed
to timely submit an Affidavit of Poverty or to post a cost bond and an appearance bond. However,
the record conclusively demonstrates that the usual requirement of filing such bonds was
inapplicable to this appeal. Appellant was excused from any obligation to pay costs or provide
bond as a condition of appeal by virtue of the Justice Court’s order finding him indigent. See C.P.
38. That indigency order, entered by the very court whose judgment was being appealed, expressly
allowed Appellant to appeal without posting security or bond. It is a “key fact” in this case, and it
appears in the record multiple times.
Not only is this fact reflected in the written record (C.P. 38, 37, 40, 61), it was also raised repeatedly
in open court. At the April 12, 2022 hearing, Appellant himself reminded the court of the indigency
order four separate times. See Hearing Tr. at C.P. 114 (lines 16–18), 115 (lines 4–5), 116 (lines 4–
8), 123 (lines 21–25). More significantly, Judge Luther acknowledged this fact on the record,
stating: “on December the 2nd Judge Childers says that you can appeal without a bond.” See C.P.
116 (lines 9–10). Judge Luther reiterated this acknowledgment later in the hearing: “Judge
Childers has already addressed the issue of whether or not you can proceed without paying any
money.” See C.P. 127 (lines 19–21). Thus, by the judge’s own words, the question of any bond
requirement was answered: no bond was required for Appellant’s appeal.
Mississippi law is clear that indigency determinations and resulting waivers of costs or bond
obligations must be respected by subsequent courts in the same matter. The Mississippi Supreme
Court has consistently held that once a defendant is deemed indigent, he should not have to re-
prove his indigency at each step of the process absent a bona fide change in circumstances. Jones
v. State, 912 So.2d 973 (Miss. 2005), emphasized this point, and the principle was echoed in
Caston v. State, 823 So.2d 473, 503 (Miss. 2002). In Caston, the Court noted that a lower court’s
finding of indigency “should carry forward to the appellate process unless there is clear evidence
of a change in the defendant’s financial status.” Similarly, the Mississippi Court of Appeals has
held that “once a determination of indigency has been made, the defendant does not have to
repeatedly prove his indigency at various stages of the proceedings.” Eggleston v. State, 800
So.2d 1234, 1236 (Miss. Ct. App. 2001). More recently, in Jefferson v. State, 304 So.3d 1139,
1144 (Miss. 2020), the Mississippi Supreme Court reaffirmed that an indigency finding persists
throughout court proceedings absent evidence of changed circumstances. These holdings are
consistent with the fundamental due process guarantee of access to the courts regardless of one’s
poverty, as embodied in Miss. Const. art. 3, § 14.
Applying these principles here, the Justice Court’s indigency ruling should have been honored by
the Circuit Court. There was no suggestion that Appellant’s financial condition had improved;
indeed, Appellant remained incarcerated for portions of this period and clearly could not afford
any bond. The Justice Court never set any bond amount, which is telling—under M.R.Cr.P. 29.3
and 29.4, the responsibility to set an appeal bond lies with the lower court judge. The absence of a
set bond amount in the record is directly attributable to Judge Childers’s decision to waive the
bond. In effect, the requirement was nullified. Therefore, the Circuit Court’s insistence that a bond
(or a timely poverty affidavit) was needed to perfect the appeal is legally erroneous.
It is worth emphasizing that Appellant’s January 7, 2022 Affidavit of Poverty (C.P. 33) was never
intended as a jurisdictional filing to “perfect” the appeal; rather, it was filed to satisfy the Circuit
Clerk’s administrative demand and to facilitate the appointment of counsel. By January 7, the
appeal had already been timely noticed by the filings in early December 2021. Appellant had every
reason to believe—correctly—based on the Justice Court’s order and the plain language of
M.R.Cr.P. 29, that no cost bond or cash deposit was required. The Circuit Court’s contrary
conclusion ignored the record and misapplied the law.
A. The Circuit Court’s Error Constitutes Plain Error
Even if one were to assume that Appellant or his counsel should have more explicitly re-asserted
the indigency protection, the Circuit Court’s dismissal of the appeal in the face of an existing
indigency order amounts to plain error. The Mississippi Supreme Court in Brown v. State, 995
So.2d 698, 703 (Miss. 2008), defined plain error as an error that “affects the defendant’s
substantive/fundamental rights, even though no objection was made at trial.” Similarly, in Smith
v. State, 986 So.2d 290, 294 (Miss. 2008), the Court explained that plain error requires a finding
that a defendant’s substantive rights were violated. In McGee v. State, 85 So.3d 275, 282 (Miss.
2012), the Court further articulated that plain error occurs “when a defendant’s substantive or
fundamental rights are affected,” especially in cases where procedural shortcomings impede the
exercise of those fundamental rights.
Mississippi Rule of Appellate Procedure 28(a)(1)(3) specifically empowers appellate courts to
consider “plain errors affecting substantial rights” even if they were not properly raised or
preserved in the trial court. And in Willie v. State, 204 So.3d 1268, 1274 (Miss. 2016), the
Supreme Court noted that procedural bars may be overcome where a fundamental constitutional
right is implicated. Here, the right to appeal is a fundamental component of due process in our
system, particularly where the appeal is de novo and the only avenue to obtain a jury trial on a
criminal charge. Appellant’s right to appeal (and to a jury trial on appeal) was effectively denied
by the Circuit Court’s action. That denial implicates due process and the Sixth Amendment right
to a trial by jury (as applied to the states via the Fourteenth Amendment).
It should have been manifest to the Circuit Court that dismissing this appeal on a bond issue was
error, given that the indigency waiver was established multiple times in the record and openly in
court. All the criteria for plain error review are met here: (1) there was error; (2) the error is clear
and obvious; and (3) the error affects substantial rights. The U.S. Supreme Court has characterized
plain error as an error that is “clear” or “obvious” and that affects the defendant’s “substantial
rights.” Usually, to affect substantial rights, the error must have been prejudicial or have affected
the outcome of the proceeding. See United States v. Duffaut, 314 F.3d 203, 209 (5th Cir. 2002).
The error in this case fits that description. By dismissing the appeal, the Circuit Court denied
Appellant a whole stage of judicial process—indeed, his only opportunity for a trial de novo. This
strikes at the heart of fairness and due process, and it certainly affected the outcome (there was no
appellate review or new trial at all).
An appellate court has discretion to correct plain error that seriously affects the fairness, integrity,
or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Duffaut, 314 F.3d at 209. Here, the denial of key due
process rights—Appellant’s right to an appeal and to a jury trial de novo—has undermined the
fairness and integrity of the judicial process in this case. Appellant has been prejudiced not only
by the loss of his appeal, but also by knock-on effects such as the considerable delay (during which
evidence may have staled). If left uncorrected, the errors in this case would result in a manifest
miscarriage of justice. This Court should not permit such an outcome. Correcting the error and
reinstating (indeed, rendering) the appeal is necessary to restore public confidence that indigent
defendants will receive the process they are due.
Moreover, Mississippi precedent establishes that it is an abuse of discretion for a Circuit Court to
refuse to reinstate an appeal from Justice Court when the facts show that the appellant’s failure to
meet a procedural requirement was not the result of willful neglect or bad faith, and where the
State would not be prejudiced by allowing the appeal to proceed. Kennard v. State, 127 So.2d
848 (Miss. 1961); Sartain v. State, 406 So.2d 43 (Miss. 1981). In Kennard, the Supreme Court
reinstated an appeal that had been dismissed on technical grounds, reasoning that the defendant
had not trifled with the court or acted in bad faith, and that allowing the appeal would not prejudice
the State. The same is true here. Appellant’s conduct was diligent and in good faith at every turn.
Any arguable technical misstep was either induced by the clerk’s actions or promptly cured with
the court’s permission. The State can never be prejudiced by affording a defendant the fair appeal
to which he is entitled—especially not in a de novo appeal, where the State gets a full opportunity
to present its case to a jury if the appeal is allowed. Conversely, it is Appellant who suffers extreme
prejudice by being denied that appeal. Accordingly, refusing to allow this appeal to go forward (in
spite of the indigency waiver and all the facts above) was an abuse of discretion and plain error
that warrants reversal.
In sum, the Circuit Court’s dismissal of the appeal on this ground cannot stand. The requirements
for cost and appearance bonds were explicitly waived, and the court was made aware of that
waiver. The dismissal of Appellant’s appeal despite this waiver was legal error of a fundamental
nature. Accordingly, Issue I should be resolved in Appellant’s favor, and the dismissal
reversed. However, the errors in this case do not end here; Appellant also was deprived of his
right to counsel during this process, as discussed next.
II. THE JUSTICE COURT AND CIRCUIT COURT IMPROPERLY DISMISSED
APPELLANT’S COURT-APPOINTED COUNSEL PRIOR TO APPEAL
The second major error in this case is that both the Justice Court and the Circuit Court failed to
ensure continuous representation of Appellant by counsel through the appeal process, in direct
violation of Mississippi law and Appellant’s constitutional rights. Appellant was indigent and had
court-appointed counsel for his Justice Court trial. Yet immediately after the trial, the Justice Court
Judge (in the December 2, 2021 order) allowed counsel to be dismissed. Later, after the appeal
was dismissed in Circuit Court on June 28, 2024, Judge Luther permitted counsel to withdraw
without appointing a replacement, leaving Appellant without counsel at a critical stage (post-
dismissal motions and the initiation of this appeal to the higher court). These actions are contrary
to the statutory mandate of Miss. Code Ann. § 99-15-15 and the provisions of the Mississippi
Rules of Criminal Procedure, and they offend Appellant’s rights to due process and assistance of
counsel.
Miss. Code Ann. § 99-15-15 provides in relevant part that for an indigent defendant charged with
a misdemeanor punishable by confinement of 90 days or more, the court may appoint counsel, and
importantly, “the accused shall have such representation available at every critical stage of the
proceeding where a substantial right may be affected.” (§ 99-15-15(3), emphasis added). In this
case, at least one of the misdemeanors (if not more) carried a potential jail sentence of 90 days or
more, so Appellant was entitled to appointed counsel under the statute. The Justice Court did
appoint counsel (Mr. Driskell) to defend Appellant at trial after finding him indigent. At that point,
the right to counsel attached under § 99-15-15 and under the Sixth Amendment. The statute’s
clear directive is that once counsel is appointed, representation must continue through all critical
stages. The filing of a notice of appeal and the appeal itself are unquestionably critical stages in a
criminal proceeding, as substantial rights are at stake (the right to a trial de novo and potential
vindication of the defendant).
Mississippi courts have reinforced this principle. The Court of Appeals held in McClendon v.
State, 748 So.2d 814, 816 (Miss. Ct. App. 1999), that “an indigent defendant is entitled to
appointed counsel on appeal when taking an appeal as of right.” Likewise, in Williams v. State,
167 So.3d 1075, 1082 (Miss. 2015), the Mississippi Supreme Court emphasized the importance of
counsel during the transition from trial to appeal, noting that this period is precisely when
defendants are most vulnerable to losing their appellate rights due to procedural technicalities.
Indeed, in Metcalf v. State, 629 So.2d 558, 562 (Miss. 1993), the Supreme Court stated that “the
right to counsel attaches at the accusatory stage and encompasses the defendant’s right to have
counsel through appeal.” And in Read v. State, 430 So.2d 832, 835 (Miss. 1983), the Court
recognized that the gap between trial and appeal is a critical stage where defendants must have the
assistance of counsel to preserve their rights.
In short, Mississippi law and precedent establish that an indigent defendant’s right to counsel
extends through the appeal of a conviction. Once counsel is appointed, the court has a duty to
ensure continuous representation. Paragraph 3 of § 99-15-15 makes this a statutory obligation: if
counsel has been appointed, the defendant “shall have such representation” at every critical stage,
which includes an appeal as of right. The word “shall” in the statute is mandatory, leaving no room
for a court to arbitrarily terminate representation at an intermediate stage. As the Eleventh Circuit
has observed, when the legislature knows how to say something but chooses not to, its silence is
controlling. Here, the legislature explicitly said counsel “shall” be available at every critical stage;
that mandate is “impervious to judicial discretion.” Anderson v. Yung Kau, 329 U.S. 482 (1947)
(recognizing that “shall” creates a firm obligation). In other words, unlike the discretionary nature
of “may,” the word “shall” is a mandatory directive. See Ivy v. Harrington, 644 So.2d 1218, 1221
(Miss. 1994) (emphasizing the mandatory character of “shall”).
The Mississippi Rules of Criminal Procedure bolster this statutory right. M.R.Cr.P. 7.2(c)
addresses the duration of appointment for indigent defendants’ counsel, and it reflects the
expectation of ongoing representation. That rule (and its predecessor, Uniform Rule of Circuit and
County Court 1.13) generally provides that appointed counsel will not be permitted to withdraw
before filing a notice of appeal. The purpose is to ensure that indigent defendants are not left
without guidance at the crucial juncture of taking an appeal. If a court does permit withdrawal,
M.R.Cr.P. 7.2(d) requires notice and good cause, and typically the court must either appoint new
counsel or be satisfied that the defendant has knowingly waived counsel. Additionally, M.R.Cr.P.
7.3(g) specifically provides that when prior counsel is allowed to withdraw, “the trial or appellate
court shall appoint new counsel for a defendant who is legally entitled to such representation on
appeal.” (Emphasis added.)
The rationale behind these rules is clear: “An accused is not only entitled to counsel at trial, but
he is entitled to counsel on appeal from a conviction on the merits. If he is indigent and unable to
afford an attorney, then he is entitled to a court-appointed attorney at trial and on appeal.” Jones
v. State, 355 So.2d 89, 91 (Miss. 1978) (emphasis added). In this case, Appellant was entitled to
counsel at trial (which he received) and on appeal (which was unjustly taken away). By
dismissing Appellant’s attorney in Justice Court immediately after the convictions, the lower court
left Appellant to fend for himself in initiating the appeal. This is precisely the scenario the law
seeks to prevent. Then, although Judge Luther eventually re-appointed Mr. Driskell at the April
2022 hearing, when the appeal was later dismissed, Judge Luther again left Appellant without
counsel by allowing withdrawal without substitution. At that point, Appellant had to navigate post-
judgment motions and the process of appealing to this Court entirely on his own. These actions by
the lower courts directly contravene § 99-15-15, the Mississippi Rules of Criminal Procedure, and
basic notions of due process and fair access to appellate review.
The consequences of being deprived of counsel were not merely theoretical in Appellant’s case;
they caused real prejudice. When his appeal was dismissed on June 28, 2024, Appellant was in
custody (for unrelated reasons) and had no immediate access to his case file or legal resources.
Without counsel to assist or advise, Appellant filed a pro se motion for reconsideration (C.P. 79–
80) that, by his own acknowledgment, addressed the issue incorrectly (he argued the wrong legal
basis). He did so because he did not recall all details of what had been filed over the past 2.5 years
and had no lawyer or record at hand. Though that motion was sincere and factually accurate, it was
misfocused, and Appellant later abandoned it upon realizing the proper argument was the one he
raises now (that costs and bonds were excused). Had counsel been in place, a proper post-dismissal
motion or immediate appeal could have been filed focusing on the indigency issue. Instead, the
Circuit Court summarily denied reconsideration (C.P. 95–98), and the delay continued.
The practices of the lower courts here—terminating counsel early and failing to appoint new
counsel—violate not only Mississippi statutes and rules but also Appellant’s constitutional rights.
The Sixth Amendment guarantees the right to counsel at all critical stages of a criminal proceeding,
and while direct appeals are not mandated by the federal Constitution, when a state provides an
appeal as of right, the proceedings on that appeal must comport with due process and equal
protection. This includes the right to counsel for indigent appellants (under the principle of
Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353 (1963), which held that states must provide counsel on a first
appeal as of right to indigent defendants). Mississippi’s own Constitution (Art. 3, § 26) similarly
guarantees that in criminal prosecutions, the accused has the right “to be heard by himself or
counsel, or both.” This applies to all critical stages of the prosecution, which logically encompasses
an appeal of right in a criminal case. By stripping an indigent appellant of counsel at critical
junctures, the lower courts in this case undermined these protections. Indigent defendants, as a
vulnerable class, depend on the courts to uphold these rights; otherwise, their ability to exercise
their legal options is illusory.
This Court should address and correct these deviations. The violation of § 99-15-15 and Rule 7,
coupled with the due process and constitutional implications, clearly rises to the level of plain error
(to the extent it was not preserved). It is “clear” and “obvious” error, and it affected Appellant’s
substantial rights by leaving him without the means to protect those rights. Indeed, this error,
combined with the bond issue, effectively cost Appellant his appeal for a significant time. The
need for continuous counsel is not a mere formality; it is a safeguard of fundamental fairness. Here,
the lack of counsel contributed to the procedural morass and delays that ensued.
For these reasons, the improper termination of Appellant’s court-appointed counsel, in both the
Justice Court and the Circuit Court, was reversible error. The Court should hold that Appellant’s
right to counsel was violated and that this violation, too, independently warrants reversal of the
dismissal. Moreover, addressing this issue will provide guidance to ensure that indigent appellants
are not similarly left without counsel in the future. Accordingly, Issue II supports reversal of
the Circuit Court’s decision.
(At a minimum, the Court should review this issue under the plain error doctrine, as in Issue I.
The errors here – violating § 99-15-15, the Mississippi Rules of Criminal Procedure, and
Appellant’s state and federal constitutional rights to counsel and due process – were clear and
obvious, and they unquestionably affected Appellant’s substantial rights. No stricter procedural
bar should prevent correction of such fundamental errors.)
III. THE CIRCUIT COURT’S DISMISSAL OF THIS APPEAL HAS RESULTED IN
MANIFEST INJUSTICE THROUGH A DENIAL OF DUE PROCESS
Considering the record as a whole, it is apparent that a grave injustice has occurred, depriving
Appellant of basic due process. The facts detailed above show that Appellant was denied: (a) his
right to a fair trial (through the irregularities in Justice Court, which were never corrected on
appeal), (b) his right to continuous representation by counsel through critical stages, and (c) his
right to an appeal on the merits, all through no fault of his own. To make matters worse, the
misdemeanor convictions at issue have now lingered for an inordinate amount of time—over four
years since the Justice Court trial. By the time this appeal is decided, nearly five years may have
elapsed since the convictions. This delay is simply intolerable and raises serious concerns about
whether a fair de novo appeal trial could even be had at this point, given faded memories, lost
evidence, or changed circumstances. In short, the combination of procedural errors and excessive
delay has worked a manifest injustice upon Appellant.
The Mississippi Supreme Court has recognized that excessive delays in the criminal process can
amount to a denial of due process and result in a fundamental injustice. In evaluating such claims,
courts often look to the factors established in Barker v. Wingo, 407 U.S. 514 (1972), which,
though formulated in the speedy trial context, provide a useful framework by analogy. Those
factors are: (1) the length of the delay; (2) the reason for the delay; (3) the defendant’s assertion of
his rights; and (4) the prejudice resulting from the delay. Id. at 530. Here, each factor weighs in
Appellant’s favor:
1. Length of delay: As noted, approximately four years have passed since the convictions (as
of late 2025), which is an extraordinarily long delay for resolving a misdemeanor appeal.
This length of delay is presumptively prejudicial.
2. Reason for the delay: The delay is attributable to court-related issues, not to Appellant.
After Appellant timely initiated the appeal, it sat largely idle on the docket awaiting the
Circuit Court’s rulings. The record reflects that Appellant and even the State (through the
prosecutor) were ready to proceed; it was the court’s failure to timely address the motions
and schedule a trial that caused the appeal to languish. Indeed, from the April 2022 hearing
to the clerk’s motion in May 2024, over two years passed with no action by the court. This
weighs heavily against the State, as a defendant should not suffer for delays caused by the
judicial system.
3. Assertion of rights: Appellant consistently asserted his rights. He filed motions to prompt
the court to act (April 2022 Notice of Hearing, etc.), he repeatedly raised the issues of
indigency and counsel, and when the appeal was wrongly dismissed, he sought
reconsideration and then pursued this appeal. There is no indication of acquiescence by
Appellant in any delay; to the contrary, he persistently tried to move the process along and
assert his right to appeal.
4. Prejudice: The delay has prejudiced Appellant in multiple ways. As observed in De La
Beckwith v. State, 707 So.2d 547 (Miss. 1997), as time passes, “witnesses’ memories fade,
evidence may be lost, and the ability to mount an effective defense is compromised.” Id.
Appellant’s case is subject to the same concern—critical witnesses or details from the 2021
trial may no longer be fresh or even available if a new trial were held. Moreover, Appellant
has had the cloud of these convictions (including any attendant consequences) hanging
over him far longer than should ever occur for misdemeanors. There is also the anxiety and
restriction of liberty (through probation or otherwise) that accompany unresolved charges.
In addition, the lack of counsel during parts of this period prejudiced Appellant’s ability to
timely and effectively assert his rights (as discussed in Issue II). Prejudice here is not just
possible; it is palpable.
Beyond the Barker v. Wingo factors, Mississippi jurisprudence provides that procedural bars or
defaults should yield when necessary to prevent a fundamental miscarriage of justice. Rowland v.
State, 42 So.3d 503, 507 (Miss. 2010) (“In criminal cases, we have suspended the procedural bars
when the case implicates a fundamental constitutional right.”). The Mississippi Supreme Court
and Court of Appeals have both recognized that the passage of time itself can create a manifest
injustice. For example, in Keys v. State, 67 So.3d 758, 762–63 (Miss. 2011), the Court noted that
“the passage of time alone can prejudice a defendant’s ability to mount an effective defense,”
underscoring that undue delay can undermine the fairness of a proceeding. Appellant’s situation
exemplifies this concern: the sheer age of the case threatens his right to a fair appeal.
Additionally, Mississippi Rule of Criminal Procedure 1.2 provides that the Rules “shall be
construed to secure simplicity in procedure, fairness in administration, and the elimination of
unjustifiable expense and delay.” (Emphasis added.) The spirit of this rule has been violated in
Appellant’s case. The procedural hurdles and delays he encountered were unjustifiable. An appeal
should not take years to be addressed, especially when the impediments (such as recognizing an
indigency order or appointing counsel) were readily solvable early on. The protracted nature of
this case runs contrary to the fundamental principle that justice delayed is justice denied.
To illustrate the prejudice concretely: When Judge Luther’s dismissal order finally came on June
28, 2024, Appellant was incarcerated on an unrelated matter and had no access to his case
documents or a law library. Human memory being fallible, Appellant did not recall every detail of
the 2.5-year-old case filings offhand. Thus, in responding to the court’s order, Appellant filed what
he could—the motion for reconsideration—which, without counsel or records, missed the key
legal argument. See C.P. 79–80. He cannot be faulted for this outcome; he had been diligent, but
circumstances (many created by the court’s own actions) put him at a severe disadvantage.
Meanwhile, the Circuit Court Judge had full access to the record and was the one who had
“forgotten” the indigency ruling despite it being presented to him twice in open court. This irony
highlights the unfairness: the court’s inattention or forgetfulness about the record (an inexcusable
oversight, given Yost v. Alderson, 58 Miss. 40, 47 (1880), which held that “mere inattention and
forgetfulness cannot be a valid excuse for failure to discharge a legal duty”) led to a dismissal, yet
Appellant—without counsel or access—was expected to immediately correct the court’s mistake.
In truth, what happened here was not a single benign oversight, but a culmination of multiple
serious due process violations starting from the Justice Court trial on November 4, 2021.
Appellant’s initial motion (C.P. 9–20) filing the appeal detailed those violations, including
constitutional issues at the trial itself (as early as C.P. 12–20). Rather than those issues being
addressed and cured in a timely manner, the process was derailed by procedural missteps not of
Appellant’s making (loss of counsel, clerk’s refusal of filings, court delays). Appellant even
provided an unrebutted affidavit (Doc. 3, C.P. 28–29) outlining the injustices suffered, which went
unaddressed. The result is that the due process violations at trial, the denial of counsel during
appeal, the plain error of dismissing the appeal, and the significant aging of the case have all
converged to substantially prejudice Appellant. This is the very definition of a manifest injustice.
At this juncture, nearly five years post-conviction, insisting that Appellant simply start over with
a new trial would itself raise concerns of fundamental fairness. The record strongly supports the
conclusion that Appellant’s convictions have been tainted by procedural irregularities and delay
to the point that they cannot be reliably upheld. To allow them to stand without remedy would
erode confidence in the judicial process and deny Appellant the basic fairness our system requires.
For these reasons, the dismissal of Appellant’s appeal — and the attendant delays and rights
deprivations — has resulted in a manifest injustice. This Court has both the authority and the duty
to remedy that injustice. As discussed in the conclusion below, the appropriate remedy under these
exceptional circumstances is to reverse the Circuit Court’s dismissal and render a judgment in
Appellant’s favor (i.e., effectively vacating the convictions). At the very least, a reversal and
remand for a new trial de novo in Circuit Court is warranted.
Finally, it must be noted that none of the errors and due process violations identified above can
be dismissed as “harmless error.” The cumulative effect of these errors has been to deprive
Appellant of fundamental rights and a fair proceeding. Harmless-error doctrine does not rescue the
State in a case like this. As the United States Supreme Court observed in Chapman v. California,
“there are some constitutional rights so basic to a fair trial that their infraction can never be
treated as harmless error.” 386 U.S. 18, 23 (1967). The rights at stake here — the right of an
indigent to appeal without bond, the right to counsel on appeal, and the right to a timely and fair
review — are of that fundamental character. Appellant has clearly suffered prejudice: he lost his
opportunity for a de novo jury trial for years, and the delays have hampered his defense.
Conversely, the State cannot claim any legitimate prejudice if this appeal is allowed to proceed (or
if the convictions are vacated), as the State was always going to have to prove the charges in a fair
forum. In fact, Mississippi law (as reflected in Kennard and Sartain, discussed above) recognizes
that when a defendant’s default is not willful and the State would not be harmed by allowing an
appeal, refusing to allow that appeal is an abuse of discretion. Here, the State benefits from the
errors, and Appellant alone suffers them. To deem such errors “harmless” would be to countenance
a serious miscarriage of justice. This Court should emphatically reject any argument by the State
that the errors below were harmless. The record establishes that they were highly prejudicial to
Appellant’s rights and the outcome of the proceedings. Accordingly, reversal is required to restore
the integrity of the process.
CONCLUSION
For the foregoing reasons, and any others apparent to the Court upon a full review of the record,
Appellant Matthew O. Reardon respectfully requests that this Court REVERSE the Lafayette
County Circuit Court’s order of June 28, 2024, and RENDER a judgment of acquittal as to the
Justice Court convictions underlying this appeal. The Mississippi Supreme Court (and by
extension, this Court) has clear authority to render a final judgment rather than remand, when the
interests of justice so require. This authority is especially apt in cases where significant time has
elapsed and where procedural errors by the lower courts have infringed fundamental rights. See
Bell v. State, 66 So.3d 90, 93 (Miss. 2011) (noting the appellate court’s power to render final
judgment when appropriate to promote justice).
Under Mississippi Rule of Appellate Procedure 2(c), this Court is empowered to suspend
procedural rules “in the interest of expediting decision, or for other good cause shown,” and
specifically where “a failure to do so would result in manifest injustice.” This provision strongly
supports the Court taking decisive action such as rendering judgment in this exceptional case to
prevent further injustice. Additionally, Miss. Code Ann. § 99-19-105(3)(a), although typically
applicable in the context of capital cases, reflects the broader principle that an appellate court
should determine “whether the trial court proceedings were so fundamentally unfair as to require
a new trial.” Here, the proceedings were fundamentally unfair to Appellant at multiple levels. By
analogy, this principle supports not only granting a new trial, but in fact recognizing that the
process has broken down so severely that final relief (acquittal) is warranted.
The circumstances of this case are indeed extraordinary. The Circuit Court disregarded the Justice
Court’s indigency determination and dismissed the appeal on improper grounds. The courts below
improperly deprived Appellant of counsel at critical moments. The appeal was delayed for an
unconscionable period, undermining the potential for a fair retrial. And notably, even the Circuit
Court, at the April 2022 hearing, acknowledged facts favorable to Appellant (no bond required,
entitlement to counsel) only to later ignore those facts in its dismissal order. Taken together, these
factors present a compelling case for this Court to exercise its remedial powers to the fullest extent.
Rendering a judgment of acquittal would finally correct the course of justice, ending a prosecution
that has been irretrievably tainted by procedural error and delay. It would also signal that courts in
Mississippi will not allow technicalities or delays to deprive an indigent defendant of his rights.
In the alternative, if the Court is not inclined to render judgment, Appellant respectfully requests
that the Court REVERSE the June 28, 2024 dismissal order and REMAND this case for a de novo
appeal trial in the Circuit Court. On remand, the Court should ensure that Appellant is allowed to
proceed in forma pauperis (with no bond required, as originally ordered) and that counsel is
appointed to represent him through the proceedings. The new trial should be conducted without
further delay, given the already excessive lapse of time.
Appellant further prays for such other relief as may be just and proper in the premises.
