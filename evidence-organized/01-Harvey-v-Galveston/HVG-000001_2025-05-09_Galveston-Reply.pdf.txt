NATURE AND STAGE OF THE PROCEEDINGS
1. Plaintiff filed suit on March 3, 2025. [Doc. 1]. Because Plaintiff’s allegations fail to
state a claim for relief, Defendants move the Court to dismiss Plaintiff’s suit. [Doc. 8].
Defendants reply in support of Defendants’ motion to dismiss.
SUMMARY OF THE ARGUMENT
2. Plaintiff’s response simply restates the legally insufficient claims alleged in
Plaintiff’s Complaint [Doc. 1] and fails to show a plausible claim for relief under the Fourth
or Fourteenth Amendment. Plaintiff still fails to allege facts that overcome Chief Roy’s
qualified immunity as to Plaintiff’s claims under the Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment.
Plaintiff still fails to allege facts that plausibly show the City violated Plaintiff’s rights.
Lastly, Plaintiff fails to allege facts that show a claim under Texas Law.
REPLY ARGUMENT AND AUTHORITIES
A. The Court need not convert the Rule 12 motion to a Rule 56 motion.
1. Defendant’s video exhibit is part of Plaintiff’s pleading.
3. As previously explained in Defendants’ motion to dismiss, “a court may consider
documents attached to a motion to dismiss if the documents are referred to in the plaintiff’s
complaint and are central to the [plaintiff’s] claims.” Collins v. Morgan Stanley Dean
Witter, 224 F.3d 496, 498–99 (5th Cir. 2000). “In so attaching, the defendant merely assists
the plaintiff in establishing the basis of the suit, and the court in making the elementary
determination of whether a claim has been stated.” Id. at 499. In Trevino v. Iden, 79 F.4th
524, 527 (5th Cir. 2023), the Fifth Circuit relied on two documents attached to the
defendant’s motion to dismiss to grant the defendant’s motion based on qualified immunity

“because both documents are referred to repeatedly in [the plaintiff’s] complaint, and [] are
central to his claims.” See also In re Katrina Canal Breaches Litig., 495 F.3d 191, 205 (5th
Cir. 2007) (“But because the defendants attached the contracts to their motions to dismiss,
the contracts were referred to in the complaints, and the contracts are central to the
plaintiffs' claims, we may consider the terms of the contracts in assessing the motions to
dismiss.”).
4. Here, Plaintiff not only repeatedly refers to the very video Defendants attached to
its motion to dismiss, Doc. 1 ¶¶ 23-25, Plaintiff also repeatedly cites Plaintiff’s own
contemporaneous recording depicting the same scene from Plaintiff’s perspective, Doc. 1
¶¶ 9, 14, and 21. Undoubtedly, both the video recordings are central to Plaintiff’s claims.
2. Because Defendant’s video exhibit is part of Plaintiff’s pleading, the
Court must analyze Defendant’s motion under Rule 12.
5. This case is distinguishable from cases in which courts have previously converted
the defendant’s motion to dismiss to a motion for summary judgment. For example, in
Hodge v. Engleman, 90 F.4th 840 (5th Cir. 2024), the Fifth Circuit converted the
defendants’ Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss to a summary judgment motion in affirming
the trial court’s order granting the motion to dismiss because defendants had attached body-
camera footage which the plaintiff “did not attach the video evidence to the pleadings, nor
did the pleadings refer to the videos sufficiently to incorporate them.” Id. at 844.
6. Further, in Isquith for & on Behalf of Isquith v. Middle S. Utilities, Inc., 847 F.2d
186, 193–94 (5th Cir. 1988), the Fifth Circuit treated defendants’ motion as a hybrid motion
to dismiss and motion for summary judgment because “looking only to the [attachments]

that defendants provided [with the motion to dismiss] [] did not permit it to evaluate
defendants' argument,” and the court had to look beyond. Id. at 193.
7. Here, the video exhibit to Defendants’ motion to dismiss is clearly referenced by
Plaintiff in his complaint. Not only that, but the video evidence referenced and attached to
Plaintiff’s complaint allow the Court to fully evaluate the merits of Defendants’ motion.
B. The Court is able to determine the facts the recordings establish.
8. It is appropriate for the Court to analyze the recordings before it and for the Court
to determine the facts the recordings plausibly show. See Benfer v. City of Baytown, 120
F.4th 1272, 1281 (5th Cir. 2024); Smith v. Heap, 31 F.4th 905, 909 (5th Cir. 2022); Harmon
v. City of Arlington, 16 F.4th 1159, 1163 (5th Cir. 2021); Griffin v. City of Sugarland, 787
Fed. Appx. 244, 245 (5th Cir. 2019) (PER CURIAM).
9. The parties have argued their interpretations of the recordings and now, the Court
has authority to determine the facts the recordings establish, including but not limited to:
(1) whether Plaintiff was seized; (2) if the Plaintiff was seized, whether Plaintiff sustained
injury that was greater than de minimis; (3) if Plaintiff was seized, whether Battalion Chief
Roy operated the fire vehicle in an objectively unreasonable manner; (4) whether Plaintiff
operated the fire vehicle recklessly; or (5) whether every reasonable firefighter would have
been on notice from some pre-existing authority that Battalion Chief Roy’s action was
clearly unconstitutional.

C. Plaintiff fails to allege facts that plausibly show a violation of the Fourth
Amendment.
1. Plaintiff fails to plausibly show he was seized.
10. In an attempt to show Chief Roy driving by Plaintiff, and potentially making contact
with Plaintiff’s tripod, is a seizure under the Fourth Amendment, Plaintiff cites to
California v. Hodari D., in which the Supreme Court held the opposite—no seizure had
occurred when a law enforcement officer neither applied physical force on the defendant
nor did defendant submit to an officer’s show of authority, either of which must occur for
a seizure under the Fourth Amendment. California v. Hodari D., 499 U.S. 621 (1991).
Contrary to Plaintiff’s argument, no constructive detention occurred. Such a seizure occurs
“only if, in view of all of the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person
would have believed that he was not free to leave.” Michigan v. Chesternut, 486 U.S. 567,
573 (1988). The factual allegations in Plaintiff’s complaint do not plausibly show any
seizure.
2. Plaintiff fails to plausibly show excessive force.
11. Even if Plaintiff could show a seizure occurred, Plaintiff still fails to allege facts
which plausibly show Chief Roy used objectively unreasonable excessive force against the
Plaintiff in the manner Battalion Chief Roy drove his fire vehicle by the Plaintiff at the
scene of a reported emergency.
12. First responders appropriately use audible and/or visual signals to warn the public
of an emergency. See TEX. TRANS. CODE. § 546.003 and § 546.004; Martinez v. United
States, 806 Fed. Appx. 293, 296 (5th Cir. 2020). And Plaintiff’s video exhibit shows
4

multiple governmental vehicles with their emergency lights activated, indicating to the
public that the officers were responding to the scene of a reported emergency. Plaintiff’s
self-serving argument otherwise is no substitute for factual allegations which plausibly
show that Battalion Chief Roy operated the fire vehicle in an objectively unreasonable
manner.
D. Plaintiff fails to allege facts that plausibly show a violation of the Fourteenth
Amendment.
13. Plaintiff’s video exhibit contradicts his argument that Battalion Chief Roy was not
performing emergency fire duties. The ongoing use of emergency lights by multiple
governmental emergency vehicles clearly show that Plaintiff and his cohort was at the
scene of an emergency when the fire vehicle drove past the Plaintiff. Plaintiff has not
alleged facts which plausibly show that Chief Roy was inspired by malice instead of
encountering “unforeseen circumstances [which] demand[ed] an instant judgment on the
part of an officer who feels the pulls of competing obligations,” Cnty. of Sacramento v.
Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 834 (1998), while responding to the scene of an emergency.
Therefore, Plaintiff fails to allege facts that plausibly show a violation of the Fourteenth
Amendment.
E. Plaintiff has not overcome Chief Roy’s qualified immunity.
14. Battalion Chief Roy is entitled to the protection of qualified immunity because
Plaintiff has not identified legal authority which would place every reasonable officer on
notice that Chief Roy’s actions were clearly unlawful. See Hunter v. Bryant, 502 U.S. 224,
228 (1991) (per curiam); Anderson v. Creighton, 483 U.S. 635, 640-641 (1987); Sorenson
5
156347041.2
Case 3:25-cv-00058 Document 12 Filed on 05/02/25 in TXSD Page 13 of 22
v. Ferrie, 134 F.3d 325, 328-30 (5th Cir. 1998). “Abstract or general statement of legal
principle untethered to analogous or near-analogous facts are not sufficient to establish a
right ‘clearly’ in a given context, rather, the inquiry must focus on whether a right is clearly
established as to the specific facts of the case.” Vincent v. City of Sulphur, 805 F.3d 543,
547 (5th Cir. 2015). “We cannot expect our [first responding] officers” to possess “a legal
scholar’s expertise in constitutional law.” Saldana v. Garza, 684 F.2d 1159, 1165 (5th Cir.
1982); accord Babb v. Dorman, 33 F.3d 472, 477-78 (5th Cir. 1994).
15. “To show the law is clearly established, a party must ‘identify a case where an
officer acting under similar circumstances … was held to have violated the Fourth
Amendment.” Nerio v. Evans, 974 F.3d 571, 575 (5th Cir. 2020) (emphasis added) (internal
quotation omitted) (quoting District of Columbia v. Wesby, 583 U.S. 48, 62 (2018)). Not
only does Plaintiff fail to make such a showing here, but he implausibly attempts to
undermine the facts established by his video exhibit—that Battalion Chief Roy was
responding to a reported emergency and was using emergency lights to warn the public
and Plaintiff of the emergency situation.
16. Plaintiff cannot state a claim that overcomes Battalion Chief Roy’s qualified
immunity with arguments he violated a section of the Texas Transportation Code, or any
other State law or administrative provision. See Davis v. Scherer, 468 U.S. 183, 194
(1984); Gagne v. City of Galveston, 805 F.2d 558, 560 (5th Cir. 1986). Such standards are
“simply irrelevant to the question of an official’s eligibility for qualified immunity in a

suit over the deprivation of a constitutional right.” Gagne, 805 F.2d at 560 (emphasis
added).
17. Likewise, Plaintiff cannot refute Battalion Chief Roy’s qualified immunity by
relying on either of the three judicial opinion Plaintiff cites. Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730,
733-735 (2002) was a suit in which a guard twice handcuffed a prison inmate to a hitching
post in the sun for hours. In Cole v. Carson, 905 F.3d 334, 343-44 (5th Cir. 2018), officers
shot a teenager who was holding a firearm to his own head when he was shot. In Checki v.
Webb, 875 F.2d 534, 535-36 (5th Cir. 1986), police officers in an unmarked vehicle
pursued for 31 miles the vehicle in which the plaintiff rode at speeds in excess of 100 miles
per hour until a road block stopped the flight, after which an officer handcuffed the
passenger, and then stuck him about the face with a revolver causing severe facial injuries.
On these facts, the Court held that a factual dispute precluded resolution of plaintiff’s claim
before trial. Id. at 538.
18. “No matter how carefully a reasonable officer read” these three judicial opinions
before Battalion Chief Roy encountered the Plaintiff, a reasonable “officer could not know”
that Battalion Chief Roy’s much different conduct clearly violated the constitution. See
City and County of San Francisco v. Sheehan, 575 U.S. 600, 616 (2015). Suffice it to say,
a reasonable [firefighter] could miss the connection between the situation confronting”
Battalion Chief Roy and the officers in the case opinions Plaintiff cited. See Kisela v.
Hughes, 584 U.S. 100, 108 (2018). Reasonable first responders cannot be expected to have
a legal scholar’s expertise in constitutional law to the degree a reasonable firefighter would
know that Battalion Chief Roy’s actions were clearly forbidden by well-established

constitutional law. See Saldana v. Garza, 684 F.2d 1159, 1165 (5th Cir. 1982). Plaintiff
has not overcome Battalion Chief Roy’s qualified immunity. Like in Kisela, 584 U.S. at
107, Plaintiff’s immunity argument “does not pass the straight-face test.” (internal citation
omitted).
F. Plaintiff fails to allege facts that plausibly show the City violated Plaintiff’s
rights.
1. All federal claims against the City fail because the pled factual
allegations do not show that Chief Roy violated Plaintiff’s federal rights.
19. Regardless of the City’s policies or actions, all claims against the City fail because
Plaintiff has not alleged facts which plausibly show he was deprived of a constitutional
right. See City of Los Angeles v. Heller, 475 U.S. 797, 811 (1986); Kitchen v. Dall. Cty.,
759 F.3d 468, 483 (5th Cir. 2014); Rios v. City of Del Rio, 444 F.3d 417, 426 (5th Cir.
2006); McKee v. City of Rockwall, 877 F.2d 409, 414 (5th Cir.), cert denied, 493 U.S. 1023
(1990).
2. Plaintiff failed to allege facts which plausibly show that a City of
Galveston policymaker was culpable for a deprivation of Plaintiff’s
rights.
20. Plaintiff asserts claims against the City on only two theories of liability, which both
require factual allegations showing culpability of the City of Galveston’s policymaker.
[Doc. 11, ¶¶ 32-39]. Plaintiff argues one theory of liability based on the premise Battalion
Chief Roy is the City’s policymaker, and a conflicting second theory of liability premised
on the Galveston Fire Chief and Galveston Assistant Fire Chief being the City of Galveston
‘s policymakers who – after the fact – ratified unconstitutional action of their subordinate
Battalion Chief Roy. Neither the law nor the facts alleged show a claim against the City on

a. Plaintiff has not pled facts which implicate the single incident
exception to state a claim against the City.
21. Plaintiff does not identify his brief any basis in law or plead facts in his complaint
which plausibly show that Battalion Chief Roy – a subordinate of the Galveston Fire Chief,
Galveston Assistant Fire Chief, and Galveston City Manager, is the City of Galveston’s
policymaker. See Brown v. City of Houston, Tex., 337 F.3d 539, 541 (5th Cir. 2003).
22. The City of Galveston is a home-rule municipality governed under a Charter. See
City of Galveston v. State of Texas, 217 S.W.3d 466, (Tex. 2007). The Court may take
judicial notice of the City’s Charter and Ordinances
[https://library.municode.com/tx/galveston/codes/code_of_ordinances]. See Fed. R. Evid.
201. In his brief, Plaintiff does not identify any legal basis supporting the contention that
Battalion Chief Roy is the City’s policymaker.
23. Plaintiff alleges “[a] Battalion Chief is a senior-level official within the Galveston
Fire Department, under only the Fire Chief and Assistant Fire Chief.” [Doc. 1, ¶ 15]. From
those alleged facts. Plaintiff presents the otherwise bare legal conclusion not entitled to
assumption of the truth that “Battalion chiefs are policymakers within the Galveston Fire
Department, responsible for setting and implementing policy regarding operations,
training, and discipline within their battalion.” [Doc. 1, ¶ 15].
24. Plaintiff’s factual allegations show that battalion chiefs are subordinate to both the
Fire Chief and Assistant Fire Chief, and unmentioned is the City Manager or City Council

in the home rule City of Galveston. And in ¶ 37 of his complaint, Plaintiff specifically
identifies the Fire Chief and Assistant Fire Chief as “the final policymakers within the
Galveston Fire Department. [Doc. 1].
25. To state a claim, Plaintiff must allege facts which connect some City policymaker
to the alleged unconstitutional act of Battalion Chief Roy. See Benfer v. City of Baytown,
120 F.4th 1272, 1285-86 (5th Cir. 2024); Longoria, 942 F.3d at 271-72. And Plaintiff has
not pled facts which plausibly show that any City policymaker delegated municipal
policymaking authority to Battalion Chief Roy. See id.
26. The Plaintiff is not typically obligated to plead the identity of the City’s
policymaker, Groden v. City of Dallas, 826 F.3d 280, 284 (5th Cir. 2016), but the Plaintiff
is still required to plead facts which plausibly show that a City of Galveston policymaker
could is culpable for a deprivation of Plaintiff’s constitutional rights. Longoria Next Friend
of M.L. v. San Benito Independent Consolidated School District, 942 F.3d 258, 271 (5th
Cir. 2019).
b. Plaintiff has not pled facts which plausibly show a City
policymaker ratified unconstitutional conduct.
27. Plaintiff’s alternative argument that City policymakers, the Fire Chief and Assistant
Fire Chief, ratified Battalion Chief Roy’s unconstitutional action also fails. “But
ratification liability requires that the authorized policymakers approve not only the
subordinate's act, but also the basis for it.” Doe on behalf of M.F. v. Harris Cnty. Precinct
Six Constable Sylvia Trevino, 452 F. Supp. 3d 548, 560 (S.D. Tex. 2020) (Eskridge, J.).
“This means that the policymaker must have actual knowledge of the improper basis for
10
156347041.2
Case 3:25-cv-00058 Document 12 Filed on 05/02/25 in TXSD Page 18 of 22
the subordinate's action and yet approve the action anyway.” Id. (citing Beattie v. Madison
County School District, 254 F.3d 595, 604 (5th Cir. 2001)). Moreover, “[u]nless the
subordinate's actions are sufficiently extreme—for instance, an obvious violation of clearly
established law—a policymaker’s ratification or defense of his subordinate's actions is
insufficient to establish an official policy or custom.” Hobart v. City of Stafford, 916 F.
Supp. 2d 783, 795–96 (S.D. Tex. 2013) (Ellison, J.).
28. To plead a ratification claim, Plaintiff must allege facts showing “[i]f the authorized
policymakers approve a subordinate’s decisions and the basis for it, their ratification would
be chargeable to the municipality because their decision is final.” City of St. Louis v.
Praprotnik, 485 U.S. 112, 127 (1988). “[Supreme Court] precedent has limited the theory
of ratification to ‘extreme factual’ situations.” Peterson v. City of Fort Worth, 588 F.3d
838, 848 (5th Cir. 2009) (citing Snyder v. Trepagnier, 142 F.3d 791, 798 (5th Cir. 1998)).
29. The recordings before the Court are sufficient on their face to eliminate Plaintiff’s
ratification claim. Battalion Chief Roy’s emergency vehicle operation is not an “extreme
factual situation,” in the circumstances of this case. Compare Peterson v. City of Fort
Worth, 588 F.3d 838, 848 (5th Cir. 2009) (did not find ratification where officer delivered
