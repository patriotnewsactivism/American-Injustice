3. Briefly describe what this case is about.

excessive force in violation of Plaintiff’s Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights by
intentionally striking him with a fire truck mirror, causing injury. Alternatively, Plaintiff
alleges that a City of Galveston firefighter recklessly struck Plaintiff with a fire truck mirror
without lawful excuse, causing injury.

The cases which the Defendants cite below claiming that a “careful procedure” is
appropriate have been overruled by the Fifth Circuit. See Carswell v. Camp, 37 F.4th 1062,
1067 (5th Cir. 2022) (“Iqbal squarely repudiated our ‘careful procedure’ for allowing
tailored discovery before a district court rules on an official’s motion to dismiss. When
defendants assert qualified immunity in a motion to dismiss, the district court may not defer
ruling on that assertion. It may not permit discovery—“cabined or otherwise”—against
immunity-asserting defendants before it has determined plaintiffs have pleaded facts
sufficient to overcome the defense.”).

Therefore, Plaintiff joins with Defendants’ proposal that the Court defer establishing any
discovery deadlines until after the Court resolves the issues in Defendants’ 12(b)(6) Motion
to Dismiss. Once a ruling is made, Plaintiff proposes that discovery proceed without any
of the additional restrictions proposed by the Defendants.
Plaintiff disagrees with the need to limit or bifurcate discovery because the issue of qualified
immunity, if established at summary judgment, would not shield the City of Galveston from
Plaintiff’s claims under Monell or the TTCA. Therefore, unless the Court grants
Defendants’ 12(b)(6) Motion to Dismiss in its entirety, Chief Roy will continue to be a key
witness concerning Plaintiff’s claims against the City of Galveston, and he is a government
official whose office would be at least partially responsible for responding to Plaintiff’s
discovery requests directed to the City of Galveston. Even if Chief Roy were to win a
dispositive motion regarding his own qualified immunity, he and his office will be subject
to discovery requests and depositions as part of Plaintiff’s continuing claims against the
City of Galveston. Therefore, limiting, delaying, or bifurcating discovery would not solve
Defendants’ chief complaint that qualified immunity should shield Chief Roy from the
burdens of discovery. It would, however, unnecessarily delay the discovery process and
invite discovery disputes concerning whether specific requests are within or outside the
scope necessary to address only qualified immunity. Such discovery restrictions would tax
judicial resources without solving any of the problems presented by Defendants.
Defendants:
Chief William Roy is a governmental official who has asserted, through a motion to dismiss
under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(b)(6), his qualified immunity from liability and burdens of
unnecessary discovery.
The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals has established a “careful procedure” in accord with
Supreme Court precedent that protects public officials from burdens of discovery unless a
plaintiff has alleged facts which show a plausible claim for relief that is not barred by
qualified immunity. See Converse v. City of Kemah, 961 F.3d 771, 780 n.8 (5th Cir. 2020).
Under the governing procedure, all discovery is stayed unless the Court denies Chief Roy’s
motion to dismiss.
Thereafter, discovery is further controlled by the procedure reinforced in Converse supra
and the authorities cited in this report. Under controlling precedent, discovery is entirely
stayed until after the Court rules on the sufficiency of Plaintiff’s pleading allegations to
state a claim that overcomes Chief Roy’s qualified immunity. See Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556

U.S. 662, 684-686 (2009); Carswell v. Camp, 54 F.4th 307, 310 (5th Cir. 2022); Backe v.
LeBlanc, 691 F.3d 645, 648 (5th Cir. 2012); Zapata v. Melson, 750 F.3d 481, 485 (5th Cir.
2014); Bustillos v. El Paso County Hospital District, 891 F.3d 214, 223 (5th Cir. 2018).
If, after evaluating the sufficiency of Plaintiff’s pleading allegations, the Court denies Chief
Roy’s claim to immunity through Rule 12, and the Court further finds that specific, narrowly
tailored discovery is necessary for the Court to rule on Chief Roy’s claim to qualified
immunity at the earliest possible stage in the litigation through a motion for summary
judgment, the Court then has discretion to authorize specific limited discovery the Court
finds necessary to rule on the claim to immunity made under Fed. R. Civ. P. 56. See Lion
Boulos v. Wilson, 834 F.2d 504, 506 (5th Cir. 1987).
Defendants propose that any initial (qualified immunity) discovery period the Court
authorizes after ruling on motions to dismiss based on qualified immunity should begin 15
days after the Court rules on immunity through the motion to dismiss and the Court
identifies the scope of discovery necessary to provide the Court with sufficient information
to rule on qualified immunity.
Defendants anticipate the need for seventy-five (75) days of discovery regarding issues
relevant to resolution of qualified immunity may be necessary, if the Court denies the claim
to immunity through the motion to dismiss.
Defendants anticipate that following any initial discovery period limited to immunity the
Court authorizes; Chief Roy will likely file a potentially dispositive motion for summary
judgment based on qualified immunity.
After resolution of Chief Roy’s pre-trial claim to immunity through Rules 12 and 56, a
second abbreviated discovery period of no more than ninety (90) days for remaining
discovery regarding issues unrelated to qualified immunity may be necessary if a claim
remains at that point.
In light of this “careful procedure” mandated by controlling precedent, Backe supra,
Defendants recommend the Court not now enter a docket control order that addresses
discovery or motions dependent on completion of discovery, but instead ask the Court to
set further deadlines after pre-trial motions based on qualified immunity are resolved.
The Supreme Court also unequivocally rejected a case management approach to proceed
with discovery related to other defendants, while deferring discovery related to a
governmental defendant who has asserted qualified immunity. See Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556

U.S. 662, 684-686 (2009). The Supreme Court explained its rationale for doing so at 685-
686,
It is no answer to these concerns to say that discovery for [a governmental
defendant who has asserted qualified immunity from burdens of litigation]
can be deferred while pretrial proceedings continue for other defendants. It is
quite likely that, when discovery as to the other parties proceeds, it would
prove necessary for [potentially immune public servants] and their counsel to
participate in the process to ensure the case does not develop in a misleading
or slanted way that causes prejudice to their position. Even if [the potentially
immune public officials] are not yet themselves subject to discovery orders,
then, they would not be free from the burdens of discovery.
Following Iqbal, the Fifth Circuit Court has held in accord prohibiting “cabin[ed]
discovery.” See Carswell, 54 F.4th at 311. Controlling Fifth Circuit precedent holds that,
“Iqbal squarely prohibits interpreting [the Fifth Circuit]’s ‘careful procedure’ as allowing
tailored discovery before a district court rules on an official’s motion to dismiss.’” Id. at
312.
This Court lacks discretion to order the impermissible discovery Plaintiff proposes that
would invite an interlocutory appeal. See Backe, 691 F.3d at 649.
Defendants propose the Court defer establishing any discovery deadline until after the Court

Roy’s qualified immunity.
