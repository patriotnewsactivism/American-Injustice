through a breath test, yet the officers escalated to a full custodial arrest and forcible blood extraction.
At the hospital, Reardon – already handcuffed and restrained – was pinned down by about 10 officers
while a needle was jabbed into his arm multiple timeslafayettems.netlafayettems.net. Such force may
be deemed excessive and unreasonable, especially if Reardon was not actively resisting in a way that
threatened safety. Courts have recognized that even a lawful search can be executed in an unreasonable
manner. For example, in Schmerber v. California, 384 U.S. 757 (1966), the Supreme Court upheld a DUI
blood draw only because it was done by medical personnel in a reasonable, non-brutal manner and with
clear probable cause. By contrast, forced medical procedures that “shock the conscience” – such as
overly brutal tactics – violate the Constitution (see Rochin v. California, 342 U.S. 165 (1952), where
forced stomach pumping violated due process). Here, jabbing a needle repeatedly into Reardon’s arm
while he’s held by a gang of officers, without evidence of intoxication, could be viewed as unreasonable
force and an unreasonable bodily search under the Fourth Amendment and also as a due process
violation (discussed below).
Moreover, if medical personnel at UTMB Hospital assisted in this force (for instance, a nurse actually
performing the blood draw under police orders), they too acted “under color of state law” and can be
liable under §1983lafayettems.net. Courts have held that a private or state-employed medical worker
who facilitates law enforcement searches or abuse can be considered a state actor (see West v. Atkins,
487 U.S. 42 (1988), holding medical professionals serving state functions are state actors). Thus, any
UTMB staff who excessively poked Reardon or proceeded without proper consent/warrant could face
liability for excessive force or unlawful search. The facts that Reardon was never shown a warrant and
that Osteen’s affidavit for the blood draw was allegedly tainted by falsehoods underscore that this
intrusion into Reardon’s body was unlawful at its inceptionlafayettems.net. Even had a warrant been
valid, the sheer level of force used to execute it may not have been reasonable if Reardon posed no
threat. This claim would cover not only the officers applying force but any supervisory officials who
condoned it and the medical staff who carried it out.
3. Unreasonable Search and Seizure of Property (Vehicle Impoundment)
Reardon’s vehicle was also seized and impounded after his arrest. The Fourth Amendment protects
against unreasonable seizures of property. While police may impound a vehicle incident to arrest under
certain circumstances (such as to protect the car or the public or to conduct an inventory search), it
must be done in accordance with law and not as a pretext to punish or harass. Here, questions arise
whether the impoundment was truly necessary or lawfully handled. Reardon had two associates on the
scene (the fellow journalists filming the stop) who potentially could have taken custody of the
carlafayettems.net. If officers refused reasonable alternatives and towed the car without a legitimate
reason (or conducted an improper inventory search of the vehicle without following procedures),
Reardon could claim an unlawful seizure of his property.
Furthermore, once the car was impounded, due process requires a prompt opportunity to reclaim the
property or challenge the impoundment. If Galveston authorities kept Reardon’s vehicle or its contents
without notice or an avenue for a post-seizure hearing, that could violate the Fourteenth Amendment’s
procedural due process (see, e.g., City of West Covina v. Perkins, 525 U.S. 234 (1999), requiring notice
of how to get seized property back). Any extended deprivation of the vehicle (and any belongings like
cameras or phones inside it) without due process would bolster a §1983 claim. While the full details of
the vehicle impound aren’t provided in the facts, it is an issue to investigate. At minimum, the initial
decision to seize the car might be unlawful if it was done out of spite or conspiracy rather than
community caretaking. This claim would lie against the Galveston officers or department responsible for
the impound.
Fourteenth Amendment Claims – Due Process and Equal Protection
1. Malicious Prosecution / Fabrication of Evidence (Due Process)
Reardon can bring a malicious prosecution claim under §1983, premised on the Fourth and Fourteenth
Amendments, for being prosecuted on false charges. The key elements are that the defendants initiated
or continued a criminal proceeding against him without probable cause, with malice, and that the
proceeding terminated in Reardon’s favor (or is such that it can be pursued – see Thompson v. Clark,
596 U.S. ___, 142 S. Ct. 1332 (2022), holding a §1983 plaintiff need only show the prior proceeding
ended without a conviction, not affirmative proof of innocence). Here, the Galveston officers (notably
Officer Osteen) allegedly lied in official reports and testimony to justify charging Reardon. Osteen’s
affidavit and statements included deliberate falsehoods – e.g., claiming a moderate odor of alcohol on
Reardon’s breath (contradicted by the 0.0 toxicology result)lafayettems.net and saying Reardon
“refused” testing (omitting that Reardon actually begged for a breath
test)lafayettems.netlafayettems.net. These fabrications were material in securing warrants and in
persuading prosecutors to charge Reardon with a felony. Additionally, at Reardon’s Mississippi
probation revocation hearing, Osteen perjured himself by asserting that the toxicology report showed
alcohol when it did notlafayettems.netlafayettems.net. Presenting fabricated evidence or false
testimony to detain or prosecute someone is a classic violation of due process – it offends fundamental
fairness and justice.
The Fourteenth Amendment’s Due Process Clause protects individuals from being framed by
government officials. Courts have recognized a constitutional cause of action when police fabricate
evidence to incriminate a person, as it results in the person being deprived of liberty without due
process of lawce9.uscourts.gov. In Reardon’s case, but for the falsified evidence and malicious actions,
there would have been no arrest, no charge, and no revocation of his probation. The harm was
substantial: he lost his freedom (detained in Texas and then imprisoned in Mississippi) and suffered
reputational and emotional injury. To succeed, Reardon will need to show the charges have ended
favorably. The Texas DWI case is apparently still pending, but if it gets dismissed (as Reardon’s Franks
motion seekslafayettems.net) or if he is acquitted, he can then pursue malicious prosecution. The
malice is evident from the alleged retaliatory motive (the desire to silence Reardon’s journalism and
protect Deputy Beavers) and the utter lack of good faith basis for the charge.
Notably, malicious prosecution claims can be brought under the Fourth Amendment (as a “seizure”
without probable cause) or under procedural due process. The Fifth Circuit (governing Texas) recognizes
such claims where the plaintiff was seized pursuant to legal process that was wrongful. Given Reardon
was arrested, bound by the charges, and had his liberty curtailed by legal process (indictment, warrants,
etc.), this claim is viable. The fabrication of evidence also gives rise to a direct due process violation:
government actors cannot knowingly use false evidence to deprive someone of liberty (Napue v. Illinois,
360 U.S. 264 (1959)). Here we have multiple instances of alleged false evidence (Osteen’s reports,
Beavers’ input) that led to Reardon’s incarceration. This behavior, if proven, is egregious and courts have
not hesitated to impose liability for such frame-ups.
2. Deprivation of Liberty without Due Process – Prolonged Detention and Denial of Fair Procedure
Beyond the malicious initiation of charges, Reardon’s treatment in custody raises due process concerns.
After his arrest, he apparently was held without bail or with excessive bail such that he could not
secure release. Normally, an arrestee has the right to a prompt magistration and the setting of
reasonable bail (the Eighth Amendment prohibits excessive bail, and the Fourteenth ensures state
compliance). If Galveston authorities kept Reardon jailed for an extended period without a probable
cause hearing or reasonable bail, that would violate Gerstein v. Pugh, 420 U.S. 103 (1975) and County
of Riverside v. McLaughlin, 500 U.S. 44 (1991) (which require a probable cause determination within 48
hours of arrest). It’s unclear from the facts how long Reardon remained in Galveston custody post-
arrest, but any unusual delay or refusal to set bail could be actionable.
More significantly, the coordination with Mississippi led to an interstate deprivation of liberty. Reardon
was on probation/parole in Mississippi, and Deputy Beavers (along with others) used the Texas arrest to
initiate a probation revocation. It appears Reardon was extradited or transported to Mississippi before
his Texas case concluded. If he was handed over without proper extradition proceedings or without an
opportunity to contest transfer, that could be considered an “illegal extradition”lafayettems.net
violating due process. Typically, the Interstate Agreement on Detainers and extradition clauses require
certain procedures, including the right to contest extradition or the issuance of a Governor’s warrant. If
Texas authorities simply allowed Mississippi to take custody of Reardon (perhaps under the guise of a
probation violation warrant) without those steps, Reardon could claim his due process rights to lawful
extradition were denied. Even if procedures were facially followed, the collusive nature – where Texas
and Mississippi officers allegedly conspired to ensure Reardon’s long-term imprisonment – may render
the process fundamentally unfair, i.e., a “sham” process used to punish him extrajudicially.
Additionally, Reardon had a constitutional right to counsel at critical stages of the proceedings. He
invoked his right to an attorney at the roadside upon being asked to do sobriety testslafayettems.net.
Although the Sixth Amendment right to counsel had not attached at that pre-arrest moment, once he
was formally charged or subjected to custodial interrogation, he had rights under the Fifth and Sixth
Amendments. If the officers proceeded to interrogate or perform the blood draw after Reardon said he
wanted a lawyer, that implicates Miranda v. Arizona (Fifth Amendment right to counsel during custodial
questioning). The blood draw is not interrogation, but if they questioned him at the hospital or jail after
he invoked, that would be a violation. Moreover, at the probation revocation hearing in Mississippi,
was Reardon afforded counsel? Probation revocation is a critical stage where due process guarantees a
limited right to counsel especially in complex cases (see Gagnon v. Scarpelli, 411 U.S. 778 (1973)).
Reardon did eventually secure an attorney (Ben Campagna) for his defenselafayettems.net, but any
period where he was fending for himself in a coordinated multi-state legal tangle could raise due
process issues. The reference to denial of right to counsel likely centers on the initial incident: his
request for legal advice during the DWI investigation was ignored. While not a clear-cut constitutional
violation at that moment (since there’s generally no right to counsel during a traffic stop or before
deciding on a breath test in Texas), the totality – including possibly refusing him access to a lawyer for
hours after arrest – could be framed as part of the oppressive, malicious conduct that deprived him of a
fair ability to defend himself.
3. Equal Protection (Selective Enforcement)
Reardon also hints at an equal protection issue, insofar as he was targeted uniquely because of who he
was (a vocal critic of law enforcement). Typically, equal protection claims require discriminatory intent
based on membership in a protected class (race, religion, etc.) or a “class-of-one” arbitrary targeting.
Here, Reardon was singled out not due to race or other protected status, but due to his activities as a
journalist and police observer. That is more properly addressed as a First Amendment retaliation claim
(discussed below) rather than equal protection. A “class-of-one” equal protection claim (claiming he was
treated differently with no rational basis) is theoretically possible – he could argue no other similarly
situated driver (sober, compliant drivers) would be treated this way, and the only reason for the
disparate treatment was personal vindictiveness. However, the Supreme Court in Engquist v. Oregon
Dept. of Agriculture, 553 U.S. 591 (2008), held that class-of-one claims are not viable in the context of
discretionary law enforcement decisions, since police must make individualized judgments. Thus, a
standalone equal protection claim here is weak unless there were evidence of discrimination against a
particular group (for example, if officials conspired to punish “First Amendment auditors” as a class). If
Reardon wanted to pursue an equal protection theory, he’d need to show a pattern of selective
enforcement against him or others like him, motivated by irrational or malicious reasons. Given the
facts, it’s more straightforward to frame the malice behind his treatment as retaliation for protected
speech (First Amendment) rather than as an EP violation. We note this issue for completeness, but it
likely does not add much beyond the First Amendment claim.
4. Abuse of Process / Fundamental Fairness
Lastly, the coordinated interstate actions can be viewed through a substantive due process lens as an
abuse of government power that “shocks the conscience.” The alleged scheme – Mississippi deputies
(Beavers and James) colluding with Texas officers to misuse the justice system and “do Lafayette
County’s dirty work” by jailing Reardon on false pretenseslafayettems.net – is not just a series of Fourth
Amendment violations, but a broader conspiracy to deprive a person of liberty without honest process.
This kind of abuse of process or conspiracy to violate rights can itself be actionable. In other words,
even if one were to argue the formalities (stop, arrest, hearing) were followed on paper, the purpose
and collusion behind them render the whole proceeding a deprivation of liberty without genuine due
process of law. Courts have recognized that extreme police misconduct can violate substantive due
process when it’s not readily addressed by specific Bill of Rights provisions – for example, intentionally
framing someone for a crime might be cognizable as a due process violation even if we also consider it
under the Fourth Amendment (see Limone v. Condon, 372 F.3d 39, 44–45 (1st Cir. 2004), describing
due process violation where officers conspired to frame men for murder). Here, the combination of
falsified evidence, multi-state collusion, and retaliatory motive is arguably so egregious that it shocks
the conscience, supporting a substantive due process claim.
In sum, Reardon’s Fourteenth Amendment-based claims include: malicious prosecution, fabrication of
evidence, procedural due process violations (denial of fair extradition/bail/counsel), and potentially
substantive due process (conscience-shocking conspiracy). These claims would largely be brought
against the individuals who orchestrated and carried out the scheme (the officers and deputies
involved), as well as potentially supervisory officials if they sanctioned or were deliberately indifferent to
such misconduct.
First Amendment Retaliation Claim
Perhaps the central theme of Reardon’s case is that he was targeted in retaliation for his protected
speech and reporting, in violation of the First Amendment. To establish a §1983 claim for First
Amendment retaliation, Reardon must show: (1) he engaged in protected speech or conduct; (2)
government defendants took adverse action against him that would chill a person of ordinary firmness
from continuing that speech; and (3) his speech was a substantial or motivating factor for the adverse
action. All three elements appear strongly supported by the facts:
• Protected Speech: Reardon’s August 2, 2023 YouTube video exposing Deputy Beavers’ alleged
perjury is core protected expressionlafayettems.netlafayettems.net. He was speaking out on a
matter of public concern (police misconduct in a criminal trial) – this is classic First Amendment
activity. Additionally, Reardon’s general work as a “First Amendment auditor” (recording police,
criticizing officials) and his refusal to be silenced are protected. Even his act of filming the
Galveston stop and verbally asserting his rights during that stop is protected speech (citizens
have the right to verbally challenge police actions and to film police in publiclafayettems.net,
per cases like City of Houston v. Hill, 482 U.S. 451 (1987), and various circuit rulings on the right
to record).
• Adverse Action: The adverse actions here were extreme – Reardon was arrested on false
charges, subjected to force, and incarcerated. A retaliatory arrest and prosecution are
unquestionably enough to deter or chill a person from future speech. The Supreme Court has
held even relatively minor punishments can satisfy this prong; arrest and jailing are major
deterrents. Reardon also suffered the takedown of his credibility and the burden of defending a
felony charge. Thus, the harm element is met.
• Causal Connection (Motivation): There is compelling evidence that Reardon’s protected speech
was the impetus for the actions against him. The timeline is conspicuous: just days after he
published the video exposing Deputy Beavers (August 2, 2023), he was set up in a bogus DWI
arrest (August 11, 2023) involving that same deputy behind the
sceneslafayettems.netlafayettems.net. Reardon even caught Officer Osteen on bodycam asking,
“You an auditor, man?” early in the stoplafayettems.net, indicating the Galveston officers knew
of Reardon’s activism. Osteen immediately took a special interest in handling Reardon (“I’m
doing this one”lafayettems.net), suggesting he recognized Reardon as someone the department
might want to make an example of or appease fellow officers by arresting. Moreover, the
direct involvement of Deputy Beavers and Deputy James in prompting the felony
chargelafayettems.net demonstrates a retaliatory motive: they wanted Reardon punished
because he embarrassed them and accused them of misconductlafayettems.net. This satisfies
the requirement that the speech was a motivating factor behind the law enforcement action.
Legally, one challenge in First Amendment retaliation claims for arrests is the existence of probable
cause. In Nieves v. Bartlett, 587 U.S. ___, 139 S. Ct. 1715 (2019), the Court held that if officers had
probable cause to arrest, it generally bars a retaliatory arrest claim (because arresting someone who
broke the law is typically justified regardless of motive) – unless others similarly situated were not
arrested (a narrow exception). Here, however, it appears probable cause was lacking or fabricated, so
Reardon can argue the Nieves bar does not apply. Similarly, Hartman v. Moore, 547 U.S. 250 (2006),
requires a plaintiff alleging retaliatory prosecution to show the absence of probable cause for the
underlying charge. Reardon can meet that burden by showing the charge was baseless (confirmed by
exculpatory evidence like the negative blood testlafayettems.net). Indeed, the entire arrest and
prosecution were a pretext – “this was never about the law—it was about silencing me,” as Reardon
aptly put itlafayettems.net. When officers invent charges to retaliate against someone’s expression, it
is a blatant First Amendment violation.
Therefore, Reardon has a strong First Amendment claim against both the Galveston officers and the
Mississippi deputies who conspired to retaliate. Each can be liable if they were part of the retaliatory
chain of events. For instance, Deputy Beavers didn’t physically arrest Reardon, but if she instigated or
encouraged Galveston PD to take action because of Reardon’s speech, she can be held liable for
retaliation under §1983 (state officials cannot indirectly do what they can’t do directly – using another
state’s officers to retaliate still makes her a participant). The same applies to Deputy Hoby James if he
was involved in planning this reprisallafayettems.net. This claim might also extend to any supervisors
who knew of the retaliatory motive and let the arrest happen. Notably, if a municipal policy or custom
existed of targeting “auditors” or critics, the City of Galveston could face Monell liability (discussed
below). At minimum, the evidence of explicit statements and timing is powerful evidence of
unconstitutional motive.
Involvement of Medical Staff – Liability of UTMB Hospital Personnel
Although not a separate constitutional right, it is important to highlight the role of medical staff at
UTMB Hospital in the blood draw and any potential liability. Under §1983, a private individual or entity
can be deemed to act “under color of state law” if they are a willful participant in joint action with the
State or performing a public function. Here, drawing blood for law enforcement purposes – especially
when done forcibly – is effectively acting as an arm of law enforcement. The nurse or phlebotomist who
performed the involuntary blood draw did so at the behest of Galveston officers executing a search
warrant (valid or not). This makes the medical staff a part of the state action. Indeed, University of
Texas Medical Branch (UTMB) is a state institution, so its employees are state actors by definition.
If the medical staff failed to follow proper medical protocols or proceeded despite Reardon’s lack of
consent and apparent distress, they may have breached Reardon’s constitutional rights as well. The
Supreme Court in Schmerber allowed blood draws for DUI only when conducted in a reasonable
manner by medical personnel. However, if the procedure here was carried out in a “brutal” or
medically unjustified way, it runs afoul of the Fourth Amendment’s reasonableness requirement. For
instance, stabbing his arm “multiple times” because of difficulty in drawing bloodlafayettems.net
suggests either incompetence or a rushed effort to get evidence regardless of pain inflicted. If Reardon
sustained injury or unnecessary pain, a claim of excessive force/physical abuse could lie against the
nurse. Some courts have found liability where medical personnel conduct procedures that are not
medically necessary at police direction – e.g., forced enemas or catheterizations to find drugs have been
deemed unconstitutional if not justified and done without consent or proper warrant.
Additionally, if UTMB staff knew there was no valid warrant presented (since none was shown to
Reardonlafayettems.net) and still proceeded, they could be liable for an unlawful search. Even though
officers said they were “applying” for a warrant, the staff ideally should have confirmed a judge’s order
before involuntary bloodletting. By not doing so, they effectively relied solely on police say-so. Finally,
from a state action standpoint, this case parallels instances where hospital staff are held liable for
assisting law enforcement in searches: for example, George v. Edholm, 752 F.3d 1206 (9th Cir. 2014),
found a doctor could be a state actor when he searched a suspect’s mouth for contraband at officers’
request. Similarly, UTMB staff here were integral to the search of Reardon’s blood.
In conclusion on this point, any medical professionals who participated can be sued under §1983 for the
unconstitutional blood draw. Potential claims against them include the Fourth Amendment
(unreasonable search/seizure) and Fourteenth Amendment (if the conduct was so egregious it shocks
the conscience). They might attempt a defense that they followed a court order or standard procedure,
but those defenses may crumble if the facts show the procedure was obviously abusive or no proper
warrant was in hand.
Conspiracy and Coordination Among Officials (42 U.S.C. §1983 and §1985)
The facts strongly indicate a conspiracy among multiple officials across state lines to violate Reardon’s
civil rights. Under §1983, a conspiracy claim allows holding each participant liable for the acts of co-
conspirators in furtherance of the scheme, so long as an actual constitutional violation resulted. To
plead a §1983 conspiracy, Reardon must show (1) an agreement between two or more persons to
deprive him of his constitutional rights, and (2) an actual deprivation of those rights resulting from the
agreement. Here, the agreement can be inferred from the communications between Deputy Beavers,
Deputy James, and Officer Osteen (and possibly others) and the coordinated actions they took.
The evidence of agreement includes: the phone call Reardon overheard in booking where Beavers
instructed Osteen to charge a felonylafayettems.netlafayettems.net; the fact that Beavers and James
were aware of Reardon’s movements (they knew he was in Galveston) and had a stake in getting him
arrested; and perhaps prior communications or planning (the false tip call by Beavers’ associate or
supporter, Bobby Kennedy, suggests pre-planninglafayettems.net). It appears Galveston PD and
Lafayette County officers acted in concert, essentially partnering to “get” Reardon. This satisfies the
“meeting of the minds” for a conspiracy. The objective of the conspiracy was to retaliate against
Reardon and secure his imprisonment, by fabricating a DWI charge. Each conspirator took actions
toward that objective: someone placed the bogus 911 call; Galveston officers made the stop and false
arrest; Mississippi deputies advised on making it a felony and then used the arrest to revoke Reardon’s
probation. The result was a successful deprivation of Reardon’s rights (his liberty was taken, he was
subjected to unreasonable searches, etc.).
Thus, a §1983 conspiracy claim is viable against all individuals involved, even those who did not
personally commit each individual act. For example, Deputy Beavers can be liable not only for the
revocation in Mississippi but also for the illegal arrest in Texas, because she allegedly orchestrated it by
agreementlafayettems.net. Conversely, Officer Osteen can be liable for the Mississippi consequences
because he knowingly entered into the plan to help Lafayette County “do indirectly what it couldn’t do
directly” (i.e., jail Reardon without new cause). The conspiracy claim does not add new constitutional
violations per se, but it is a vehicle to tie all the defendants together and hold each accountable for the
full scope of harm.
Additionally, Reardon could consider a claim under 42 U.S.C. §1985(2) or (3), which address conspiracies
to interfere with civil rights. Section 1985(3) covers conspiracies to deprive equal protection or equal
privileges and immunities. However, it traditionally requires a class-based discriminatory animus (such
as race-based conspiracies). Reardon is not a member of a traditionally protected class, and being a
journalist or whistleblower is not a recognized class under §1985(3). There is an exception for
conspiracies aimed at hindering someone’s equal protection rights or punishing them for exercising
rights, but courts are split on whether purely personal or political vendettas fall under §1985(3). Here,
the motive was retaliatory but not related to race or similar classification, so a §1985(3) claim would
likely fail for lack of class-based animus.
Section 1985(2) has two parts: the first part deals with conspiracies to deter witnesses from attending or
testifying in federal court; the second part (which could apply to state proceedings) involves
conspiracies to interfere with justice in state courts with intent to deprive equal protection. One could
argue that Deputy Beavers and others conspired to impede Reardon’s pursuit of justice in his appeal or
to retaliate for his exposing her perjury (which is tangentially related to a court proceeding). But again,
the equal protection element would be tricky unless we frame Reardon as being denied equal
protection as a class-of-one or as a outspoken critic (not a typical usage). These §1985 avenues are
complex and not as straightforward as a §1983 conspiracy claim, which doesn’t require class-based
animus.
In summary, the best approach is a §1983 conspiracy claim against all involved officers and deputies.
This claim would be outlined in the complaint to ensure the interstate coordination and agreement to
violate rights is front and center. It sets the stage for holding Mississippi actors liable in a Texas federal
court (or vice versa) for the joint misconduct. Each participant can also be liable for failure to prevent
the conspiracy if they were in a position to stop it but didn’t (conceptually related to failure to
intervene, which is another aspect: any officer who knew a fellow officer was violating Reardon’s rights
and had a chance to intercede but didn’t could be liable for that failure under §1983). In this case, for
instance, if Officer Larry Murph or others heard Osteen’s plan to fabricate cause and did nothing, they
too join the conspiracy by acquiescence or are liable for failing to intervene to stop the constitutional
violations.

Civil RICO Claim – Viability of a Racketeering Action
Reardon has inquired about the possibility of a Civil RICO claim given the pattern of coordinated
retaliatory actions. RICO (Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act), 18 U.S.C. §§ 1961-1968,
provides a civil cause of action (with treble damages and attorneys’ fees) for a person injured by a
pattern of racketeering activity conducted through an enterprise. To state a civil RICO claim, Reardon
would need to establish: (1) the existence of an “enterprise” affecting interstate commerce; (2) that
the defendants engaged in a “pattern of racketeering activity” – meaning at least two qualifying
predicate crimes within 10 years; (3) a connection between the racketeering acts and the enterprise;
and (4) that he was injured in his business or property by the racketeering conduct.
Applying this here is challenging but not implausible. The “enterprise” could be an association-in-fact
between the corrupt officials in Texas and Mississippi (essentially a network of individuals who banded
together for this scheme). That enterprise clearly affects interstate commerce in some way (law
enforcement agencies, interstate communications, etc., suffice for the low bar of affecting commerce).
The real hurdle is identifying predicate criminal acts that fall under RICO’s definition of racketeering.
RICO predicates are specific crimes listed in 18 U.S.C. § 1961(1), such as bribery, kidnapping, extortion,
obstruction of justice, mail or wire fraud, witness tampering, etc. We need to see if the defendants’
actions constitute any of those.
Potential predicate acts here:
• Obstruction of Justice / Witness Retaliation (18 U.S.C. §§ 1503, 1512, 1513): One could argue
that Deputy Beavers and others engaged in obstruction of justice by fabricating evidence and
perjuring themselves to affect Reardon’s legal case (e.g., to ensure his probation was revoked
and to cover up her own perjury). Federal obstruction statutes (like §1512) can cover tampering
with a witness or evidence. Reardon was essentially a witness to Beavers’ wrongdoing (he was
appealing her conduct, exposing it). The retaliation against him for producing evidence (the
video) can be framed as retaliation against a witness/informant, which is a federal crime under
§1513 if done by killing or causing bodily harm or property damage. Here they caused harm to
his liberty. It might be a stretch, but one could analogize it to retaliating against someone for
attending or testifying in court (the spirit is similar – he spoke about a court case publicly, and
they hit back).
• Kidnapping or False Imprisonment: RICO predicates include kidnapping (which is typically a
state crime that can be a predicate if punishable by over a year). Was Reardon’s false arrest
effectively a kidnapping? The officers had the trappings of law, but since it was allegedly
unlawful and part of a conspiracy, it could be argued they unlawfully seized him which, outside
color of law, is kidnapping. However, courts might be reluctant to call an arrest “kidnapping”
unless the officers were truly rogue acting entirely outside their authority. If the arrest warrant
was invalid due to fraud, some might consider that akin to a kidnapping under color of law.
There is also a federal crime of conspiracy against rights (18 U.S.C. §241) and deprivation of
rights under color of law (§242), but these are not listed RICO predicates.
• Mail and Wire Fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1343): Often used in civil RICO because almost any
scheme can involve some use of mail or wires. If the conspirators communicated via phone
(wires) or email across state lines to set this up, one might creatively allege wire fraud – but
fraud means a scheme to obtain money or property by false pretenses. Here their scheme was
to deprive Reardon of liberty, not to obtain his property (unless one argues they got him
imprisoned to obstruct his property right in a lawsuit? Unlikely). So mail/wire fraud doesn’t
neatly fit because there’s no clear monetary gain or property of Reardon that they obtained by
deceit. RICO predicate must be one of the enumerated crimes; civil rights violations per se are
not listed.
• Extortion or Hobbs Act (18 U.S.C. §1951): Extortion under color of official right (like public
officials taking bribes) is a predicate. If we twist the facts, were they extorting something from
Reardon? Possibly they wanted him to shut up (stop speaking about Beavers) – that’s not
property, but the Hobbs Act sometimes covers intangible property. However, extortion usually
involves obtaining the victim’s property (e.g., money, or forcing them to give up something
tangible). Telling someone “recant or we’ll jail you” could be considered coercion, but not
clearly a Hobbs Act violation unless it’s about property.
• Bribery: No indication of bribes or money exchanged.
• Witness Tampering (§1512): If one considers Reardon a potential witness in an official
proceeding (his own appeal, or if an investigation into Beavers’ perjury was possible), then
framing him could be seen as tampering – to discredit him or to stop him from testifying.
Section 1512 covers using intimidation, threats, or corrupt persuasion to influence or hinder
testimony or retaliation thereof. They certainly intimidated and retaliated against him. This
could be a strong candidate: 18 U.S.C. §1512(b) (tampering with a witness by harassment or
false evidence) or §1513 (retaliating against a witness/victim) might fit the facts, at least
conceptually.
• Travel Act (18 U.S.C. §1952): If any of them traveled across state lines or used interstate
facilities to promote this unlawful activity, that could be invoked as a predicate. For instance, if
Mississippi deputies traveled to Texas or vice versa in furtherance of the scheme, the Travel Act
could be in play (but normally that act is used for underlying crimes like drug trafficking, not for
civil rights violations).
The pattern requirement means at least two predicate acts. Potentially:
• Act 1: Deputy Beavers committed perjury in 2021 (perjury in federal court is a predicate if it
obstructs justice; in state court not sure if counted, but if it was part of a scheme, maybe
considered obstruction).
• Act 2: In 2023, Beavers and others committed witness retaliation/tampering by framing
Reardon (a violation of §1513 or §1512).
• Or Act 1: Conspiracy to falsely arrest (kidnapping under state law) and Act 2: Witness retaliation
under federal law.
These are legally nuanced, and any civil RICO claim would surely be met with motions to dismiss. Courts
often view attempts to convert a civil rights case into RICO with skepticism, unless there’s clear evidence
of ongoing criminal conduct that fits the statute. RICO is primarily aimed at organized crime and
schemes for economic gain, whereas here the motive was silencing a critic (retaliation), not money.
That said, one advantage of RICO if it fits is the possibility of treble damages and attorney fees, plus a
longer statute of limitations (4 years for civil RICO) which might help if some acts (like 2021 perjury) are
older. It also could be a lever for federal jurisdiction over all parties (though we already have that via
§1983).
Viability assessment: The enterprise could be defined as an informal association of the corrupt officers
in Lafayette County and Galveston. The pattern could be the repeated misuse of law enforcement
powers against Reardon (and maybe others, if evidence shows a broader pattern of these actors framing
or retaliating against other citizens – any prior bad acts by Beavers or Osteen could strengthen a RICO
case by showing a practice). If it’s just Reardon’s incidents, we have at least two incidents (the 2021
false conviction and the 2023 false arrest), which might be enough for two predicate acts. However,
RICO also requires continuity – either open-ended (a threat of continuing racketeering activity) or
closed-ended (a series of related acts over a substantial period). Two incidents over a couple of years
might qualify as a closed-ended pattern, though courts sometimes expect a longer timeframe or
multiple victims. Here, Reardon might argue he is not the only victim – the corruption in Lafayette
County could affect the public at large (e.g., any case where Beavers testified could be tainted, as
Reardon notedlafayettems.netlafayettems.net, he plans to inform other defendants of her lies).
Bottom line on RICO: It’s an aggressive approach that could be pled in the alternative. The clearest
predicates likely involve obstruction of justice (witness tampering/retaliation) and possibly
kidnapping/false imprisonment as state law predicates. If one or more of the defendants used
interstate communications (phone/email) in furtherance of the scheme, one might even attempt to
frame it as wire fraud by arguing they defrauded the State of Mississippi and Texas (obtaining salary
while engaging in unlawful acts, or defrauding the public of honest services). “Honest services fraud” (18
U.S.C. §1346) could be invoked: public officials have a fiduciary duty to provide honest services; if
Beavers and Osteen schemed to deprive their employers and the public of their honest services by
engaging in corruption, and used wires in that scheme, that is a form of wire fraud which is a RICO
predicate. This concept was used in prosecuting corrupt officials (like a sheriff taking bribes – here it’s
not bribes, but they did betray the public trust to carry out a vendetta). It’s novel, but a colorable
argument.
However, pursuing RICO could complicate the case, and courts might sever it or dismiss it if not clearly
supported. Many civil rights cases stick to §1983, since RICO has higher pleading requirements (Rule 9(b)
for fraud predicates, etc.). If Reardon’s goal is comprehensive justice, including RICO as a count might be
worth trying, to claim treble damages and emphasize the organized nature of the misconduct. The
viability is moderate at best; it’s not a slam dunk because it’s atypical to use RICO in a one-victim
retaliation scenario, but it’s not unheard of when officials act as a criminal enterprise (especially across
state lines).
In summary, a civil RICO claim could be brought by defining the enterprise (e.g., “an association-in-fact
enterprise comprised of Deputy Beavers, Deputy James, Officer Osteen, and others, existing for the
common purpose of silencing and punishing Matthew Reardon and others who challenge their
authority”), and listing predicate acts such as:
• Retaliation against a witness (18 U.S.C. §1513) for the scheme to frame Reardon after his
YouTube testimony.
• Possibly obstruction of justice (18 U.S.C. §1503) for destroying or concealing the exculpatory
evidence in 2021 (the missing video evidence from his prior trial, though that might be on state
actors, if done intentionally could be an obstruction predicate).
• Honest services fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1341, 1346) for each official’s deceit of their employer and
misuse of resources in committing these acts (each phone call or email could be a wire fraud
act).
• Kidnapping (Texas Penal Code or Mississippi Code violation) treated as a predicate via
§1961(1)(A) which covers state offenses like kidnapping or bribery if punishable by >1 year.
The risk is a court might find this too tenuous and throw out the RICO count, but including it could
provide leverage in settlement negotiations due to the threat of treble damages and attorney fees.
Conclusion on RICO: It’s an avenue worth exploring with caution. If Reardon can uncover more
evidence of a broader pattern or additional predicate acts (for example, maybe they also tried to frame
him for stalking in Mississippi – indeed he had an earlier charge of stalking the mayor, banishment, etc.,
which could be part of a continuous campaign of retaliation), then the RICO pattern becomes stronger.
If it’s presented as one cohesive campaign from 2017 through 2023 to harass and silence Reardon
involving multiple incidents, that starts to look like an ongoing racket of abusing law enforcement
powers. In any event, a thorough legal analysis would acknowledge RICO’s possibility but likely conclude
that §1983 claims are the more straightforward path and RICO, while possibly viable, would face
significant legal hurdles.

Wrongful imprisonment is a major damage component. Juries often award significant sums for
each day of wrongful incarceration, especially if the plaintiff can show the mental anguish and
life disruptions it caused. Even a few days of wrongful jailing can lead to tens of thousands in
damages; months or years can yield hundreds of thousands or more. For instance, in one case a
man wrongfully jailed for 2 months on false charges received a jury award of $2 million
