Case Overview
The prosecution of Matthew Reardon stems from an arrest that occurred on
August 25, 2025, outside a federal courthouse in Lafayette, Louisiana, allegedly
for conduct violating 41 C.F.R. § 102-74.390, which prohibits loitering, disor-
derly conduct, and other disruptive behavior on federal property [1]. Reardon,
identifiedasajournalistandactivist,hasfiledamotiontodismissthebillofin-
formation, asserting that the charges unconstitutionally infringe upon his First
Amendment rights to free speech, press, and petition for redress of grievances.
ThehearingforthismotionisscheduledforNovember2025andremainspending
before the court. This report analyzes the assessed strength of the defendant's
motiontodismissinconjunctionwiththeU.S.Attorney'sresponse,considering
legal precedent, statutory interpretation, and procedural context.
Strength of the Defendant's Motion to Dismiss
First Amendment Protection in Public Forums
The cornerstone of Matthew Reardon’s motion rests on the legal principle that
expressive activity is constitutionally protected in traditional public forums,
particularly in locations such as the steps and sidewalks outside government
buildings. The defense explicitly relies on United States v. Grace, 461 U.S.
171 (1983), where the Supreme Court aﬀirmed that areas surrounding federal
buildings, including courthouses, function as traditional public forums and are
protected from content-based restrictions on speech [citation: extraction from
Document 16]. Since Reardon was arrested outside the courthouse, this prece-
dent applies directly. The factual confirmation from wtpnews.org that Rear-
don was “violently arrested by U.S. Marshals outside a federal courthouse in
Lafayette, Louisiana” on August 25, 2025, is critical because it places his con-
duct in a classic public forum, not within a nonpublic interior space such as a
lobby or oﬀice area [22].
The Supreme Court’s decision in Edwards v. South Carolina, 372 U.S. 229
(1963), further strengthens the defense’s argument. In Edwards, the Court held
that peaceful protests involving loud and passionate speech on state capitol
grounds were protected, even though they risked inciting unrest. The defense
asserts that Reardon’s actions — including expressive language and recording
public oﬀicials — were even more orderly and protected, as he reportedly com-
plied with orders, unlike the demonstrators in Edwards who refused to disperse.

First Amendment-protected activity:
• Political speech, citing New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254
(1964), in opposition to government conduct;
• Journalistic documentation, including livestreaming and recording,
protected under Branzburg v. Hayes, 408 U.S. 665 (1972), Richmond
Newspapers, Inc. v. Virginia, 448 U.S. 555 (1980), and Glik v. Cunniffe,

cases like United Mine Workers of Am., Dist. 12 v. Illinois State Bar
Ass’n, 389 U.S. 217 (1967), and reinforced by Reardon’s reported intent
to file a FOIA request at the courthouse.

Facial and As-Applied Constitutional Challenges to 41 C.F.R. § 102-
74.390
The regulation at 41 C.F.R. § 102-74.390 prohibits persons on federal property
from “loitering, exhibiting disorderly conduct or exhibiting other conduct on
property” that interferes with operations [1]. The defense argues that the reg-
ulation is both facially overbroad and unconstitutional as applied given
the location of the arrest.
Afacialchallengecontendsthatalawissobroaditchillsprotectedspeecheven
when applied to non-disruptive conduct. By attempting to criminalize expres-
sive activity outside a courthouse, the regulation risks suppressing legitimate
protest and journalism. This is particularly problematic in traditional public
forums, where restrictions must survive strict scrutiny.
Further, the defense cites Brown v. State of La., 383 U.S. 131 (1966), standing
for the principle that even if conduct violates a regulation, charges must be
dismissed if enforcement infringes First Amendment rights. This supports the
claimthatReardon’sconduct,regardlessoftechnicalviolationssuchasnoiseor
loitering, cannot be prosecuted if it is part of protected expressive activity.
Strength of the U.S. Attorney’s Response
Classification of the Forum as Nonpublic
The government's primary legal defense is that the area where Reardon was
arrested constitutes a nonpublic forum, a classification which would lower
the constitutional standard from strict scrutiny to mere reasonableness. This
2
argument relies on Perry Educ. Ass’n v. Perry Local Educators’ Ass’n, 460
U.S. 37 (1983), and Cornelius v. NAACP Legal Def. & Educ. Fund, Inc.,
473 U.S. 788 (1985), which permit reasonable, content-neutral restrictions in
nonpublicforum spaces suchas federal building lobbies, entrances, and parking
areas [citation: government opposition filing].
However, the factual record — confirmed by third-party reporting — under-
mines this claim. Reardon was arrested outside the courthouse, not within a
controlled entranceway. Therefore, Perry and Cornelius are inapplicable, and
thegovernment'sforumclassificationargumentfailsasamatteroffactandlaw.
Claim of Content and Viewpoint Neutrality
The U.S. Attorney argues that § 102-74.390 is content-neutral and viewpoint-
neutral, citing Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 791 (1989), and
Rosenberger v. Rector & Visitors of Univ. of Va., 515 U.S. 819, 828–29 (1995).
According to this position, the regulation prohibits certain behaviors (noise,
obstruction) regardless of the speaker’s message, satisfying the intermediate
scrutiny standard for time, place, and manner regulations.
While facial neutrality is a strong argument, the defense counters with allega-
tions of selective prosecution: Reardon was targeted specifically for criticizing
judicial figures, suggesting content-based enforcement. The presence of a new
prosecutor, Myers P. Namie, added on September 19, 2025, and formally en-
rolledonSeptember22,2025,byorderofMagistrateJudgeCarolB.Whitehurst
[citation: Document 19], does not resolve this concern and could be interpreted
as bolstering prosecutorial capacity in a potentially contentious case. The op-
position filing does not adequately rebut the claim of selective enforcement,
weakening its overall persuasiveness.
Government Interests in Security and Order
The government cites Cox v. Louisiana, 379 U.S. 559 (1965), Adderley v.
Florida, 385 U.S. 39 (1966), and Huminski v. Corsones, 396 F.3d 53 (2d
Cir. 2005), to assert its interest in maintaining courthouse security, access,
and decorum [citation: government opposition filing]. These interests are valid
and substantial, especially in preventing disruptions to judicial proceedings.
However, such interests do not automatically justify arresting an individual
engaged in recording, speaking, or protesting at a distance from courthouse
entrances. The defense maintains that Reardon did not obstruct entrances
or incite violence. If the evidence confirms peaceful conduct and compliance
with marshals (as asserted), then the application of the regulation becomes
unjustified.
Moreover, precedent such as Grayned v. City of Rockford, 408 U.S. 104 (1972),
requires that restrictions be “narrowly drafted and must not frustrate the le-
gitimate exercise of First Amendment rights” [citation: government opposition
3
filing]. A blanket application of § 102-74.390 to journalism and protest outside
a courthouse risks overreach.
Procedural and Jurisdictional Considerations
Authority of Magistrate Judge Whitehurst
A significant procedural issue is the authority of Magistrate Judge Carol B.
Whitehurst to rule on the motion to dismiss. The case docket shows she han-
dled the Motion to Enroll counsel (Document 19), but dispositive motions such
as motions to dismiss in criminal cases typically fall under the jurisdiction of
district judges unless parties consent under 28 U.S.C. § 636(c).
According to 28 U.S.C. § 636(b)(1)(A), magistrate judges may hear and deter-
mine pretrial matters except motions to dismiss or suppress evidence, unless
specificallyreferredbyadistrictjudge. Websearchresultsconfirmthis: ajudge
may assign a magistrate to hear pretrial matters, but “except a motion for in-
junctiverelief,forjudgment,orfordismissal”underRule72oftheFederalRules
of Civil Procedure, which applies analogously in criminal cases [citation:29, ci-
tation:30, citation: opposition filing interpretation].
Therefore,whileJudgeWhitehurstmayconductthehearing,thefinaldecision—
and issuance of a recommended ruling—would likely require adoption by a dis-
trict judge. This procedural nuance may influence the timing and authority of
the upcoming decision.
Combined Assessment of Motion Strength and Likely Out-
come
Weighing the Arguments
• The defendant’s motion is strong because:
– It is factually grounded in a traditional public forum (”outside” the
courthouse) [22].
– It invokes well-established precedent protecting protest, journalism,
and petitioning.
– The facial challenge to § 102-74.390 has merit if applied broadly to
expressive conduct in public areas.
• The government’s response is moderately strong because:
– It correctly articulates content-neutral standards and legitimate se-
curity interests.
– However, it mischaracterizes the location of the arrest (outside vs.
inside).
– It fails to convincingly rebut selective enforcement or overbreadth.
4
Predicted Likelihood of Success
The combined assessment favors the motion to dismiss. Under Grace and
Edwards, speech in outdoor public spaces adjacent to courthouses enjoys the
highestlevelofconstitutionalprotection. Anyattempttoprosecuteajournalist
using a conduct-based regulation must demonstrate clear, narrowly tailored
justification—particularly when expressive activity is involved.
If Reardon’s conduct was peaceful, non-disruptive, and consisted of recording
and verbal protest, prosecution under § 102-74.390 is unlikely to survive con-
stitutional scrutiny. The government would need to prove actual obstruction
or disorderly conduct beyond mere expressive activity, which its current filing
does not decisively establish.
Conclusion
The motion to dismiss has substantial legal strength due to the location of the
incident—outsideafederalcourthouse—makingUnited States v. Gracedirectly
applicable and placing the activity in a traditional public forum protected by
the First Amendment. The defendant’s arguments regarding speech, press, and
petition are well-supported by precedent. The U.S. Attorney’s response raises
legitimate concerns about security and order but fails to overcome the consti-
tutional protections afforded to expressive conduct in public spaces, especially
given the factual misalignment in forum classification.
Final Assessment: The motion to dismiss is likely to be granted following
the November hearing, contingent upon the evidentiary record confirming that
Reardon’s actions did not involve physical obstruction, threats, or actual dis-
order that would justify enforcement of the regulation. The presence of a new
prosecuting attorney does not alter the constitutional calculus. The balance
of legal arguments, when combined with the specific facts of the case, strongly
favors the defendant.
