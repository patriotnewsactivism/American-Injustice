Basis of Knowledge
I make this affidavit based upon my direct, personal observations and experiences as a United
States Marine assigned to Camp Devil Dog, Marine Combat Training East, School of Infantry,
Marine Corps Base Camp Lejeune, North Carolina, during May 2008. I was physically
present at the location where Private First Class Bradley Norman Dante Foust died on May
10, 2008. I witnessed the immediate aftermath of his death, participated in the emergency
response, was questioned extensively by Naval Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) agents,
and was shown the physical evidence of his death by my combat instructor the following
morning.
The events I describe in this affidavit are seared into my memory with the clarity that only
profound trauma provides. Though seventeen years have passed since that night, certain
details remain vivid: the unmistakable sound of three rounds fired in rapid succession, the
controlled chaos of the emergency response, the hours of NCIS questioning, and most
significantly, the explicit statement by a Marine Corps sergeant that PFC Foust had "put his
rifle on three round burst and suck started"—a colloquial military term for suicide by firearm
placed in the mouth.
II. CIRCUMSTANCES OF PRESENCE AT CAMP DEVIL DOG
Assignment to Marine Combat Training
In May 2008, I was a student Marine assigned to Marine Combat Training (MCT) East at
Camp Devil Dog, a training facility within the School of Infantry complex at Marine Corps
Base Camp Lejeune, North Carolina. MCT is the mandatory training all Marines complete
immediately following boot camp, except those proceeding directly to Infantry Training
Battalion.
I had recently graduated from Marine Corps Recruit Depot and was in the early weeks of the
approximately one-month MCT course. The training environment at MCT is characterized by
intense physical demands, severe sleep deprivation, and the psychological transition from
recruit to operational Marine. Students at MCT are not encouraged to form close personal
relationships; the focus is on basic combat skills, weapons handling, and preparation for
follow-on military occupational specialty schools.
Living Conditions and Facility Layout
During my time at Camp Devil Dog in May 2008, student Marines were housed in Quonset
hut-style barracks arranged in rows. These temporary structures are semi-cylindrical metal
buildings that provide basic shelter. The layout of the camp, as I recall it, was as follows:
From the perspective of entering through the main gate:
First row of barracks (where I was housed)
Shower facilities
Armory facility (where PFC Foust died)
Additional training facilities and structures
The armory was a cinder block structure that housed weapons and ammunition for the training
company. It was staffed by a rotating guard detail that included both student Marines and
permanent personnel.
Training Schedule and Sleep Deprivation
A critical contextual element that affects my recall of specific details is the severe sleep
deprivation that characterized MCT. Student Marines operated on minimal sleep—often three
to four hours per night—for extended periods. We would conduct training exercises, stand
watch rotations, perform maintenance, and receive instruction in a continuous cycle that left
little time for rest.
This sleep deprivation is relevant because:
1. It explains why certain peripheral details may be unclear in my memory while the core
traumatic event remains vivid
2. It demonstrates the vulnerability of student Marines during this period
3. It contextualizes the profound impact of witnessing a violent death while already
physically and mentally exhausted
Despite the sleep deprivation, my memory of the specific events of May 10, 2008, and the
morning of May 11, 2008, remains clear because of the traumatic nature of what I witnessed.
III. EVENTS OF MAY 10, 2008
The Night of the Incident
On the night of May 10, 2008, I was in my assigned barracks, the first Quonset hut in the first
row when entering Camp Devil Dog from the main gate. The time was late evening,
approximately between 2200 hours (10:00 PM) and 2400 hours (midnight), though I cannot
state the exact time with certainty.
The Sound of Gunfire
At some point during this timeframe, I heard the distinctive sound of gunfire—specifically,
three rounds fired in rapid succession. Based on my training, I immediately recognized this as
a three-round burst from an M16A2 service rifle.
Significance of Three-Round Burst:
This detail is critical for several reasons:
1. Training Protocol Violation: During MCT training, student Marines are never
authorized to fire weapons in three-round burst mode. All training fire is conducted in
semi-automatic mode only. The deliberate selection of burst mode was immediately
anomalous.
2. Time and Location: There were no authorized live-fire training exercises scheduled at
that hour or in that location within Camp Devil Dog. The sound of live ammunition fire in
the camp itself was completely irregular.
3. Immediate Recognition of Irregularity: My first thought upon hearing the burst was
not that someone had been injured, but rather that someone had obtained live ammunition
and was "playing around" to see what three-round burst felt like—a serious violation that
would result in severe disciplinary action for everyone in the training company.
4. Expectation of Mass Punishment: I specifically remember thinking that we (the entire
training platoon) were "going to get destroyed" as a result of this violation—meaning
subjected to intensive disciplinary physical training and other corrective measures.
The fact that I heard three distinct rounds, not one, and that they were fired in the rapid
cadence characteristic of burst mode, eliminates any possibility that this was an accidental
discharge. A Marine does not accidentally select burst mode, accidentally ensure a round is
chambered, accidentally disengage the safety, accidentally acquire a target, and accidentally
pull the trigger with sufficient pressure to fire three rounds. This was a deliberate action.
Emergency Response
Following the gunfire, there was immediate activity. Though I was not directly involved in the
initial response (as I was in my barracks, not on post), I heard and observed:
1. Shouting and orders being given by instructors
2. Marines moving rapidly toward the armory
3. Emergency response protocols being initiated
The atmosphere shifted instantly from routine late-evening camp operations to crisis response.
Running to the Duty Hut
Though the precise sequence of events immediately following the gunfire is somewhat
unclear due to the chaos and my physical distance from the armory, I was instructed to run to
the duty hut to report what had happened. A Marine Corps sergeant—specifically, Sergeant
Price (or a sergeant whose last name was Price)—ran with me to the duty hut.
The duty hut served as the administrative and communication center for Camp Devil Dog. It
was from this location that communication with higher command would be initiated and from
which the formal emergency response would be coordinated.
Upon reaching the duty hut, I reported what I had heard—the three-round burst of gunfire
from the direction of the armory. The duty personnel immediately initiated emergency
protocols.
NCIS Response and Questioning
Within what I estimate to be 30 minutes to one hour of the incident, agents from the Naval
Criminal Investigative Service (NCIS) arrived at Camp Devil Dog. The rapidity of their
response suggests either they were already on base for other purposes or that the nature of the
incident was immediately understood to require criminal investigation rather than routine
accident response.
Duration and Nature of Questioning:
I was detained at the duty hut and questioned by NCIS agents for approximately three hours.
This was not a brief interview or routine statement-taking. This was intensive, detailed
interrogation focused on:
1. What I heard: The exact nature of the gunfire, number of rounds, timing, my location
when I heard it
2. What I observed: Any visual observations before, during, or after hearing the gunfire
3. What I knew about PFC Foust: Whether I knew him personally, had observed his
behavior, had any indication of his mental state
4. Timeline reconstruction: Precise timing of when I heard the shots, when I ran to the
duty hut, who else was present, who I spoke with
5. Knowledge of other Marines: Whether other Marines had mentioned anything unusual,
whether there had been any prior incidents or concerns
The length and intensity of this questioning indicated that NCIS understood they were
investigating a serious incident, likely a death, and potentially a criminal matter. This was not
the casual information-gathering that would accompany a simple training accident.
Immediate Impression from NCIS Questioning:
The nature of the questions, while never explicitly stating what had occurred, made clear that:
Someone had died
The death involved a weapon and live ammunition
NCIS was conducting a thorough investigation
My observations and testimony were considered significant
After approximately three hours, I was released from questioning and instructed to return to
my barracks. We were told not to discuss the incident.
IV. EVENTS OF MAY 11, 2008 - THE MORNING AFTER
Accountability Formation
The morning following PFC Foust's death, we conducted accountability formation as was
standard procedure. During this formation, all Marines would be accounted for, and any
administrative matters would be addressed before the day's training commenced.
The atmosphere was subdued. There was an unspoken awareness among the Marines that
something significant had occurred, though explicit discussion of the incident had been
discouraged or prohibited.
My Combat Instructor's Direct Statement
During or immediately following the accountability formation, while Marines were still
forming up and preparing for the day, my combat instructor—a sergeant whom I recall as
being somewhat overweight in appearance ("a little bit of a fat body" in Marine Corps
parlance) and who wore glasses—approached me individually.
The Sergeant's Question:
The sergeant asked me directly: "Have you ever seen brains before?"
I responded that I had not—at least not human brains.
The Sergeant's Deliberate Action:
The sergeant then said, "Come here," and deliberately led me to the armory where PFC
Foust had died.
This was not a casual suggestion. This was a direct order from a superior non-commissioned
officer to a junior enlisted Marine. I followed him to the armory.
What I Witnessed at the Scene
Upon entering or approaching the armory, I observed the following:
1. Physical Evidence of Cleaning Efforts:
The area had been treated with bleach, and the overwhelming smell of bleach was present
throughout the space
Despite cleaning efforts, significant evidence remained visible
2. Biological Material:
Blood was still visible on surfaces
Brain tissue was present and visible
Most significantly: Skull fragments were embedded in the cinder block walls
The presence of skull fragments driven into cinder block with sufficient force to become
embedded indicates the extreme violence of the gunshot wounds. This is not evidence
consistent with an accidental discharge or a weapon malfunction. This is evidence consistent
with a contact or near-contact gunshot wound to the head.
3. The Sergeant's Explicit Statement:
After showing me this scene—the blood, the brain tissue, the skull fragments embedded in the
wall—the sergeant stated explicitly what had occurred.
His words, which I remember clearly, were:
"He put his rifle on three round burst and suck started."
Analysis of the Sergeant's Statement
This statement is extraordinary for several reasons:
"Suck started" is military slang for suicide by firearm placed in the mouth. It is crude, direct
terminology that leaves no ambiguity about what is being described.
The sergeant's statement explicitly identified:
1. The method: Firearm placed in the mouth
2. The weapon configuration: M16A2 rifle set to three-round burst mode
3. The cause of death: Suicide, not accident
Why the Sergeant Showed Me This:
I have reflected extensively on why this sergeant made the deliberate decision to show me the
scene and explicitly tell me what had occurred. Several possibilities exist:
1. Recognition of my involvement: Having been present, having heard the shots, having
been questioned by NCIS for three hours, I was already deeply involved in the incident.
Perhaps the sergeant believed I deserved to know the truth rather than be left with
uncertainty.
2. Educational purpose: In the harsh culture of Marine Corps training, there may have
been a belief that witnessing the reality of suicide would serve as a powerful deterrent or
lesson.
3. Processing his own trauma: The sergeant himself had likely been involved in
responding to the scene and may have been processing his own trauma by sharing the
burden with someone else who had been affected.
4. Honesty in the face of impending dishonesty: If the sergeant knew or suspected that the
official classification would be "training accident" rather than suicide, he may have felt a
moral obligation to ensure that at least some Marines knew the truth.
Whatever his motivation, the sergeant's decision to show me the physical evidence and
explicitly state that PFC Foust had committed suicide has proved to be one of the most
significant pieces of evidence contradicting the official "training accident" narrative.
V. OFFICIAL CLASSIFICATION AS "TRAINING ACCIDENT"
Discovery of the Official Classification
In the days and weeks following PFC Foust's death, it became clear that the official
classification of his death would be "training accident," not suicide.
This classification appeared in:
Official casualty reports
Communications with the training company
Obituaries and public death notices
Any official documentation referencing the incident
My Immediate Recognition of Discrepancy
Even as a junior enlisted Marine with limited experience, I found the "training accident"
classification suspicious and inconsistent with what I had witnessed and been told.
Specifically:
1. The Sergeant's Explicit Statement: I had been directly told by a Marine Corps sergeant
that PFC Foust had committed suicide by placing his rifle in his mouth and firing on
three-round burst.
2. The Physical Evidence: The skull fragments embedded in cinder block, the volume and
distribution of biological material, and the overall scene were inconsistent with any
accidental discharge scenario I could conceive.
3. The Nature of the Gunfire: The three-round burst I heard was not consistent with any
training protocol or accidental discharge. It represented a deliberate selection of fire mode
and deliberate trigger pull.
4. The NCIS Response: The intensity and duration of NCIS questioning suggested they
were investigating something more serious than a simple training accident.
However, as a junior Marine in training, I had no authority to question official classifications,
no access to investigative reports, and no platform to voice concerns. Additionally, we had
been instructed not to discuss the incident, which created a climate of enforced silence.
Questions About Family Notification
A question that has haunted me since that night: Did PFC Foust's family know the truth?
I recall that at some point—either shortly after the incident or later in the training cycle—PFC
Foust's mother was brought to Camp Devil Dog. The training company was assembled, and
she addressed us while showing photographs of her son as a child and discussing her grief.
The Memory of This Formation:
I remember this event vividly, though not the exact date it occurred. What I recall is:
1. We had not slept in approximately a month—the cumulative sleep deprivation of MCT
was at its peak
2. Marines were literally putting hand sanitizer in their eyes in a desperate attempt to stay
awake during this formation (a common if inadvisable technique among sleep-deprived
service members)
3. PFC Foust's mother was showing baby pictures and photographs of her son growing up
4. She was visibly devastated, crying throughout her remarks
5. The profound discomfort and guilt of standing there knowing what we knew while she
grieved what she believed to be a "training accident"
The Ethical Weight:
Standing in that formation, exhausted beyond measure, watching a mother grieve her son
while showing us pictures of his childhood, knowing that we had been told it was suicide but
she believed it was an accident—this created a moral burden that persists to this day.
The question remains: Did she know? Was the family told the truth in private while the
public story remained "training accident"? Or were they, like the public and like most of the
Marines, told a sanitized version that protected some institutional interest while denying them
the full truth about their son's death?
VI. THE ADDITIONAL WITNESS: THE BOYFRIEND
Discovery at Communication School
After completing MCT, I was assigned to Military Occupational Specialty school for
Communications Repair (MOS 2800) at Marine Corps Air Ground Combat Center Twenty-
Nine Palms, California.
Twenty-Nine Palms is a remote desert installation in the Mojave Desert, often described as
one of the least desirable duty stations in the Marine Corps. There is limited off-base
recreation, and Marines stationed there tend to spend significant time socializing with each
other in the barracks.
Conversation About the MCT Incident
At some point during communication school—weeks or months after the incident—a group of
us were "sitting there shooting the shit" (engaging in casual conversation). Someone brought
up our time at MCT, and I mentioned the incident where a Marine had "blown his brains out"
at Camp Devil Dog.
The Unexpected Reaction
One Marine in the group—I cannot recall his name with certainty, but I remember he had a
round nose and what Marines call a "dirtbag haircut" (hair that is somewhat outside
regulation, specifically a fade that went from zero directly to three without the gradual
transition)—became visibly and immediately angry.
His reaction was strong: "Man, don't talk about that."
I responded that I would talk about whatever I wanted to unless he gave me a good reason not
to.
The Revelation
At that point, this Marine revealed information that I had not previously known:
PFC Bradley Foust had killed himself because his girlfriend had been unfaithful to him.
More specifically, this Marine revealed:
1. The girlfriend had cheated on Foust while he was home on boot leave (the brief leave
period Marines receive between boot camp and MCT)
2. She had not just cheated abstractly—she had sexual relations with someone else
3. That someone else was this Marine—the man sitting in front of me at Twenty-Nine
Palms
Additional Context Provided
The Marine further explained:
1. He claimed he did not know the woman had a boyfriend when the encounter occurred,
suggesting it was not a deliberate betrayal but rather the result of the woman's dishonesty
2. Foust had found out about this infidelity (presumably through a cell phone that he had
snuck into MCT, as many Marines did despite regulations)
3. This discovery triggered the suicide
Geographic Connections
Later investigation and conversation revealed:
PFC Foust was from Virginia or had connections to Virginia
The Marine who had the encounter with Foust's girlfriend was also from Virginia
This geographic connection explained how the two men, though not knowing each other
personally, could have been connected through this woman
VII. MY VA DISABILITY CLAIM AND DENIAL
Decision to File PTSD Claim
Years after my discharge from the Marine Corps, I made the decision to file a claim with the
Department of Veterans Affairs for service-connected disability compensation for Post-
Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD).
The decision to file this claim was not made lightly. Like many veterans, I struggled with
whether to seek help, whether my experiences "counted" as traumatic compared to combat
veterans, and whether I could adequately explain and document what I had experienced.
However, the symptoms were undeniable:
Intrusive memories of the incident
Sleep disturbances
Hypervigilance
Difficulty with emotional regulation
Persistent anxiety in certain situations
Other symptoms consistent with PTSD
Preparation of Claim
When preparing my PTSD claim, I had to identify the specific traumatic "stressor" event that
caused my condition. For PTSD claims, the VA requires veterans to identify the specific
incident or incidents that led to the disorder, and the VA then attempts to verify that the
stressor occurred.
My Identified Stressor:
I clearly identified the stressor as: The death of PFC Bradley Norman Dante Foust on May
10, 2008, at Camp Devil Dog, Marine Combat Training East, Marine Corps Base Camp
Lejeune, North Carolina.
In my claim, I detailed:
1. That I was present at Camp Devil Dog when PFC Foust died
2. That I heard the gunfire
3. That I participated in the emergency response
4. That I was questioned by NCIS for approximately three hours
5. That I was shown the scene the following morning by my combat instructor
6. That I was explicitly told it was suicide
7. The traumatic nature of these experiences and their lasting impact on my mental health
Evidence Provided to VA
In support of my claim, I provided:
1. My personal account of the incident, written in detail
2. Obituary notices for PFC Bradley Norman Dante Foust, which I located online
3. News article references to the incident, including at least one headline from
RichmondPress.com or a similar publication stating that "Marine Corps investigation into
PFC Bradley Foust death is likely to take months before an answer is released" (this
article was behind a paywall, so I could only access the headline)
The obituaries confirmed:
PFC Bradley Norman Dante Foust's identity
His death occurred on May 10, 2008
His death occurred while serving in the Marine Corps
The official cause was listed as "training accident"
VA's Response: Denial Based on "Cannot Verify"
The Department of Veterans Affairs denied my PTSD claim.
The stated reason for denial: The VA claimed they "could not verify" that the incident
occurred.
Analysis of the VA's Denial
This denial is extraordinary and, I believe, fundamentally fraudulent for the following
reasons:
1. Official Records Confirm the Death Occurred:
The VA has access to official Marine Corps casualty records. These records indisputably
confirm that PFC Bradley Norman Dante Foust died on May 10, 2008, while serving at
Marine Combat Training East. I provided obituaries referencing this death. The death is a
matter of official record.
2. The Issue is Classification, Not Occurrence:
The VA's claim that they "cannot verify" the incident is disingenuous. They can verify that
PFC Foust died on the date and location I specified. What they cannot verify—or claim they
cannot verify—is whether it was suicide or a training accident.
But this distinction is irrelevant to my PTSD claim.
Whether PFC Foust died by suicide or in a training accident, the traumatic impact on me as a
witness is identical:
I heard the gunfire that killed him
I participated in the emergency response
I was questioned for three hours by criminal investigators
I was shown a scene with blood, brain tissue, and skull fragments embedded in walls
I was told explicitly what had occurred
I witnessed his mother's grief
The cause of death does not change the fact that I witnessed and was affected by a violent
death.
3. The VA's Use of False Classification to Deny Claims:
The pattern that emerges is deeply troubling:
Step 1: The Marine Corps classifies PFC Foust's suicide as a "training accident"
Step 2: This false classification becomes part of the official record
Step 3: When veterans who witnessed the actual events file PTSD claims based on those
events, the VA uses the Marine Corps' false classification to claim the events "cannot be
verified"
Step 4: Veterans are denied disability compensation for legitimate trauma based on
institutional dishonesty
This is not administrative error. This is systematic fraud designed to deny benefits to veterans
who witnessed events the military prefers to suppress.
The Absurdity of "Cannot Verify"
Consider the absurdity of the VA's position:
Official casualty records: Confirm PFC Foust died on May 10, 2008, at Camp Devil
Dog
Obituaries: Confirm his death on that date
News reports: Reference the incident and ongoing investigation
My testimony: Provides detailed, specific account of my presence and involvement
Physical evidence: Existed at the scene (though obviously not preserved for my later
claim)
Other witnesses: Exist and could provide corroborating testimony
Marine Corps policy changes: Were implemented specifically in response to this
incident (as documented by Matthew Reardon's FOIA requests)
Yet the VA claims it "cannot verify" that the incident occurred.
What they mean is: "We cannot verify it in a way that would require us to approve your claim
and pay benefits."
Impact of Denial
The denial of my PTSD claim had multiple impacts:
1. Financial Impact:
Denial of monthly disability compensation that I am entitled to
Denial of healthcare benefits for PTSD treatment through the VA system
Ongoing financial strain from seeking private mental health care
2. Psychological Impact:
Invalidation of my trauma and experiences
The message that my suffering is not real or not legitimate
Increased distrust of governmental institutions
Exacerbation of PTSD symptoms through the re-traumatization of having to recount the
incident only to have it dismissed
3. Moral Impact:
The feeling that the truth has been suppressed
The knowledge that other veterans may be experiencing similar denials
The obligation to speak out despite potential consequences
VIII. CONNECTING WITH MATTHEW REARDON
Discovery of Reardon's Investigation
In November 2025—seventeen years after PFC Foust's death—I encountered Matthew Oliver
Reardon's work investigating this incident.
I do not recall the exact mechanism by which I first encountered his investigation (whether
through social media, his podcast "RICO Tapes," or other means), but I recognized
immediately that he was investigating the same incident I had witnessed and that his
investigation was serious, detailed, and based on substantial documentation.
Recognition of Common Experience
Upon reviewing Reardon's materials, I recognized:
1. He had been present at Camp Devil Dog on May 10, 2008
2. He had filed FOIA requests with the Marine Corps seeking information about the incident
3. He had obtained Standard Operating Procedures showing policy changes implemented
after Foust's death
4. His PTSD claim had been denied by the VA on the same grounds as mine—inability to
"verify" the stressor
5. He had experienced severe retaliation for his investigation
The Significance of the SOP Changes
One of the most significant pieces of evidence Reardon obtained through FOIA was
documentation of Standard Operating Procedure changes implemented by the Marine Corps
approximately 3.5 months after PFC Foust's death.
Before May 10, 2008: Standard Operating Procedure ScolO P1500.3N (dated December 14,
2007) explicitly authorized student Marines at Camp Devil Dog to carry loaded weapons with
live security ammunition while performing guard duty at the armory.
After August 25, 2008: Standard Operating Procedure ScolO P1500.3N w/Ch 1 (Change 1,
dated August 25, 2008) prohibited students from carrying security ammunition. Students
could perform guard duty but could not be armed with live ammunition. Only permanent
personnel were authorized to carry loaded weapons.
Why This Policy Change Proves Suicide
This policy change is the "smoking gun" evidence that the Marine Corps knew PFC Foust's
death was suicide:
If the death had truly been a training accident caused by:
Negligent weapons handling
Equipment malfunction
Inadequate training
Inadequate supervision
Then the institutional response would have been:
Enhanced weapons safety training
More intensive supervision during guard duty
Equipment inspections and maintenance procedures
Additional safety briefings
Instead, the Marine Corps:
Removed ammunition access from students entirely
Required permanent personnel supervision for all guard duties
Implemented this change across all entry-level training (MCT, ITB, HQBN)
This is exclusively a suicide prevention measure.
The Marine Corps recognized that unsupervised student access to loaded weapons posed a
suicide risk. They implemented policy changes to eliminate that risk. But they never changed
the official classification of PFC Foust's death from "training accident" to suicide.
They knew it was suicide. They prevented future suicides. But they maintained the lie about
what had already occurred.
Decision to Come Forward
After connecting with Matthew Reardon and understanding the scope of his investigation and
the pattern of VA denials affecting multiple veterans, I made the decision to come forward as a
witness.
This decision was not without risk. Reardon has documented extensive retaliation for his
investigation, including:
Arrest within hours of filing FOIA requests
Multi-year legal persecution
Loss of custody of his children
Incarceration
Loss of housing
Ongoing federal prosecution
Systematic harassment across multiple states
However, several factors compelled me to come forward despite these risks:
1. The Truth Matters: PFC Bradley Norman Dante Foust died by suicide. His family may
not know the truth. The Marine Corps has deliberately falsified records. This cannot stand
unchallenged.
2. Other Veterans Are Being Harmed: The pattern of VA denials based on "cannot verify"
demonstrates that this is not just about one death or one cover-up. This is a systematic
mechanism to deny benefits to veterans traumatized by events the military wants suppressed.
3. My Integrity Has Been Questioned: The VA's denial of my claim essentially calls me a
liar. They are claiming that what I witnessed, what I was told, what I experienced—none of it
is real or verifiable. I will not accept that characterization.
4. I Have Unique Evidence: My testimony about the sergeant explicitly stating that Foust
committed suicide is potentially unique and critical evidence. If I remain silent, this evidence
is lost.
5. Obligation to Fellow Marines: Marines are taught that we never leave a fellow Marine
behind. PFC Foust is being left behind—his truth suppressed, his death mischaracterized, his
memory dishonored. Speaking out is an obligation to a fallen Marine.

X. PATTERNS OF INSTITUTIONAL MISCONDUCT
The Larger Fraud
Based on my experiences and the evidence gathered by Matthew Reardon, I believe the
misclassification of PFC Foust's death is not an isolated incident but rather evidence of a
systematic pattern of fraud by the Marine Corps and potentially other military branches.
Evidence Supporting Systematic Fraud Theory
1. Policy Changes Applied Broadly:
The August 25, 2008 policy change removing ammunition access from students was not
limited to Camp Devil Dog. According to the Standard Operating Procedures obtained by
Reardon, the changes were implemented across:
Marine Combat Training (MCT)
Infantry Training Battalion (ITB)
Headquarters Battalion (HQBN)
All entry-level training environments at School of Infantry East
This broad implementation suggests the Marine Corps recognized a systemic suicide risk
among entry-level students, not just an isolated incident.
2. Institutional Incentives for Misclassification:
During the 2007-2008 timeframe, military suicide rates were a matter of significant public
and congressional concern. High suicide numbers:
Triggered congressional oversight
Resulted in increased scrutiny of command climate
Could affect command careers
Generated negative publicity
Required resource allocation to prevention programs
By contrast, "training accidents"—while tragic—did not carry the same institutional
consequences.
The incentive structure therefore encouraged classification of suicides as accidents.
3. The "Death Benefits" Justification:
I recall being told, either during or shortly after the incident, that the classification as "training
accident" was necessary to ensure PFC Foust's family received death benefits, as suicide
would allegedly disqualify them from certain benefits.
This justification served multiple purposes:
It provided a humanitarian veneer to the misclassification
It discouraged questioning of the official classification
It suggested the deception was for the family's benefit
However, this justification is questionable for several reasons:
Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance (SGLI) generally pays regardless of cause of
death
The death gratuity is not typically withheld for suicide
Even if some benefits were affected, the family's right to truth should not be sacrificed
4. Multiple Veterans Denied Benefits Using Same Mechanism:
The fact that both Matthew Reardon and I filed separate PTSD claims, years apart, both citing
this specific incident as our stressor, and both were denied using the identical justification
("cannot verify") demonstrates this is not random administrative error but rather a systematic
mechanism.
Other Potential Misclassified Deaths
Given the breadth of the policy changes and the institutional incentives for misclassification, I
believe there may be numerous other deaths at Marine Corps training facilities (and
potentially other service branches) that were classified as "training accidents" despite being
suicides.
Questions that demand investigation:

accidents"?

How many involved students versus permanent personnel?
