LEGAL BACKGROUND 
28 U.S.C. Â§ 1915 permits indigent parties to proceed in forma pauperis, waiving filing fees. Subsection (e) 
(2) authorizes courts to dismiss claims that are frivolous, malicious, fail to state a claim, or seek monetary relief from immune defendants. Courts have used this provision to impose mandatory pre-service 
"screening" of indigent complaints. 
The current version of Â§ 1915(e)(2) derives from the Prison Litigation Reform Act of 1996 ("PLRA"), Pub. L. 
No. 104-134, 110 Stat. 1321. While the PLRA's legislative history focuses primarily on prisoner litigation, 
courts have expanded its application to all indigent civil litigants, despite the absence of congressional 
findings justifying such a broad application to non-prisoners. See Lister v. Dep't of Treasury, 408 F.3d 1309, 
1312 (10th Cir. 2005) (applying Â§ 1915(e)(2) to non-prisoner cases without addressing constitutional 
concerns). 
This judicial practice has created an unconstitutional two-tiered system: 
Paying litigants face no pre-service judicial review absent a defendant's motion. 
Indigent litigants must survive secretive, unilateral review by the Court without adversarial 
participation. 
Unlike the Rule 12(b)(6) standard applied to fee-paying plaintiffs, indigent plaintiffs face a heightened risk. 
of dismissal through a non-adversarial process that lacks the procedural safeguards guaranteed to those 
with financial means. This divergence burdens indigent plaintiffs' constitutional rights in violation of well- 
settled law. 
ARGUMENT 
I. The 1915(e)(2) Screening Process Violates the First Amendment Right to Petition for 
Redress 
The First Amendment guarantees not only the right to speak freely, but the right to petition the government for redress of grievances-including through access to the courts. The Supreme Court has 
consistently recognized this right as fundamental to our constitutional structure: 
"The right of petition is one of the freedoms protected by the Bill of Rights, and we cannot... close our 
eyes to the fact that the effective exercise of this right is the most likely way to obtain a peaceful redress of grievances." 
- NAACP v. Button, 371 U.S. 415, 429-31 (1963). 
"The right of access to the courts is indeed but one aspect of the right of petition." 
California Motor Transport Co. v. Trucking Unlimited, 404 U.S. 508, 510 (1972). 
"The First Amendment right to petition Government for redress of grievances includes a right of access 
to the courts." 
Borough of Duryea v. Guarnieri, 564 U.S. 379, 387 (2011). 
Courts must be particularly vigilant when procedural barriers disproportionately impact those seeking to 
vindicate constitutional rights. See NAACP v. Button, 371 U.S. at 438 ("[I]n the context of NAACP 
objectives, litigation is not a technique of resolving private differences; it is a means for... obtaining the 
vindication of legal rights."). When First Amendment rights are at stake, "the Court has emphasized that 
standards of permissible statutory vagueness are strict." Button, 371 U.S. at 432. 
The government may not impose burdens on the right to petition that are discriminatory, chilling, or based on wealth status. Yet that is precisely what Â§ 1915(e)(2) screening does. It imposes a second-class 
procedure on indigent litigants, subjecting them to delays, stays, and dismissals before their grievances 
even reach an adversarial process. This creates what the Supreme Court has termed "inequitable 
treatment under the law." M.L.B. v. S.L.J., 519 U.S. 102, 120 (1996). 
The harm is not merely theoretical. In this case, the screening process has prevented Plaintiff from documenting ongoing retaliation by government officials directly connected to his original claims. This 
demonstrates what the Supreme Court has described as "the real risk of 'cutting off... access to events or 
information which we would know if free to... inquire."" Branzburg v. Hayes, 408 U.S. 665, 728 (1972) (Stewart, J., dissenting). 
By treating the right to access courts as conditional upon surviving an ex parte screening based solely on economic status, the current practice infringes the First Amendment right to petition for redress-a right 
that "extends to all departments of the Government," including courts. California Motor Transport, 404 
U.S. at 510. 
II. The 1915(e)(2) Screening Regime Violates the Fifth Amendment's Due Process 
Clause 
Procedural due process imposes constraints on governmental decisions that deprive individuals of 
"liberty" or "property" interests. Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 332 (1976). The Supreme Court has consistently held that: 
"The fundamental requisite of due process of law is the opportunity to be heard... at a meaningful time 
and in a meaningful manner." 
Goldberg v. Kelly, 397 U.S. 254, 267 (1970) (citations omitted). 
Before government action deprives an individual of a protected right-such as access to the courts-the 
individual must receive notice and an opportunity to be heard. Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust 
Co., 339 U.S. 306, 313 (1950). The Court has specifically recognized that "access to the courts is a 
constitutionally protected component of the due process right." Boddie v. Connecticut, 401 U.S. 371, 376 
(1971). 
The Â§ 1915(e)(2) screening process violates these bedrock principles in multiple ways: 
â¢ No Notice: Plaintiffs are not formally notified of the specific deficiencies being considered before 
dismissal. 
â¢ No Opportunity to be Heard: Plaintiffs are denied a chance to amend, argue, or correct alleged 
deficiencies before dismissal. 
â¢ No Adversarial Testing: The entire screening process occurs ex parte, without the truth-testing function of adversarial presentation. 
This contrasts sharply with the process afforded to fee-paying litigants. In all other civil actions, dismissal 
for failure to state a claim occurs only after the defendant moves under Rule 12(b)(6), the plaintiff has an 
opportunity to respond, and both sides present their arguments. The Supreme Court has emphasized 
that "fairness can rarely be obtained by secret, one-sided determination of facts decisive of rights." Joint 
Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath, 341 U.S. 123, 170 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring). 
In Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976), the Court established a three-part test for procedural due process claims: 
1. The private interest affected by the official action; 
2. The risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used, and the probable value of additional 
procedural safeguards; and 
3. The Government's interest, including the function involved and fiscal and administrative burdens. 
Applied here: 
1. The private interest is substantial-access to courts to vindicate constitutional rights. 
2. The risk of erroneous deprivation is high-non-adversarial, secretive screening without notice or 
opportunity to respond. 
3. The government interest is minimal-judicial resources are not significantly conserved by pre-service 
screening when compared to standard Rule 12 practice. 
As Justice Frankfurter observed, "The validity and moral authority of a conclusion largely depend on the 
mode by which it was reached." Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm., 341 U.S. at 171 (Frankfurter, J., 
concurring). By subjecting indigent plaintiffs to a procedurally deficient screening process, Â§ 1915(e)(2) violates fundamental due process principles. 
III. The 1915(e)(2) Screening Violates Equal Protection Under the Fifth Amendment 
Though the Equal Protection Clause explicitly applies to the States via the Fourteenth Amendment, the 
Supreme Court has recognized an analogous equal protection guarantee under the Fifth Amendment's 
Due Process Clause that constrains federal action. Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497 (1954). 
"[T]he concepts of equal protection and due process, both stemming from our American ideal of 
fairness, are not mutually exclusive." 
- Bolling, 347 U.S. at 499. 
The Court has specifically recognized that discrimination based on wealth in the context of court access triggers heightened scrutiny, not mere rational basis review: 
"The equal protection concern relates to the legitimacy of fencing out would-be appellants based solely on their inability to pay core costs... [This] classification, which turns on the ability to pay, [is] examined with heightened scrutiny." 
M.L.B. v. S.L.J., 519 U.S. 102, 120-24 (1996). 
In Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), the Court established the principle that states may not condition 
access to courts on ability to pay, declaring that "there can be no equal justice where the kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of money he has." Id. at 19. 
This principle has been consistently reaffirmed: 
"The State cannot adopt procedures which leave an indigent defendant entirely cut off from any 
appeal at all, by virtue of his indigency." 
Lane v. Brown, 372 U.S. 477, 481 (1963). 
"[O]nce the Government chooses to provide such protections [as court access], it may not discriminate 
against the indigent in affording them." 
Britt v. North Carolina, 404 U.S. 226, 227 (1971). 
Under the Â§ 1915(e)(2) screening regime: 
â¢ Indigent plaintiffs are segregated into a less favorable procedural system. 
â¢ The sole basis for differential treatment is poverty. 
â¢ No compelling (or even rational) justification exists for this discrimination. 
This classification is particularly suspect because it both discriminates based on wealth status and burdens. 
a fundamental right-access to courts. The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that such dual-nature 
classifications trigger the most stringent judicial scrutiny. See Harper v. Virginia State Bd. of Elections, 383 
U.S. 663, 670 (1966) ("[W]here fundamental rights and liberties are asserted under the Equal Protection 
Clause, classifications which might invade or restrain them must be closely scrutinized."). 
The Â§ 1915(e)(2) screening regime cannot survive such scrutiny. As Justice Douglas observed, "The Equal 
Protection Clause requires that these appellate procedures be administered with an even hand." Rinaldi v. 
Yeager, 384 U.S. 305, 309-10 (1966). 
IV. Heightened Scrutiny Must Apply to These Constitutional Violations 
The constitutional violations described above trigger heightened scrutiny for multiple reasons: 
1. Fundamental Right: Access to courts is a fundamental right. Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509, 533-34 
(2004) (describing "the right of access to the courts" as "a fundamental right protected by the 
Constitution"). 
2. Suspect Classification: Wealth-based classifications that affect fundamental rights are inherently 
suspect. Harper, 383 U.S. at 668 ("Lines drawn on the basis of wealth or property, like those of race, 
are traditionally disfavored."). 
3. First Amendment Rights: Burdens on First Amendment rights trigger strict scrutiny. Button, 371 U.S. 
at 438 ("The decisions of this Court have consistently held that only a compelling state interest... can justify limiting First Amendment freedoms."). 
Under heightened scrutiny, the government must demonstrate that the challenged practice is narrowly 
tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest. See Washington v. Glucksberg, 521 U.S. 702, 721 
(1997). 
The Â§ 1915(e)(2) screening process fails this test:

service review regardless of the nature or merit of the claims.

Less restrictive alternatives exist, such as applying the same post-service review standards to all litigants regardless of economic status. 
As the Supreme Court has stated, "The Constitution cannot confer the right to proceed in forma pauperis 
and then make its exercise frustrating and futile." Lane, 372 U.S. at 484-85.
