IV. STANDARD OF REVIEW
11. “When considering a motion to dismiss, the court accepts as true the well-pled
factual allegations in the complaint and construes them in light most favorable to the
plaintiff.” Taylor v. Books A Million, Inc., 296 F.3d 376, 378 (5th Cir. 2002); see also U.S.
ex rel. Willard v. Humana Health Plan of Tex., Inc., 336 F.3d 375, 379 (5th Cir. 2003)
(internal citation omitted); see also True v. Robles, 571 F.3d 412, 417 (5th Cir. 2009).
“Dismissal is improper if the allegations support relief on any possible theory.” Wilson v.
Birnberg, 667 F.3d 591, 595 (5th Cir. 2012) (quotation marks omitted) (quoting Cinel v.
Connick, 15 F.3d 1338, 1341 (5th Cir. 1994)). “[O]f course, a wellpleaded complaint may
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 10
proceed even if it strikes a savvy judge that actual proof of the facts alleged is improbable,
and ‘that a recovery is very remote and unlikely.’” Bell Atl. Corp. v. Twombly, 550 U.S.
544, 556 (2007) (quoting Scheuer v. Rhodes, 416 U. S. 232, 236 (1974)).
12. “[T]o survive a motion to dismiss, a complaint need not contain ‘detailed factual
allegations’; rather, it need only allege facts sufficient to “state a claim for relief that is
plausible on its face.” Littell v. Houston Independent School District, 213 F. Supp. 3d 849
(5th Cir. 2016) (quoting Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009). “Dismissal is
appropriate only if the complaint fails to plead ‘enough facts to state a claim of relief that
is plausible on its face.’” Leal v. McHugh, 731 F.3d 405, 410 (5th Cir. 2013) (quoting
Twombly, 550 U.S. at 570). A claim is plausible on its face “when the pleaded factual
content allows the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable for the
misconduct alleged.” Montoya v. FedEx Ground Package Sys., Inc., 614 F.3d 145, 148 (5th
Cir. 2010) (quoting Ashcroft v. Iqbal, 556 U.S. 662, 678 (2009)). This Court in Thomas v.
City of Galveston, 800 F. Supp. 2d 826, 841 (S.D. Tex. 2011) held that:
[A]ll the Rules require is “a short and plain statement of the claim” that will
give the defendant fair notice of what the plaintiff’s claim is and the grounds
upon which it rests. . . .
In the context of municipal liability, as opposed to individual officer liability,
it is exceedingly rare that a plaintiff will have access to (or personal
knowledge of) specific details regarding the existence or absence of internal
policies or training procedures prior to discovery.
Accordingly, only minimal factual allegations should be required at the
motion to dismiss stage. Moreover, those allegations need not specifically
state what the policy is, as the plaintiff will generally not have access to it,
but may be more general.
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 11
Id. at 842-43 (internal citations omitted).
13. A Rule 12(b)(6) motion to dismiss must be evaluated solely on the basis of the
pleadings. See Morin v. Caire, 77 F.3d 116, 120 (5th Cir. 1996); Jackson v. Procunier, 789
F.2d 307, 309 (5th Cir. 1986). A court reviewing a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6)
may consider “(1) the facts set forth in the complaint, (2) documents attached to the
complaint, and (3) matters of which judicial notice may be taken under Federal Rule of
Evidence 201.” Rivera v. City of Pasadena, 555 F. Supp. 3d 443, 453 (S.D. Tex. 2021)
(citing Inclusive Cmtys. Project, Inc. v. Lincoln Prop. Co., 920 F.3d 890, 900 (5th Cir.
2019)). The Court’s inquiry should focus on the complaint as a whole, “regardless of how
much of it is discussed in the motion to dismiss.” Wilson v. Birnberg, 667 F.3d 591, 595
(5th Cir. 2012).
V. ARGUMENTS AND AUTHORITIES
A. Plaintiff has pled facts supporting a Fourth Amendment claim.
1. Plaintiff was seized.
14. Plaintiff plausibly alleges that Chief Roy seized him by intentionally striking him
with a City vehicle. “There can be constructive detention, which will constitute an arrest,
although the party is never actually brought within the physical control of the party making
an arrest. This is accomplished by merely touching, however slightly, the body of the
accused, by the party making the arrest and for that purpose, although he does not succeed
in stopping or holding him even for an instant.” California v. Hodari D., 499 U.S. 621, 625
(1991) (internal citations omitted.). “We think it enough for a seizure that a person be
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 12
stopped by the very instrumentality set in motion or put in place in order to achieve that
result.” Brower v. Cnty. of Inyo, 489 U.S. 593, 599 (1989). A civilian is “seized the moment
the officer used force . . .” Vess v. City of Dall., 608 F. Supp. 3d 434, 445-46 (N.D. Tex.
2022).
15. The Complaint alleges that Chief Roy intentionally drove a fire department vehicle
into the pedestrian area and made contact with Plaintiff, causing bodily injury. Such
conduct—use of a vehicle to physically control a pedestrian’s movement—qualifies as a
seizure under clearly established law.
2. Plaintiff alleges a claim of excessive force.
16. The allegations support an excessive force claim. Under Graham v. Connor, 490
U.S. 386, 397 (1989), the Court evaluates excessive force by considering: (1) the severity
of the crime at issue, (2) whether the suspect posed an immediate threat, and (3) whether
he actively resisted. Here, Plaintiff posed no threat, committed no crime, was not resisting,
and was not interfering with emergency operations. The Complaint asserts that Chief Roy’s
actions were motivated by annoyance and malice, not any need for governmental
intervention. These facts meet the standard for excessive force under Flores v. City of
Palacios, 381 F.3d 391, 399 (5th Cir. 2004).
17. Plaintiff has alleged: (1) a physical injury (herniated discs, hearing loss, etc.); (2) use
of force grossly disproportionate to any need (vehicle contact without provocation or
threat); and (3) objective unreasonableness (no split-second emergency; ample time to
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 13
assess the scene). See id. These facts are sufficient under Fifth Circuit standards to survive
dismissal.
i. Chief Roy did not reasonably respond to the scene of an emergency.
18. Defendants repeatedly assert that Chief Roy was responding to an emergency,
justifying the use of lights, sirens, and driving maneuvers. This characterization is directly
contradicted by the allegations in the Complaint and the attached video evidence. Chief
Roy’s use of his vehicle occurred after the emergency had passed. Plaintiff was not
obstructing emergency operations, and video footage shows first responders standing idly,
with one officer explicitly stating that there was no fire. (Plaintiff’s Recording, 3:45).
Further, Chief Roy exited the parking lot moments after striking Plaintiff, suggesting he had
no legitimate emergency-related reason to be in that area.
19. “Excessive force claims are necessarily fact-intensive; whether the force used is
‘excessive’ or ‘unreasonable’ depends on ‘the facts and circumstances of each particular
case.’” Deville v. Marcantel, 567 F.3d 156, 167 (5th Cir. 2009), citing Graham v. Connor,
490 U.S. 386, 396 (1989). Even in the context of law enforcement, courts have emphasized
that a vehicle used to strike a person must be narrowly justified by a legitimate threat or
imminent need. The absence of any such need here renders Chief Roy’s actions objectively
unreasonable.
ii. Plaintiff alleges injuries greater than de minimis under the circumstances.
20. Defendants argue that Plaintiff’s injuries are insufficient to support a constitutional
claim. However, the Complaint alleges that Plaintiff suffered herniated discs, torn
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 14
ligaments, hearing loss, and ongoing pain and impairment requiring medical treatment.
These injuries are well beyond the “de minimis” threshold and meet the Fifth Circuit’s
injury requirement under Solis v. Serrett, 31 F.4th 975, 981 (5th Cir. 2022) (“[A]lthough a
de minimis injury is not cognizable, the extent of injury necessary to satisfy the injury
requirement is ‘directly related to the amount of force that is constitutionally permissible
under the circumstances. . . if the officer’s force was unreasonably excessive, [the plaintiff]
need only show ‘some injury.’” Internal citations omitted.). The physical impact of a 5,000-
pound fire vehicle—even if limited to a mirror strike—can cause severe and lasting injuries,
as confirmed by Plaintiff’s medical records.
21. Further, the Court must take these factual allegations as true at this stage. Twombly
and Iqbal require plausibility, not proof. See Montoya v. FedEx Ground Package Sys., 614
F.3d 145, 148 (5th Cir. 2010) (a complaint survives dismissal where it alleges “enough facts
to state a claim for relief that is plausible on its face”).
B. Plaintiff pleads facts which plausibly show a claim under the Fourteenth
Amendment.
22. Plaintiff’s alternative claim under the Fourteenth Amendment is based on conduct
that shocks the conscience — namely, using a vehicle to intentionally harm a pedestrian.
23. Where force is applied outside the context of a lawful seizure or detention, the
substantive due process protections of the Fourteenth Amendment are implicated. See
County of Sacramento v. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 849 (1998) (“conduct intended to injure in
some way unjustifiable by any government interest is the sort of official action most likely
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 15
to rise to the conscience-shocking level.”). Conduct that “shocks the conscience” and is
deliberately indifferent to a person’s bodily integrity violates the Fourteenth Amendment.
Id. at 846–47. Therefore, if Chief Roy was only attempting to strike Plaintiff rather than
detain him, a Fourteenth Amendment violation has occurred.
24. The Complaint alleges that Chief Roy was not performing any law enforcement or
emergency task when he intentionally drove his vehicle toward Plaintiff. This act—done
out of annoyance or malice and without justification—was arbitrary and conscience-
shocking. See Shillingford v. Holmes, 634 F.2d 263, 265 (5th Cir. 1981) (use of force against
a pedestrian who was not interfering with police operations stated a due process claim).
25. At a minimum, Plaintiff has alleged facts that state an alternative constitutional basis
for relief. The existence of both Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment theories does not bar
proceeding under both at this stage. See Petta v. Rivera, 143 F.3d 895, 910 n.25 (5th Cir.
1998) (“where a plaintiff's excessive force claim, whether he be a prisoner, arrestee,
detainee, or an innocent bystander of tender years, falls outside the specific protections of
the Bill of Rights, that plaintiff may still seek redress under the due process clause of the
Fourteenth Amendment.”); see also Shillingford v. Holmes, 634 F.2d 263, 265 (5th Cir.
1981) (“The right to be free of state-occasioned damage to a person’s bodily integrity is
protected by the fourteenth amendment guarantee of due process. The right has also been
premised on the fourth amendment guarantee of ‘(The) right of … people to be secure in
their persons,’ made applicable to the states by the fourteenth amendment.” Internal
citations omitted).

26. The specific fact question, which cannot be resolved prior to discovery, of whether
Chief Roy intended to detain Plaintiff or merely to harm him will determine whether
Plaintiff is entitled to relief under the Fourth Amendment, Fourteenth Amendment, or both.
C. Plaintiff pleads facts which plausibly overcome qualified immunity.
27. Qualified immunity shields officials only when their conduct does not violate clearly
established law. See Harlow v. Fitzgerald, 457 U.S. 800, 818 (1982). In assessing qualified
immunity, courts ask (1) whether the official violated a constitutional right, and (2) whether
that right was clearly established at the time. Saucier v. Katz, 533 U.S. 194, 201 (2001).
Plaintiff has adequately alleged both.
1. Clearly established legal authority prohibited Chief Roy’s conduct.
28. The right to be free from excessive force when not posing a threat is clearly
established. See Cole v. Carson, 905 F.3d 334, 344 (5th Cir. 2018) (“where a suspect posed
no threat, ‘the violation of his constitutional rights would have been obvious even without
a body of relevant case law,’” internal citations omitted); see also Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S.
730, 739–41 (2002) (“a general constitutional rule already identified in the decisional law
may apply with obvious clarity to the specific conduct in question, even though ‘the very
action in question has [not] previously been held unlawful.’” Internal citations omitted.).
29. Further, in Checki v. Webb, 785 F.2d 534, 538 (5th Cir. 1986), the court held that
using a vehicle to intimidate or harm a civilian can violate clearly established constitutional
rights. The conduct alleged—accelerating a government vehicle toward a civilian, causing
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 17
injury—is within the ambit of clearly established law prohibiting arbitrary, malicious abuse
of power. Thus, Chief Roy is not entitled to qualified immunity at this stage.
30. Not only was this prohibition clearly established by prior case law, but also by Texas
statute. The Texas Transportation Code requires that emergency vehicles be operated safely
and without “reckless disregard for the safety of others.” Tex. Transp. Code §546.005. As
alleged in the Complaint, “Chief Roy’s actions of accelerating into a parking lot across a
pedestrian right-of-way, where he knows there are pedestrians and where pedestrians are
likely to be present, involves an extreme degree of risk considering the probability and
magnitude of potential harm to the pedestrians. Chief Roy had actual knowledge of the
Plaintiff’s presence directly in his path where Chief Roy accelerated his vehicle. Despite
this awareness, Chief Roy proceeded to accelerate towards Plaintiff, demonstrating a
conscious indifference to the rights, safety, or welfare of Plaintiff and other pedestrians.”
(Doc. 1, ¶51-52). Therefore, Chief Roy’s conduct was prohibited by statute as well as case
law.

response to the fire scene in this case was reasonable or not prohibited by well-

31. Here, Chief Roy had time to assess the situation and was not responding to a true
emergency. No reasonable official could believe that intentionally striking a pedestrian
with a truck in an otherwise calm situation was lawful conduct.

D. Plaintiff has alleged facts which state a plausible claim against the City.
32. Municipal liability under 42 U.S.C. §1983 attaches where a constitutional violation
results from (1) a policy or custom, (2) the decision of a final policymaker, or (3) ratification
of unconstitutional conduct. See Monell v. Dep’t of Soc. Servs., 436 U.S. 658, 694 (1978);
Pembaur v. City of Cincinnati, 475 U.S. 469, 480 (1986); Grandstaff v. City of Borger, 767
F.2d 161, 171 (5th Cir. 1985).
33. Defendants’ arguments in ¶¶36-45 of the Motion narrowly focus on the absence of a
broader “pattern or practice,” which is inapposite to Plaintiff’s theories of direct
policymaker liability and ratification. These alternative paths to Monell liability are
recognized by the Fifth Circuit and are independently sufficient to state a claim. These
claims go unaddressed in the Motion, and therefore Defendants’ Motion must be denied.
1. Decision by a policymaker.
34. Plaintiff has alleged that Battalion Chief Roy was a policymaker for purposes of
operations and discipline within the Galveston Fire Department: “A Battalion Chief is a
senior-level official within the Galveston Fire Department, under only the Fire Chief and
Assistant Fire Chief. Battalion chiefs are policymakers within the Galveston Fire
Department, responsible for setting and implementing policy regarding operations, training,
and discipline within their battalion.” (Doc. 1, ¶15).
35. “A policymaker is an employee ‘whose responsibilities require more than simple
ministerial competence, whose decisions create or implement policy, and whose discretion
in performing duties or in selecting duties to perform is not severely limited by statute,
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 19
regulation, or policy determinations made by supervisors.’” Wiggins v. Lowndes Cty., 363
F.3d 387, 390 (5th Cir. 2004) (quoting Stegmaier v. Trammell, 597 F.2d 1027, 1035 (5th
Cir. 1979)). Chief Roy clearly meets this definition, and is therefore a City policymaker.
36. Plaintiff has alleged that a City policymaker, acting in his official capacity, deprived
Plaintiff of his rights to be free from excessive force. See Brown v. Bryan Cnty., 219 F.3d
450, 462 (5th Cir. 2000) (“a single decision by a policy maker may, under certain
circumstances, constitute a policy for which the County may be liable.”); see also Pembaur
v. City of Cincinnati, 475 U.S. 469, 481 (1986) (“it is plain that municipal liability may be
imposed for a single decision by municipal policymakers under appropriate
circumstances.”).
37. Chief Roy’s conduct, if found to be a deliberate act within the scope of his
policymaking authority, constitutes municipal policy for which the City is liable. See Brown
v. Bryan Cnty., 219 F.3d 450, 462 (5th Cir. 2000).
2. Ratification.
38. Plaintiff has further alleged that the City has ratified Chief Roy’s actions, which
separately gives rise to a legal claim. See Young v. Bd. of Supervisors of Humphreys Cnty.,
927 F.3d 898, 903 (5th Cir. 2019) (“One way of establishing liability is to show that a
policymaker ratified the acts of a subordinate.”); see also Covington v. City of Madisonville,
812 F. App’x 219, 228-29 (5th Cir. 2020) (reversing the dismissal of a ratification claim
under Monell where the policymakers had covered up the perpetrator’s misconduct and
failed to turn over recordings to investigators). The City’s refusal to discipline Chief Roy,
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 20
the destruction or suppression of evidence, and the closure of any internal or criminal
investigation, as described in Plaintiff’s Complaint (Doc. 1, ¶¶17, 18, 20, 22, 24-27),
constitute ratification. See Milam v. City of San Antonio, 113 F. App’x 622, 628 (5th Cir.
2004) (ratification may be shown by inaction, cover-up, or affirming unconstitutional acts).
39. The City’s ratification of Chief Roy’s conduct is also evidence of a broader City
policy: “The failure to take disciplinary action in response to [illegal conduct], when
combined with other evidence, could tend to support an inference that there was a
preexisting de facto policy of [illegal conduct]: the policymaker did not discipline the
employee because, in the policymakers’ eyes, the employee’s illegal conduct actually
conformed with municipal policy.” Milam, 113 F. App’x at 628 (5th Cir. 2004); see also
McRorie v. Shimoda, 795 F.2d 780, 784 (9th Cir. 1986) (suggesting that failure of officials
to admit the employees’ conduct was in error could be interpreted as a municipal policy.)
E. Plaintiff states a claim under Texas law.
40. Plaintiff’s alternative state-law claims—brought under the Texas Tort Claims Act
(“TTCA”), Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §§101.001 et seq.—are properly pled and not

1. The Texas election of remedies statute does not bar a suit against Chief Roy in his
individual capacity.
41. While Plaintiff agrees here that state law claims against Chief Roy are barred, the
election of remedies provision of the Texas Tort Claims act does not bar an action under
federal law. Plaintiff’s claims against Chief Roy in his individual capacity under §1983 are

constitutionally based and are not barred by the election-of-remedies statute. See
Tex. Dep't of Aging & Disability Servs. v. Cannon, 453 S.W.3d 411 (Tex. 2015) (holding
§1983 claims against employees in their individual capacity are not subject to §101.106(e)).
Therefore, if it is found that Chief Roy intentionally deprived Plaintiff of his constitutional
right to substantive due process or to be free from unreasonable seizure, Chief Roy will be
personally liable for that deprivation. See also Mission Consol. Indep. Sch. Dist. v. Garcia,
253 S.W.3d 653, 659 (Tex. 2008) (“Claims against the government . . . that exist apart from
the Tort Claims Act are not brought under [the Tort Claims Act].”).
2. The City is not immune from claims asserted under Texas law.
42. Plaintiff alleges that he was struck by a City-owned vehicle operated by a City
employee, acting in the scope of employment. This directly invokes the waiver of immunity
found in Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §101.021(1)(A), which waives governmental
immunity for “property damage, personal injury, and death arising from the operation or
use of a motor-driven vehicle.”
i. Plaintiff did not fail to allege facts plausibly showing that he timely submitted
notice of a claim to the City in accordance with Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code

43. Under the TTCA, a party seeking to impose liability must provide a governmental
entity formal, written notice not later than six months after the day the incident giving rise
to the claim occurred, reasonably describing (1) the damage or injury claimed; (2) the time
and place of the incident; and (3) the incident. Cathey v. Booth, 900 S.W.2d 339, 340 (Tex.

1995); Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code Ann. §101.101(a). The formal notice requirement does
not apply “if the governmental unit has actual notice of the death, injury, or property damage
claimed.” Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code Ann. §101.101(c).
44. Written notice was provided within the statutory period: “On August 28, 2024,
Plaintiff sent the City of Galveston notice of his claim as required by Texas Civil Practice
and Remedies Code §101.101. As demonstrated by the facts cited above, however,
representatives of the City of Galveston had actual knowledge of Plaintiff’s injuries and the
City of Galveston’s potential culpability immediately after the incident occurred.” (Doc. 1,
¶28). The incident occurred on June 22, 2024. August 28, 2024, is within the six months
required by statute to provide notice of such a claim, and so Plaintiff has pled facts showing
that this notice requirement was met.
45. Actual notice was given immediately when police and fire officials responded to the
scene. See §101.101(c) (notice requirement is satisfied when a governmental unit has actual
notice of the injury and its potential liability). Plaintiff explicitly alleged: “Defendant City

Defendants’ potential liability immediately upon the occurrence of the collision. Plaintiff
also sent notice to Defendant City of Galveston as required by law.” (Doc. 1, ¶65).
46. Therefore, even if the written notice requirement was somehow not met, Plaintiff
has pled that Defendants had actual knowledge of Plaintiff’s claim, which would mean that
the formal notice requirement is not required. Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code Ann.
§101.101(c).

liable under the TTCA if Chief Roy’s actions were merely negligent or reckless, or liable

iii. Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §101.055(2) in inapplicable.
51. Defendants’ contention that the emergency-response exception under §101.055(2)
applies is premature and fact-bound. The statute applies only if the employee is responding
to an actual emergency and complies with applicable laws and ordinances. City of San
Antonio v. Maspero, 640 S.W.3d 523, 530 (Tex. 2022). Here, Plaintiff has alleged—and
video evidence supports—that Chief Roy’s actions were not part of an emergency response,
and no legitimate emergency existed at the time of the incident.
52. According to the Complaint and video evidence, the scene was calm, with no fire or
ongoing hazard. First responders stated on camera that there was no fire, and Chief Roy
did not take action consistent with fire suppression or rescue. After striking Plaintiff, he
exited the scene without performing emergency duties.
53. The existence of an emergency is a question of fact. Here, Plaintiff has plausibly
alleged there was no emergency, making §101.055(2) inapplicable at the motion to dismiss
stage.
54. Furthermore, Chief Roy was not in compliance with applicable laws. Even if
Defendants argue an emergency existed, the exception fails if Chief Roy’s conduct was
unlawful. See Maspero, 640 S.W.3d at 530 (emergency exception does not apply if the
employee violates applicable traffic laws or otherwise acts unlawfully).
55. Plaintiff alleges that Chief Roy maneuvered a fire truck to strike a pedestrian, failed
to yield or act with due regard for pedestrian safety, and took no steps to avoid the contact
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 25
or de-escalate. These are clear factual allegations of noncompliance with traffic and safety
laws, which preclude the application of §101.055.
56. Whether §101.055(2) applies is typically a fact question, not appropriate for
dismissal at the pleading stage. Because Plaintiff contests both the existence of an
emergency and the lawfulness of Chief Roy’s conduct, this defense is premature and cannot
support dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6).
iv. Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §101.055(3) is inapplicable.
57. Plaintiff’s claims do not arise from policy decisions concerning “fire protection.”
Texas courts interpret the exception in Tex. Civ. Prac. & Rem. Code §101.055(3) narrowly,
applying it only to core discretionary decisions about whether, when, or how to provide
police or fire services, not operational negligence or affirmative misconduct. In State v.
Terrell, 588 S.W.2d 784 (Tex. 1979), the Texas Supreme Court concluded that the
legislature’s purpose in enacting this section was to avoid judicial review of the policy
decisions governmental units must make in deciding how much, if any, police or fire
protection to provide for a community. Id. at 788. The language of this section was thus
interpreted as requiring that if the negligence alleged relates to the formulation of policy,
the governmental unit remains immune from liability. Id. If, however, the negligence is in
the manner in which policy is carried out, liability may exist under the TTCA. Id. See also
Jenicke v. City of Forest Hill, 873 S.W.2d 776, 779 (Tex. App.—Fort Worth 1994)
(“allegations of negligence [which] arise from carrying out the normal function of
PLAINTIFF’S RESPONSE TO DEFENDANTS’ MOTION TO DISMISS PLAINTIFF’S CLAIMS PAGE 26
protecting a community from fires . . . may form the basis of liability for a governmental
unit, where no emergency situation is alleged.”).
58. In this case, Plaintiff does not challenge the City’s decision to respond to a fire, how
many personnel to send, or the allocation of emergency resources. Plaintiff challenges
Chief Roy’s use of a vehicle to intentionally, recklessly, or negligently strike a pedestrian
— a claim based on operational conduct, not policy.
v. Plaintiff has alleged facts plausibly showing a claim of negligence and gross
negligence.
59. Plaintiff has pled a viable negligence claim. The Complaint alleges that Chief Roy,
acting in the course and scope of his employment, failed to exercise reasonable care when
operating the GFD truck, proximately causing Plaintiff’s injuries. This satisfies the
elements of a TTCA negligence claim under Texas law: duty, breach, causation, and
damages. See W. Invs., Inc. v. Urena, 162 S.W.3d 547, 550 (Tex. 2005). Plaintiff has
furthered alleged such conditions that would constitute gross negligence or recklessness
under the circumstances. (Doc. 1, ¶¶49-53).

60. Plaintiff is entitled to punitive damages from Chief Roy under his §1983 claim. See
Smith v. Wade, 461 U.S. 30 (1983).
61. Plaintiff does not seek punitive or exemplary damages against the City in this case.
