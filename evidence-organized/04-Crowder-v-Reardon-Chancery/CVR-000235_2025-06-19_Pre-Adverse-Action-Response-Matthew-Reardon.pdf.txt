IV. TITLE VII DISPARATE IMPACT AND EMPLOYMENT DISCRIMINATION
A. Systematic Discriminatory Impact of Criminal Background Screening
DoorDash's blanket application of criminal background exclusions creates disparate impact on protected
classes under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964. The Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's
Enforcement Guidance on the Consideration of Arrest and Conviction Records in Employment Decisions
(2012) establishes clear standards for lawful consideration of criminal records in employment.
Statistical Foundation for Disparate Impact: Criminal background screening policies disproportionately
exclude African American and Hispanic applicants at significantly higher rates than similarly situated white
applicants. This statistical disparity creates prima facie evidence of unlawful employment discrimination
under federal civil rights law.
EEOC Green Factors Analysis: Under established EEOC guidance, employers must conduct individualized
assessments considering:
1. The nature and gravity of the offense or conduct
2. The time that has passed since the offense or conduct and completion of sentence
3. The nature of the job held or sought
B. Business Necessity Defense Failure
DoorDash's systematic application of criminal exclusions without individualized assessment fails to meet
the business necessity standard required under Title VII. Green v. Missouri Pacific Railroad, 523 F.2d 1290
(8th Cir. 1975), established that blanket exclusions based on criminal records violate federal employment
discrimination law absent specific job-related justification.
Supporting Federal Authority:
EEOC v. Sheetz, Inc. (2024): Recent enforcement action challenging systematic criminal background
exclusions
Griggs v. Duke Power Co., 401 U.S. 424 (1971): Foundational disparate impact doctrine under Title VII
42 U.S.C. § 1981: Federal civil rights protections in contractual relationships
