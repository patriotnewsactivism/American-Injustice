## I. INTRODUCTION AND SUMMARY OF ARGUMENT
Access to justice cannot depend on the size of one's bank account. This fundamental principle,
woven throughout our constitutional jurisprudence, is violated daily by the application of 28
U.S.C. § 1915(e)(2)'s screening procedures to indigent civil rights plaintiffs. Plaintiff Matthew
Oliver Reardon respectfully submits this Memorandum of Law in support of his Motion
challenging the constitutionality of these procedures as applied.
The question presented is whether federal courts may constitutionally impose a mandatory pre-
service screening procedure exclusively on indigent plaintiffs—a procedure that subjects their
complaints to secretive, ex parte judicial review before service, while allowing fee-paying
plaintiffs to proceed directly to adversarial proceedings. This bifurcated system of justice
violates three distinct constitutional guarantees:
First, § 1915(e)(2) screening violates the First Amendment right to petition for redress of
grievances. By erecting procedural barriers to court access based solely on economic status, the
screening regime impermissibly burdens a fundamental right in a manner that
disproportionately affects those most in need of constitutional protection.
Second, the screening process violates procedural due process under the Fifth Amendment.
Indigent plaintiffs receive no notice that dismissal is being contemplated, no opportunity to
respond to perceived deficiencies, and no chance to participate in an adversarial testing of their
claims—procedural protections routinely afforded to fee-paying litigants.
Third, the current application of § 1915(e)(2) violates the equal protection component of the
Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. By creating two separate and unequal tracks of justice
based solely on wealth status, the screening procedure imposes an unconstitutional
classification that cannot withstand heightened scrutiny.
These constitutional violations are not merely theoretical. In this case, the Court's pre-service
screening stay has prevented Plaintiff from supplementing the record with evidence of ongoing
retaliation by government officials directly connected to his original claims. While his case
remains in procedural limbo, the constitutional violations alleged in his complaint persist and
compound, with no judicial recourse available.
As Justice William O. Douglas observed in *Griffin v. Illinois*, 351 U.S. 12, 19 (1956), "There can
be no equal justice where the kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of money he
has." Six decades later, this essential principle remains unfulfilled in the federal courts'
application of § 1915(e)(2) screening procedures. The time has come to remedy this
constitutional infirmity.

## III. STATUTORY FRAMEWORK AND LEGAL CONTEXT
The in forma pauperis statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1915, permits indigent litigants to proceed without
prepayment of filing fees. Section 1915(e)(2) provides:
> "Notwithstanding any filing fee, or any portion thereof, that may have been paid, the court
shall dismiss the case at any time if the court determines that—
>
> (A) the allegation of poverty is untrue; or
>
> (B) the action or appeal—
>
> (i) is frivolous or malicious;
>
> (ii) fails to state a claim on which relief may be granted; or
>
> (iii) seeks monetary relief against a defendant who is immune from such relief."
The current version of § 1915(e)(2) stems from the Prison Litigation Reform Act of 1996
("PLRA"), Pub. L. No. 104-134, 110 Stat. 1321. While the PLRA's legislative history focused
primarily on prisoner litigation, courts have expanded its application to all indigent civil litigants.
See *Lopez v. Smith*, 203 F.3d 1122, 1126 n.7 (9th Cir. 2000) (noting that § 1915(e)(2) "applies
to all in forma pauperis complaints").
Prior to the PLRA, § 1915 authorized dismissal only if an action was "frivolous or malicious." The
amended version adds two grounds for dismissal that mirror the language of Federal Rule of
Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) ("fails to state a claim") and the doctrine of immunity. However, unlike
Rule 12(b)(6) motions, which occur after service and within an adversarial context, § 1915(e)(2)
screening happens ex parte, before service, and without input from the plaintiff.
This evolution has created a two-tiered system of justice:
1. Fee-paying plaintiffs file complaints, which are promptly served on defendants. Defendants
may then move to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), after which plaintiffs have an opportunity to
respond. The court decides the motion based on adversarial briefing.
2. Indigent plaintiffs file complaints, which are subjected to pre-service judicial screening. The
court may dismiss sua sponte, without notice to the plaintiff that dismissal is being
contemplated, without allowing the plaintiff to respond to perceived deficiencies, and without
the truth-testing function of adversarial proceedings.
This bifurcated procedural framework forms the basis of Plaintiff's constitutional challenge.
## IV. STANDARD OF REVIEW
When a statute is challenged as unconstitutional as applied, courts must determine: (1) the
nature of the right at issue; (2) the appropriate level of scrutiny; and (3) whether the challenged
application survives that scrutiny. See *Hooper v. Bernalillo County Assessor*, 472 U.S. 612,
618-23 (1985).
The appropriate level of scrutiny depends on the rights affected. Classifications that burden
fundamental rights or create suspect classifications trigger heightened scrutiny. *Clark v. Jeter*,
486 U.S. 456, 461 (1988). Under strict scrutiny, the government must demonstrate that the
challenged practice is narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest.
*Washington v. Glucksberg*, 521 U.S. 702, 721 (1997).
Here, heightened scrutiny is appropriate for three independent reasons:
1. The right of access to courts is a fundamental right protected by the First Amendment's
Petition Clause. *California Motor Transport Co. v. Trucking Unlimited*, 404 U.S. 508, 510
(1972).
2. Due process requires fundamental fairness in judicial proceedings, especially when
constitutional claims are at stake. *Logan v. Zimmerman Brush Co.*, 455 U.S. 422, 429-30
(1982).
3. Wealth-based classifications that affect fundamental rights are subject to heightened scrutiny
under equal protection principles. *M.L.B. v. S.L.J.*, 519 U.S. 102, 120-24 (1996).
The Supreme Court has emphasized that "the right to sue and defend in the courts is the
alternative of force. In an organized society it is the right conservative of all other rights, and lies
at the foundation of orderly government." *Chambers v. Baltimore & O.R. Co.*, 207 U.S. 142,
148 (1907).
## V. ARGUMENT
### A. THE § 1915(e)(2) SCREENING PROCEDURE VIOLATES THE FIRST AMENDMENT RIGHT TO
PETITION
#### 1. Access to Courts Is a Core Component of the Petition Clause
The First Amendment guarantees "the right of the people... to petition the Government for a
redress of grievances." U.S. Const. amend. I. This right is "among the most precious of the
liberties safeguarded by the Bill of Rights." *United Mine Workers of America, Dist. 12 v. Illinois
State Bar Ass'n*, 389 U.S. 217, 222 (1967).
The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that the Petition Clause protects access to
courts:
> "The right of access to the courts is indeed but one aspect of the right of petition."
>
> — *California Motor Transport Co. v. Trucking Unlimited*, 404 U.S. 508, 510 (1972).
> "[T]he right to petition extends to all departments of the Government," including courts.
>
> — *Borough of Duryea v. Guarnieri*, 564 U.S. 379, 387 (2011).
> "[T]he right of access to the courts is an aspect of the First Amendment right to petition the
Government for redress of grievances."
>
> — *Bill Johnson's Restaurants, Inc. v. NLRB*, 461 U.S. 731, 741 (1983).
The Tenth Circuit has likewise recognized that "the right of access to the courts is a fundamental
right protected by the Constitution." *Nordgren v. Milliken*, 762 F.2d 851, 853 (10th Cir. 1985).
This right "assures that no person will be denied the opportunity to present to the judiciary
allegations concerning violations of fundamental constitutional rights." *Wolff v. McDonnell*,
418 U.S. 539, 579 (1974).
#### 2. The Current Screening Regime Impermissibly Burdens the Right to Petition
The application of § 1915(e)(2) screening procedures exclusively to indigent plaintiffs imposes a
significant burden on their First Amendment right to petition the courts. This burden takes
several forms:
First, the screening process imposes procedural obstacles based solely on economic status. As
the Supreme Court emphasized in *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. 415, 438 (1963), "First
Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive" and cannot be curtailed based on
arbitrary classifications.
Second, the process creates delay that is not experienced by fee-paying litigants. Courts have
recognized that "undue delay in processing claims can constitute a denial of due process."
*Wright v. Cuyler*, 624 F.2d 455, 457 (3d Cir. 1980). This delay is particularly problematic in
cases like Plaintiff's, where ongoing constitutional violations continue while pre-service
screening is pending.
Third, the screening regime has a chilling effect on the exercise of First Amendment rights. The
Supreme Court has long recognized that procedures that chill the exercise of First Amendment
rights require particular scrutiny. *Dombrowski v. Pfister*, 380 U.S. 479, 486-87 (1965). By
subjecting indigent plaintiffs to heightened barriers to court access, the § 1915(e)(2) procedure
deters individuals from seeking redress for constitutional violations—precisely the type of
chilling effect the First Amendment prohibits.
As Justice Brennan observed in *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at 433:
> "The threat of sanctions may deter almost as potently as the actual application of sanctions...
Because First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive, government may regulate
in the area only with narrow specificity."
The § 1915(e)(2) screening process lacks this "narrow specificity" and instead imposes a broad
barrier to court access based solely on economic status.
#### 3. The Petition Right Has Special Force When Vindicating Constitutional Violations
The right to petition takes on special significance when constitutional violations are alleged. The
Supreme Court has emphasized that litigation aimed at vindicating constitutional rights "is thus
a form of political expression" deserving heightened protection. *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at
429.
> "Groups which find themselves unable to achieve their objectives through the ballot
frequently turn to the courts... And under the conditions of modern government, litigation may
well be the sole practicable avenue open to a minority to petition for redress of grievances."
>
> — *NAACP v. Button*, 371 U.S. at 429-30.
This principle is particularly relevant to civil rights plaintiffs like Mr. Reardon, who seek judicial
redress for alleged constitutional violations by government officials. As the Supreme Court
noted in *Chambers*, 207 U.S. at 148, access to courts "is the right conservative of all other
rights"—that is, it preserves and protects all other rights.
By imposing additional procedural burdens exclusively on indigent civil rights plaintiffs, the §
1915(e)(2) screening process creates precisely the kind of barrier to petition rights that the First
Amendment forbids. This barrier is particularly unjustifiable when, as here, the plaintiff alleges
ongoing constitutional violations by government officials.
#### 4. The Ongoing Harm to Plaintiff's Petition Rights
The application of § 1915(e)(2) screening to Plaintiff's case vividly illustrates the First
Amendment harm. On March 31, 2025—the same day this Court imposed its pre-service
screening stay—Plaintiff experienced retaliation from federal officials when attempting to report
misconduct. Due to the stay, Plaintiff has been unable to document this retaliation or
supplement the record with evidence directly relevant to his claims.
This creates a catch-22: Plaintiff cannot petition the Court regarding ongoing constitutional
violations because the very mechanism designed to screen his initial petition prevents him from
doing so. As the Supreme Court observed in *Christopher v. Harbury*, 536 U.S. 403, 413 (2002),
the right of access to courts may be violated when official action "presently denies an
opportunity to litigate" constitutional claims.
The ongoing nature of the violations makes the First Amendment harm particularly acute. Each
day that passes without service of process allows the alleged constitutional violations to
continue unchecked, with no judicial oversight or opportunity for relief. This precisely
contravenes the purpose of the Petition Clause, which is to ensure that citizens have meaningful
access to government institutions for the redress of grievances.
### B. THE § 1915(e)(2) SCREENING PROCEDURE VIOLATES PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS
#### 1. Access to Courts Is Protected by Due Process
The Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be "deprived of life, liberty, or property,
without due process of law." U.S. Const. amend. V. This guarantee includes procedural due
process—the right to fair procedures before a deprivation occurs.
The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that access to courts is protected by due
process:
> "The due process clause requires that every man shall have the protection of his day in court."
>
> — *Truax v. Corrigan*, 257 U.S. 312, 332 (1921).
> "[D]ue process of law signifies a right to be heard in one's defense."
>
> — *Hovey v. Elliott*, 167 U.S. 409, 417 (1897).
In *Boddie v. Connecticut*, 401 U.S. 371, 377 (1971), the Court emphasized that "due process
requires, at a minimum, that absent a countervailing state interest of overriding significance,
persons forced to settle their claims of right and duty through the judicial process must be given
a meaningful opportunity to be heard."
This "meaningful opportunity to be heard" is precisely what the § 1915(e)(2) screening process
denies to indigent plaintiffs.
#### 2. The Current Screening Process Fails to Provide Adequate Procedural Safeguards
The essence of procedural due process is notice and an opportunity to be heard "at a
meaningful time and in a meaningful manner." *Armstrong v. Manzo*, 380 U.S. 545, 552 (1965).
Yet the § 1915(e)(2) screening process systematically denies these basic protections to indigent
plaintiffs.
First, indigent plaintiffs receive no notice that dismissal is being contemplated. Unlike fee-paying
plaintiffs facing a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, indigent plaintiffs are not informed of the specific
deficiencies the court perceives in their complaints before dismissal occurs.
Second, indigent plaintiffs are denied any opportunity to respond to perceived deficiencies. In
the normal Rule 12(b)(6) context, plaintiffs have an opportunity to file a response brief, present
counter-arguments, and address any misunderstandings. The § 1915(e)(2) process eliminates
this crucial safeguard.
Third, the screening process lacks the truth-testing function of adversarial proceedings. As
Justice Frankfurter observed in *Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath*, 341 U.S. 123,
170 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring):
> "Fairness can rarely be obtained by secret, one-sided determination of facts decisive of
rights... [N]o better instrument has been devised for arriving at truth than to give a person in
jeopardy of serious loss notice of the case against him and opportunity to meet it."
The § 1915(e)(2) screening process contradicts these fundamental principles, substituting
secret, one-sided determinations for the adversarial testing that is the hallmark of our judicial
system.
#### 3. Application of the Mathews v. Eldridge Balancing Test
In *Mathews v. Eldridge*, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976), the Supreme Court established a three-part
test for procedural due process claims:
1. The private interest affected by the official action;
2. The risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used, and the probable value of
additional procedural safeguards; and
3. The Government's interest, including the function involved and fiscal and administrative
burdens.
Aaaŵ
Applied to the § 1915(e)(2) screening process, this test reveals a clear due process violation:
First, the private interest at stake is substantial—access to courts to vindicate constitutional
rights. The Supreme Court has recognized this as "the right conservative of all other rights."
Chambers, 207 U.S. at 148. When constitutional claims are at issue, as in Plaintiff's case, this
interest takes on heightened importance. See Bounds v. Smith, 430 U.S. 817, 828 (1977)
(emphasizing the fundamental nature of court access, particularly for constitutional claims).
Second, the risk of erroneous deprivation is severe. One-sided, non-adversarial review increases
the likelihood of error, as the judge lacks the benefit of the plaintiff's response to perceived
deficiencies. Moreover, the absence of notice and opportunity to be heard means that
misunderstandings cannot be corrected and ambiguities cannot be clarified. These risks are
particularly acute for pro se litigants, who may lack legal training to anticipate all potential
objections to their pleadings. See Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519, 520 (1972) (recognizing that
pro se complaints must be held "to less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted by
lawyers").
The value of additional safeguards—specifically, notice and an opportunity to respond before
dismissal—is substantial. These basic procedures would significantly reduce the risk of
erroneous dismissal without imposing undue burdens on the judicial system.
Third, the government interest does not justify the current procedure. While courts have an
interest in dismissing frivolous claims, this interest can be served through the standard Rule
12(b)(6) process that applies to fee-paying litigants. The marginal benefit of pre-service
screening does not outweigh the substantial risk of erroneous deprivation, particularly for
constitutional claims.
As the Supreme Court emphasized in Boddie, 401 U.S. at 380, "a State must afford to all
individuals a meaningful opportunity to be heard if it is to fulfill the promise of the Due Process
Clause." The § 1915(e)(2) screening process fails to provide this "meaningful opportunity" to
indigent plaintiffs.
4. The Procedural Due Process Deprivation in Plaintiff's Case
The procedural due process violation is clearly illustrated in Plaintiff's case. When this Court
stayed proceedings on March 31, 2025, pending pre-service screening, it effectively prevented
Plaintiff from submitting evidence of subsequent retaliation directly related to his original
claims. Plaintiff has had no opportunity to explain why this new evidence is relevant, no notice
of any specific deficiencies the Court may perceive in his complaint, and no chance to address
those concerns before a dismissal decision.
This absence of basic procedural protections starkly contrasts with the treatment afforded to
fee-paying litigants, who would face no such barriers to supplementing the record or responding
to challenges to their complaints. The § 1915(e)(2) screening process thus creates a procedural
trap for indigent plaintiffs that violates fundamental due process principles.
C. THE § 1915(e)(2) SCREENING PROCEDURE VIOLATES EQUAL PROTECTION
1. Wealth-Based Classifications Affecting Fundamental Rights Trigger Heightened Scrutiny
Although the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment explicitly applies only to
the states, the Supreme Court has recognized that the Due Process Clause of the Fifth
Amendment contains an equal protection component that applies to federal action. Bolling v.
Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954).
The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that wealth-based classifications affecting fundamental
rights trigger heightened scrutiny:
"The Equal Protection Clause limits the distinction that a State may make in the application of
laws to different persons subject to its jurisdiction. When a statutory classification significantly
interferes with a fundamental right or operates to the peculiar disadvantage of a suspect class,
the classification must be justified by a compelling state interest."
Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312 (1976) (internal citation
omitted).
In Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 19 (1956), the Court established the principle that governments
may not condition access to courts on ability to pay: “There can be no equal justice where the
kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of money he has. Destitute defendants must be
afforded as adequate appellate review as defendants who have money enough to buy
transcripts."
The Court has since extended this principle to various contexts involving court access. See, e.g.,
Burns v. Ohio, 360 U.S. 252, 258 (1959) (invalidating filing fee for appeals by indigents); Smith v.
Bennett, 365 U.S. 708, 714 (1961) (invalidating filing fee for habeas corpus petitions by
indigents); Mayer v. City of Chicago, 404 U.S. 189, 196-97 (1971) (extending Griffin to non-felony
appeals).
In M.L.B. v. S.L.J., 519 U.S. 102, 120-24 (1996), the Court explicitly held that wealth-based
classifications affecting court access are subject to heightened scrutiny: "The equal protection
concern relates to the legitimacy of fencing out would-be appellants based solely on their
inability to pay core costs... [This] classification, which turns on the ability to pay, [is] examined
with heightened scrutiny."
2. The § 1915(e)(2) Procedure Creates an Impermissible Two-Tiered System of Justice
The application of § 1915(e)(2) screening procedures exclusively to indigent plaintiffs creates
precisely the type of two-tiered system of justice that equal protection principles forbid:
1. Fee-paying plaintiffs have their complaints served immediately and face dismissal only after
adversarial proceedings under Rule 12(b)(6).
2. Indigent plaintiffs face pre-service screening without notice, opportunity to respond, or
adversarial testing.
This classification is based solely on wealth status—the very type of classification that triggers
heightened scrutiny when it affects fundamental rights like court access. See M.L.B., 519 U.S. at
120-24.
As Justice Douglas observed in Williams v. Illinois, 399 U.S. 235, 242 (1970), "the Equal
Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that the statutory ceiling placed on
imprisonment for any substantive offense be the same for all defendants irrespective of their
economic status." Similarly, the procedural "ceiling" placed on access to courts must be the
same for all plaintiffs irrespective of their economic status.
3. No Compelling Interest Justifies the Discriminatory Treatment
Under heightened scrutiny, a challenged classification must be narrowly tailored to serve a
compelling governmental interest. Washington v. Glucksberg, 521 U.S. 702, 721 (1997).
The § 1915(e)(2) screening procedure fails this test for several reasons:
First, any legitimate interest in filtering frivolous claims can be served through the ordinary
adversarial process under Rule 12—the same process used for fee-paying litigants. Courts
regularly dismiss meritless claims through Rule 12(b)(6) motions, without need for special pre-
service screening of indigent complaints.
Second, the current screening procedure is not narrowly tailored, as it subjects all indigent
complaints to pre-service review regardless of the nature or merit of the claims. This broad-
brush approach sweeps in meritorious constitutional claims alongside potentially frivolous ones,
creating an impermissible burden on fundamental rights.
Third, less restrictive alternatives exist, such as:
- Applying the same post-service review standards to all litigants regardless of economic status
- Providing notice and an opportunity to respond before dismissal
- Limiting pre-service screening to specific categories of clearly frivolous claims
In Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353, 357 (1963), the Court emphasized that "[t]here is lacking
that equality demanded by the Fourteenth Amendment where the rich man... enjoys the
benefit of counsel's examination into the record, research of the law, and marshalling of
arguments on his behalf, while the indigent... is forced to shift for himself." Similarly, there is
lacking that equality demanded by the Fifth Amendment where the fee-paying plaintiff enjoys
the benefits of adversarial proceedings, while the indigent plaintiff is subjected to secretive, ex
parte screening.
4. The Equal Protection Violation in Plaintiff's Case
The equal protection violation is clearly illustrated in Plaintiff's case. Due to his indigent status,
Plaintiff's complaint has been subjected to pre-service screening that has effectively prevented
him from supplementing the record with evidence of continuing constitutional violations. A fee-
paying plaintiff facing identical circumstances would have had his complaint served immediately,
with no pre-service judicial review or stay of proceedings.
This disparate treatment serves no compelling governmental interest and cannot be justified
under the heightened scrutiny that applies to wealth-based classifications affecting
fundamental rights. As the Supreme Court emphasized in San Antonio Independent School
District v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1, 20 (1973), the judicial system must be particularly vigilant
when classifications based on wealth "tend to be drawn along lines of political power."
D. HEIGHTENED SCRUTINY MUST APPLY TO THIS CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE
1. Multiple Constitutional Rights Are Implicated
The constitutional challenge to § 1915(e)(2) screening implicates multiple fundamental rights,
each of which independently triggers heightened scrutiny:
First, access to courts is protected as a fundamental right under the First Amendment's Petition
Clause. California Motor Transport, 404 U.S. at 510. The Supreme Court has consistently applied
strict scrutiny to burdens on First Amendment rights. Button, 371 U.S. at 438 ("The decisions of
this Court have consistently held that only a compelling state interest... can justify limiting First
Amendment freedoms.").
Second, court access is protected by due process, particularly when constitutional claims are at
stake. Boddie, 401 U.S. at 377. The Court has recognized that procedures affecting this right
must meet heightened standards of fairness. *Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm.*, 341 U.S. at
170 (Frankfurter, J., concurring).
Third, wealth-based classifications affecting fundamental rights trigger heightened equal
protection scrutiny. M.L.B., 519 U.S. at 120-24.
The convergence of these multiple constitutional protections "calls for more exacting judicial
scrutiny." Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872, 881 (1990) (discussing the heightened
scrutiny that applies when multiple constitutional rights are implicated).
2. Wealth-Based Classifications Affecting Court Access Are Inherently Suspect
The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that wealth-based classifications affecting
access to courts are constitutionally suspect. As Justice Harlan observed, even while dissenting
in Griffin, there is a "constitutional rule" that "a man's mere property status, without more,
cannot be used by a state to test his rights to equal treatment in the face of the law." Griffin,
351 U.S. at 35 (Harlan, J., dissenting).
This principle has deep roots in our constitutional tradition. In Chambers, 207 U.S. at 148, the
Court emphasized that "the right to sue and defend in the courts... lies at the foundation of
orderly government." When this foundational right is burdened based solely on economic
status, the most exacting judicial scrutiny is required.
The Court has specifically held that "lines drawn on the basis of wealth or property, like those of
race, are traditionally disfavored." Harper v. Virginia State Bd. of Elections, 383 U.S. 663, 668
(1966). The § 1915(e)(2) screening procedure draws precisely such a line, subjecting indigent
plaintiffs to additional procedural hurdles solely because of their economic status.
3. The § 1915(e)(2) Procedure Fails Heightened Scrutiny
Under heightened scrutiny, the government must demonstrate that the challenged practice is
narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest. Washington v. Glucksberg, 521
U.S. 702, 721 (1997).
The § 1915(e)(2) screening process fails this test for several reasons:
First, the government cannot demonstrate a compelling interest in treating indigent plaintiffs
differently from fee-paying plaintiffs at the pleading stage. Any legitimate interest in filtering
frivolous claims applies equally to all plaintiffs, regardless of economic status.
Second, the current screening procedure is not narrowly tailored, as it imposes a categorical
disadvantage on all indigent plaintiffs without regard to the nature or merit of their claims. This
"blunderbuss approach" is antithetical to the narrow tailoring required by heightened scrutiny.
See Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491 U.S. 781, 800 (1989).
Third, less restrictive alternatives exist that would serve any legitimate governmental interest
without imposing differential burdens based on wealth status. These include applying the same
Rule 12(b)(6) standard to all plaintiffs after service, with the same procedural protections for all.
As the Court emphasized in Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509, 533 (2004), "ordinary
considerations of cost and convenience alone cannot justify a State's failure to provide
individuals with a meaningful right of access to the courts." Similarly, considerations of judicial
efficiency alone cannot justify the federal courts' failure to provide indigent plaintiffs with the
same procedural protections afforded to fee-paying litigants.
E. THE APPROPRIATE REMEDY IS IMMEDIATE ISSUANCE OF SUMMONS
1. Declaratory Relief Is Appropriate for Constitutional Violations
Declaratory relief is particularly appropriate when challenging the constitutionality of
governmental practices. The Declaratory Judgment Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2201, specifically empowers
federal courts to "declare the rights and other legal relations of any interested party seeking
such declaration, whether or not further relief is or could be sought."
As the Supreme Court noted in Green v. Mansour, 474 U.S. 64, 74 (1985), declaratory relief is
designed to "clarify[] the legal relationship between the parties." Here, a declaration that the §
1915(e)(2) screening procedure is unconstitutional as applied would clarify that Plaintiff is
entitled to the same procedural protections as fee-paying litigants.
2. Immediate Issuance of Summons Is Necessary to Prevent Ongoing Harm
Beyond declaratory relief, practical considerations weigh heavily in favor of immediately issuing
summons and lifting the stay in this case.
First, the alleged constitutional violations are ongoing. Each day that passes without service of
process allows the alleged retaliation by government officials to continue unchecked. As the
Supreme Court emphasized in Elrod v. Burns, 427 U.S. 347, 373 (1976), "[t]he loss of First
Amendment freedoms, for even minimal periods of time, unquestionably constitutes
irreparable injury."
Second, the procedural catch-22 created by the pre-service screening stay prevents Plaintiff
from documenting continuing violations directly related to his original claims. This compounds
the constitutional harm and frustrates the truth-seeking function of the judicial process.
Third, Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 4(b) provides that "[o]n or after filing the complaint, the
plaintiff may present a summons to the clerk for signature and seal." The rule contains no
exception for IFP plaintiffs. By directing the clerk to issue summons, the Court would simply be
applying the same procedural rules to Plaintiff that apply to all other civil litigants.
3. This Court Has Authority to Remedy Constitutional Violations
This Court has both the authority and the duty to remedy constitutional violations in its
procedures. As the Supreme Court emphasized in *Marbury v. Madison*, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137,
180 (1803), "a law repugnant to the constitution is void, and... courts, as well as other
departments, are bound by that instrument."
The unconstitutional application of § 1915(e)(2) is precisely the type of constitutional violation
that courts must remedy. As Chief Justice Marshall wrote in *Marbury*, 5 U.S. at 163, "The very
essence of civil liberty certainly consists in the right of every individual to claim the protection
of the laws, whenever he receives an injury." This foundational principle remains as vital today
as it was at the dawn of our Republic.
In Jones v. Bock, 549 U.S. 199, 214 (2007), the Supreme Court emphasized that when the PLRA
does not "explicitly or implicitly" include special procedural requirements, courts should "follow
the usual procedural practice." Similarly, this Court should follow the usual procedural practice
of issuing summons and allowing the case to proceed under the standard Rules of Civil
Procedure.
VI. CONCLUSION
The application of § 1915(e)(2) screening procedures to indigent civil rights plaintiffs creates an
unconstitutional two-tiered system of justice that violates the First Amendment right to
petition, the Fifth Amendment guarantee of due process, and the equal protection component
of the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause. This bifurcated procedural framework imposes
significant burdens on fundamental rights based solely on economic status—burdens that
cannot withstand the heightened scrutiny that applies when wealth-based classifications affect
court access.
Plaintiff's case vividly illustrates the constitutional harm inflicted by the current screening
regime. While his complaint remains in pre-service limbo, ongoing retaliation by government
officials continues unchecked, with no judicial recourse available. This not only denies Plaintiff
his constitutional right to petition for redress but also undermines the judicial system's truth-
seeking function by preventing the timely documentation of relevant evidence.
As Justice Brennan observed, "Meaningful access to the courts is a fundamental right of
citizenship in our democracy." *Lewis v. Casey*, 518 U.S. 343, 391 (1996) (Brennan, J.,
concurring). This access must be available equally to all citizens, regardless of economic status.
"There can be no equal justice," Justice Black wrote, "where the kind of trial a man gets
depends on the amount of money he has." *Griffin*, 351 U.S. at 19.
The time has come to remedy the constitutional infirmity in the application of § 1915(e)(2)
screening procedures. Plaintiff respectfully asks this Court to:
1. Declare that the application of § 1915(e)(2) screening procedures, as currently practiced,
violates the First and Fifth Amendments as applied to indigent civil rights plaintiffs;
2. Order the immediate issuance of summons to all defendants named in Plaintiff's First
Amended Complaint;
3. Lift the stay to allow filing of Plaintiff's Second Sworn Declaration documenting retaliatory
conduct by federal officials; and
4. Allow this case to proceed according to the standard Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, with
the same procedural protections afforded to all civil litigants.
