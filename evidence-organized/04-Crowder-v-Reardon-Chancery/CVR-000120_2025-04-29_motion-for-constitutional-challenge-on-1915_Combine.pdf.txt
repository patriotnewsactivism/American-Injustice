LEGAL BACKGROUND
28 U.S.C. § 1915 permits indigent parties to proceed in forma pauperis, waiving
filing fees. Subsection (e)(2) authorizes courts to dismiss claims that are frivolous,
malicious, fail to state a claim, or seek monetary relief from immune defendants.
Courts have used this provision to impose mandatory pre-service "screening" of
indigent complaints.
The current version of § 1915(e)(2) derives from the Prison Litigation Reform Act
of 1996 ("PLRA"), Pub. L. No. 104-134, 110 Stat. 1321. While the PLRA's
legislative history focuses primarily on prisoner litigation, courts have expanded its
application to all indigent civil litigants, despite the absence of congressional
findings justifying such a broad application to non-prisoners. See Lister v. Dep't of
Treasury, 408 F.3d 1309, 1312 (10th Cir. 2005) (applying § 1915(e)(2) to non-
prisoner cases without addressing constitutional concerns).
This judicial practice has created an unconstitutional two-tiered system:
2
• Paying litigants face no pre-service judicial review absent a defendant's motion.
• Indigent litigants must survive secretive, unilateral review by the Court without
adversarial participation.
Unlike the Rule 12(b)(6) standard applied to fee-paying plaintiffs, indigent plaintiffs
face a heightened risk of dismissal through a non-adversarial process that lacks the
procedural safeguards guaranteed to those with financial means. This divergence
burdens indigent plaintiffs' constitutional rights in violation of well- settled law.
ARGUMENT
I. The 1915(e)(2) Screening Process Violates the First Amendment Right to Petition for Redress
The First Amendment guarantees not only the right to speak freely, but the right to
petition the government for redress of grievances—including through access to the
courts. The Supreme Court has consistently recognized this right as fundamental to
our constitutional structure: "The right of petition is one of the freedoms protected
by the Bill of Rights, and we cannot... close our eyes to the fact that the effective
exercise of this right is the most likely way to obtain a peaceful redress of
grievances."- NAACP v. Button, 371 U.S. 415, 429-31 (1963).
"The right of access to the courts is indeed but one aspect of the right of petition."-
California Motor Transport Co. v. Trucking Unlimited, 404 U.S. 508, 510 (1972).
"The First Amendment right to petition Government for redress of grievances
3
includes a right of access to the courts"- Borough of Duryea v. Guarnieri, 564 U.S.
379, 387 (2011).
Courts must be particularly vigilant when procedural barriers disproportionately
impact those seeking to vindicate constitutional rights. See NAACP v. Button, 371
U.S. at 438 ("[l]n the context of NAACP objectives, litigation is not a technique of
resolving private differences; it is a means for... obtaining the vindication of legal
rights."). When First Amendment rights are at stake, "the Court has emphasized that
standards of permissible statutory vagueness are strict." Button, 371 U.S. at 432.
The government may not impose burdens on the right to petition that are
discriminatory, chilling, or based on wealth status. Yet that is precisely what §
1915(e)(2) screening does. It imposes a second-class procedure on indigent litigants,
subjecting them to delays, stays, and dismissals before their grievances even reach
an adversarial process. This creates what the Supreme Court has termed "inequitable
treatment under the law." M.L.B. v. S.L.J., 519 U.S. 102, 120 (1996).
The harm is not merely theoretical. In this case, the screening process has prevented
Plaintiff from documenting ongoing retaliation by government officials directly
connected to his original claims. This demonstrates what the Supreme Court has
described as "the real risk of 'cutting off... access to events or information which we
would know if free to... inquire.'" Branzburg v. Hayes, 408 U.S. 665, 728 (1972)
4
(Stewart, J., dissenting).
By treating the right to access courts as conditional upon surviving an ex parte
screening based solely on economic status, the current practice infringes the First
Amendment right to petition for redress—a right that "extends to all departments of
the Government," including courts. California Motor Transport, 404 U.S. at 510.
II. The 1915(e)(2) Screening Regime Violates the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause
Procedural due process imposes constraints on governmental decisions that deprive
individuals of "liberty" or "property" interests. Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319,
332 (1976). The Supreme Court has consistently held that: "The fundamental
requisite of due process of law is the opportunity to be heard... at a meaningful time
and in a meaningful manner." - Goldberg v. Kelly, 397 U.S. 254, 267 (1970)
(citations omitted).
Before government action deprives an individual of a protected right—such as
access to the courts—the individual must receive notice and an opportunity to be
heard. Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306, 313 (1950).
The Court has specifically recognized that "access to the courts is a constitutionally
protected component of the due process right." Boddie v. Connecticut, 401 U.S. 371,
376 (1971).
The § 1915(e)(2) screening process violates these bedrock principles in multiple
5
ways:
• No Notice: Plaintiffs are not formally notified of the specific deficiencies being
considered before dismissal.
• No Opportunity to be Heard: Plaintiffs are denied a chance to amend, argue, or
correct alleged deficiencies before dismissal.
• No Adversarial Testing: The entire screening process occurs ex parte, without
the truth-testing function of adversarial presentation.
This contrasts sharply with the process afforded to fee-paying litigants. In all other
civil actions, dismissal for failure to state a claim occurs only after the defendant
moves under Rule 12(b)(6), the plaintiff has an opportunity to respond, and both
sides present their arguments. The Supreme Court has emphasized that "fairness
can rarely be obtained by secret, one-sided determination of facts decisive of
rights." Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm. v. McGrath, 341 U.S. 123, 170 (1951)
(Frankfurter, J., concurring).
In Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319 (1976), the Court established a three-part test
for procedural due process claims:
1. The private interest affected by the official action;
2. The risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used, and the probable
value of additional procedural safeguards; and
3. The Government's interest, including the function involved and fiscal and
6
administrative burdens.
Applied here:
1. The private interest is substantial—access to courts to vindicate constitutional
rights.
2. The risk of erroneous deprivation is high—non-adversarial, secretive screening
without notice or opportunity to respond.
3. The government interest is minimal—judicial resources are not significantly
conserved by pre-service screening when compared to standard Rule 12
practice.
As Justice Frankfurter observed, "The validity and moral authority of a conclusion
largely depend on the mode by which it was reached." Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee
Comm., 341 U.S. at 171 (Frankfurter, J., concurring). By subjecting indigent
plaintiffs to a procedurally deficient screening process, § 1915(e)(2) violates
fundamental due process principles.
III. The 1915(e)(2) Screening Violates Equal Protection Under the Fifth Amendment
Though the Equal Protection Clause explicitly applies to the States via the
Fourteenth Amendment, the Supreme Court has recognized an analogous equal
protection guarantee under the Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause that
constrains federal action. Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497 (1954).
"[T]he concepts of equal protection and due process, both stemming from our
7
American ideal of fairness, are not mutually exclusive."- Bolling, 347 U.S. at 499.
The Court has specifically recognized that discrimination based on wealth in the
context of court access triggers heightened scrutiny, not mere rational basis review:
"The equal protection concern relates to the legitimacy of fencing out would-be
appellants based solely on their inability to pay core costs... [This] classification,
which turns on the ability to pay, [is] examined with heightened scrutiny." -M.L.B.
v. S.L.J., 519 U.S. 102, 120-24 (1996).
In Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12 (1956), the Court established the principle that
states may not condition access to courts on ability to pay, declaring that "there can
be no equal justice where the kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of
money he has." Id. at 19.
This principle has been consistently reaffirmed: "The State cannot adopt procedures
which leave an indigent defendant entirely cut off from any appeal at all, by virtue
of his indigency." - Lane v. Brown, 372 U.S. 477, 481 (1963).
"Once the Government chooses to provide such protections [as court access], it may
not discriminate against the indigent in affording them." -Britt v. North Carolina,
404 U.S. 226, 227 (1971).
Under the § 1915(e)(2) screening regime:
8
• Indigent plaintiffs are segregated into a less favorable procedural system.
• The sole basis for differential treatment is poverty.
• No compelling (or even rational) justification exists for this discrimination.
This classification is particularly suspect because it both discriminates based on
wealth status and burdens a fundamental right—access to courts. The Supreme Court
has repeatedly held that such dual-nature classifications trigger the most stringent
judicial scrutiny. See Harper v. Virginia State Bd. of Elections, 383 U.S. 663, 670
(1966) ("[W]here fundamental rights and liberties are asserted under the Equal
Protection Clause, classifications which might invade or restrain them must be
closely scrutinized.").
The § 1915(e)(2) screening regime cannot survive such scrutiny. As Justice Douglas
observed, "The Equal Protection Clause requires that these appellate procedures be
administered with an even hand." Rinaldi v. Yeager, 384 U.S. 305, 309-10 (1966).
IV. Heightened Scrutiny Must Apply to These Constitutional Violations
The constitutional violations described above trigger heightened scrutiny for
multiple reasons:
1. Fundamental Right: Access to courts is a fundamental right. Tennessee v. Lane, 541
U.S. 509, 533-34 (2004) (describing "the right of access to the courts" as "a
9
fundamental right protected by the Constitution").
2. Suspect Classification: Wealth-based classifications that affect fundamental rights
are inherently suspect. Harper, 383 U.S. at 668 ("Lines drawn on the basis of wealth
or property, like those of race, are traditionally disfavored.").
3. First Amendment Rights: Burdens on First Amendment rights trigger strict scrutiny.
Button, 371 U.S. at 438 ("The decisions of this Court have consistently held that
only a compelling state interest... can justify limiting First Amendment freedoms.").
Under heightened scrutiny, the government must demonstrate that the challenged
practice is narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest. See
Washington v. Glucksberg, 521 U.S. 702, 721 (1997).
The § 1915(e)(2) screening process fails this test:
• Any legitimate interest in filtering frivolous claims can be served through the
ordinary adversarial process under Rule 12—the same process used for fee-paying
litigants.
• The current screening procedure is not narrowly tailored, as it subjects all indigent
complaints to pre service review regardless of the nature or merit of the claims.
• Less restrictive alternatives exist, such as applying the same post-service review
standards to all litigants regardless of economic status.
As the Supreme Court has stated, "The Constitution cannot confer the right to
10
proceed in forma pauperis and then make its exercise frustrating and futile." Lane,
372 U.S. at 484-85.

III. STATUTORY FRAMEWORK AND LEGAL CONTEXT
The in forma pauperis statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1915, permits indigent litigants to proceed
without prepayment of filing fees. Section 1915(e)(2) provides:
"Notwithstanding any filing fee, or any portion thereof, that may have
been paid, the court shall dismiss the case at any time if the court
determines that—
(A) the allegation of poverty is untrue; or
(B) the action or appeal—
(i) is frivolous or malicious;
(ii) fails to state a claim on which relief may be granted; or
(iii) seeks monetary relief against a defendant who is immune from such relief."
The current version of § 1915(e)(2) stems from the Prison Litigation Reform Act
of 1996 ("PLRA"), Pub. L. No. 104-134, 110 Stat. 1321. While the PLRA's
legislative history focused primarily on prisoner litigation, courts have expanded
its application to all indigent civil litigants. See Lopez v Smith, 203 F.3d 1122, 1126
(9th Cir. 2000) (noting that § 1915(e)(2) "applies to all in forma pauperis
complaints").
Prior to the PLRA, § 1915 authorized dismissal only if an action was "frivolous or
malicious." The amended version adds two grounds for dismissal that mirror the
language of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) ("fails to state a claim") and
the doctrine of immunity. However, unlike Rule 12(b)(6) motions, which occur
6
after service and within an adversarial context, § 1915(e)(2) screening happens ex
parte, before service, and without input from the plaintiff.
This evolution has created a two-tiered system of justice:
1. Fee-paying plaintiffs file complaints, which are promptly served on defendants.
Defendants may then move to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6), after which
plaintiffs have an opportunity to respond. The court decides the motion based
on adversarial briefing.
2. Indigent plaintiffs file complaints, which are subjected to pre-service judicial
screening. The court may dismiss sua sponte, without notice to the plaintiff that
dismissal is being contemplated, without allowing the plaintiff to respond to
perceived deficiencies, and without the truth-testing function of adversarial
proceedings.
This bifurcated procedural framework forms the basis of Plaintiff's constitutional challenge.
IV. STANDARD OF REVIEW
When a statute is challenged as unconstitutional as applied, courts must determine:
(1) the nature of the right at issue; (2) the appropriate level of scrutiny; and (3)
whether the challenged application survives that scrutiny. See Hooper v Bernalillo
County Assessor, 472 U.S. 612, 618-23 (1985).
The appropriate level of scrutiny depends on the rights affected. Classifications that
burden fundamental rights or create suspect classifications trigger heightened
scrutiny. Clark v Jeter, 486 U.S. 456,461 (1988). Under strict scrutiny, the
government must demonstrate that the challenged practice is narrowly tailored to
7
serve a compelling governmental interest. Washington v. Glucksberg, 521 U.S.
702, 721 (1997).

As Justice Brennan observed in NAACP v Button, 371 U.S. at 433: "The threat of
sanctions may deter almost as potently as the actual application of sanctions...
Because First Amendment freedoms need breathing space to survive, government

The § 1915(e)(2) screening process lacks this "narrow specificity" and instead
imposes a broad barrier to court access based solely on economic status.

B. THE § 1915(e)(2) SCREENING PROCEDURE VIOLATES
PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS
1. Access to Courts Is Protected by Due Process
The Fifth Amendment guarantees that no person shall be "deprived of life, liberty,
or property, without due process of law." U.S. Const, amend. V. This guarantee
includes procedural due process—the right to fair procedures before a deprivation
occurs.
The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that access to courts is protected by
due process:
"The due process clause requires that every man shall have the protection of his day
13
in court."- Truax v Corrigan, 257 U.S. 312, 332 (1921). "[D]ue process of law
signifies a right to be heard in one's defense."- Hovey v Elliott, 167 U.S. 409, 417
(1897). In Boddie v Connecticut, 401 U.S. 371, 377 (1971), the Court emphasized
that "due process requires, at a minimum, that absent a countervailing state interest
of overriding significance, persons forced to settle their claims of right and duty
through the judicial process must be given a meaningful opportunity to be heard."
This "meaningful opportunity to be heard" is precisely what the § 1915(e)(2)
screening process denies to indigent plaintiffs.
2. The Current Screening Process Fails to Provide Adequate Procedural Safeguards
The essence of procedural due process is notice and an opportunity to be heard "at
a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner." Armstrong v Manzo, 380 U.S. 545,
552 (1965). Yet the § 1915(e)(2) screening process systematically denies these
basic protections to indigent plaintiffs.
First, indigent plaintiffs receive no notice that dismissal is being contemplated.
Unlike fee-paying plaintiffs facing a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, indigent plaintiffs are
not informed of the specific deficiencies the court perceives in their complaints
before dismissal occurs.
Second, indigent plaintiffs are denied any opportunity to respond to perceived
deficiencies. In the normal Rule 12(b)(6) context, plaintiffs have an opportunity to
14
file a response brief, present counter-arguments, and address any
misunderstandings. The § 1915(e)(2) process eliminates this crucial safeguard.
Third, the screening process lacks the truth-testing function of adversarial
proceedings. As Justice Frankfurter observed in Joint Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm,
v McGrath, 341 U.S. 123, 170 (1951) (Frankfurter, J., concurring): "Fairness can
rarely be obtained by secret, one-sided determination of facts decisive of rights...
[N]o better instrument has been devised for arriving at truth than to give a person
in jeopardy of serious loss notice of the case against him and opportunity to meet
it."
The § 1915(e)(2) screening process contradicts these fundamental principles,
substituting secret, one sided determinations for the adversarial testing that is the
hallmark of our judicial system.
3. Application of the Mathews v. Eldridge Balancing Test
In Mathews v Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 335 (1976), the Supreme Court established
a three-part test for procedural due process claims:
1. The private interest affected by the official action;
2. The risk of erroneous deprivation through the procedures used, and the probable
value of additional procedural safeguards; and
3. The Government's interest, including the function involved and fiscal and
15
administrative burdens.
Applied to the § 1915(e)(2) screening process, this test reveals a clear due process
violation:
First, the private interest at stake is substantial—access to courts to vindicate
constitutional rights. The Supreme Court has recognized this as "the right
conservative of all other rights." Chambers, 207 U.S. at 148. When constitutional
claims are at issue, as in Plaintiff's case, this interest takes on heightened
importance. See Bounds v. Smith, 430 U.S. 817, 828 (1977) (emphasizing the
fundamental nature of court access, particularly for constitutional claims).
Second, the risk of erroneous deprivation is severe. One-sided, non-adversarial
review increases the likelihood of error, as the judge lacks the benefit of the
plaintiff's response to perceived deficiencies. Moreover, the absence of notice and
opportunity to be heard means that misunderstandings cannot be corrected and
ambiguities cannot be clarified. These risks are particularly acute for pro se
litigants, who may lack legal training to anticipate all potential objections to their
pleadings. See Haines v. Kerner, 404 U.S. 519, 520 (1972) (recognizing that pro se
complaints must be held "to less stringent standards than formal pleadings drafted
by lawyers"). The value of additional safeguards—specifically, notice and an
opportunity to respond before dismissal— is substantial. These basic procedures
16
would significantly reduce the risk of erroneous dismissal without imposing undue
burdens on the judicial system.
Third, the government interest does not justify the current procedure. While courts
have an interest in dismissing frivolous claims, this interest can be served through
the standard Rule 12(b)(6) process that applies to fee-paying litigants. The marginal
benefit of pre-service screening does not outweigh the substantial risk of erroneous
deprivation, particularly for constitutional claims.
As the Supreme Court emphasized in Boddie, 401 U.S. at 380, "a State must afford
to all individuals a meaningful opportunity to be heard if it is to fulfill the promise
of the Due Process Clause." The § 1915(e)(2) screening process fails to provide this
"meaningful opportunity" to indigent plaintiffs.
4. The Procedural Due Process Deprivation in Plaintiff's Case
The procedural due process violation is clearly illustrated in Plaintiff's case. When
this Court stayed proceedings on March 31, 2025, pending pre-service screening,
it effectively prevented Plaintiff from submitting evidence of subsequent retaliation
directly related to his original claims. Plaintiff has had no opportunity to explain
why this new evidence is relevant, no notice of any specific deficiencies the Court
may perceive in his complaint, and no chance to address those concerns before a
dismissal decision.
17
This absence of basic procedural protections starkly contrasts with the treatment
afforded to fee-paying litigants, who would face no such barriers to supplementing
the record or responding to challenges to their complaints. The § 1915(e)(2)
screening process thus creates a procedural trap for indigent plaintiffs that violates
fundamental due process principles.
C. THE § 1915(e)(2) SCREENING PROCEDURE VIOLATES EQUAL PROTECTION
1. Wealth-Based Classifications Affecting Fundamental Rights Trigger Heightened Scrutiny
Although the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment explicitly
applies only to the states, the Supreme Court has recognized that the Due Process
Clause of the Fifth Amendment contains an equal protection component that
applies to federal action. Bolling v. Sharpe, 347 U.S. 497, 499 (1954).
The Supreme Court has repeatedly held that wealth-based classifications affecting
fundamental rights trigger heightened scrutiny: "The Equal Protection Clause
limits the distinction that a State may make in the application of laws to different
persons subject to its jurisdiction. When a statutory classification significantly
interferes with a fundamental right or operates to the peculiar disadvantage of a
suspect class, the classification must be justified by a compelling state interest."-
Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia, 427 U.S. 307, 312 (1976).
In Griffin v. Illinois, 351 U.S. 12, 19 (1956), the Court established the principle
18
that governments may not condition access to courts on ability to pay: "There can
be no equal justice where the kind of trial a man gets depends on the amount of
money he has. Destitute defendants must be afforded as adequate appellate review
as defendants who have money enough to buy transcripts."
The Court has since extended this principle to various contexts involving court
access. See, e.g., Burns v. Ohio, 360 U.S. 252, 258 (1959) (invalidating filing fee
for appeals by indigents); Smith v. Bennett, 365 U.S. 708, 714 (1961) (invalidating
filing fee for habeas corpus petitions by indigents); Mayer v. City of Chicago, 404
U.S. 189, 196-97 (1971) (extending Griffin to non-felony appeals).
In M.L.B. v. S.L.J., 519 U.S. 102, 120-24 (1996), the Court explicitly held that
wealth-based classifications affecting court access are subject to heightened
scrutiny: "The equal protection concern relates to the legitimacy of fencing out
would-be appellants based solely on their inability to pay core costs... [This]
classification, which turns on the ability to pay, [is] examined with heightened
scrutiny."
2. The § 1915(e)(2) Procedure Creates an Impermissible Two-Tiered System of Justice
The application of § 1915(e)(2) screening procedures exclusively to indigent
plaintiffs creates precisely the type of two-tiered system of justice that equal
protection principles forbid:
19
1. Fee-paying plaintiffs have their complaints served immediately and face
dismissal only after adversarial proceedings under Rule 12(b)(6).
2. Indigent plaintiffs face pre-service screening without notice, opportunity to
respond, or adversarial testing.
This classification is based solely on wealth status—the very type of classification
that triggers heightened scrutiny when it affects fundamental rights like court
access. See 519 U.S. at 120-24.
As Justice Douglas observed in Williams v. Illinois, 399 U.S. 235, 242 (1970), "the
Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires that the statutory
ceiling placed on imprisonment for any substantive offense be the same for all
defendants irrespective of their economic status." Similarly, the procedural
"ceiling" placed on access to courts must be the same for all plaintiffs irrespective
of their economic status.
3. No Compelling Interest Justifies the Discriminatory Treatment
Under heightened scrutiny, a challenged classification must be narrowly tailored to
serve a compelling governmental interest. Washington v. Glucksberg, 521 U.S.
702, 721 (1997).
The § 1915(e)(2) screening procedure fails this test for several reasons:
20
First, any legitimate interest in filtering frivolous claims can be served through the
ordinary adversarial process under Rule 12—the same process used for fee-paying
litigants. Courts regularly dismiss meritless claims through Rule 12(b)(6) motions,
without need for special pre-service screening of indigent complaints.
Second, the current screening procedure is not narrowly tailored, as it subjects all
indigent complaints to pre-service review regardless of the nature or merit of the
claims. This broad-brush approach sweeps in meritorious constitutional claims
alongside potentially frivolous ones, creating an impermissible burden on
fundamental rights.
Third, less restrictive alternatives exist, such as:
• Applying the same post-service review standards to all litigants regardless of
economic status
• Providing notice and an opportunity to respond before dismissal
• Limiting pre-service screening to specific categories of clearly frivolous claims.
In Douglas v. California, 372 U.S. 353, 357 (1963), the Court emphasized that
"[t]here is lacking that equality demanded by the Fourteenth Amendment where the
rich man... enjoys the benefit of counsel's examination into the record, research of
the law, and marshalling of arguments on his behalf, while the indigent... is forced
to shift for himself." Similarly, there is lacking that equality demanded by the Fifth
21
Amendment where the fee-paying plaintiff enjoys the benefits of adversarial
proceedings, while the indigent plaintiff is subjected to secretive, ex parte
screening.
4. The Equal Protection Violation in Plaintiff's Case
The equal protection violation is clearly illustrated in Plaintiff's case. Due to his
indigent status, Plaintiff's complaint has been subjected to pre-service screening
that has effectively prevented him from supplementing the record with evidence of
continuing constitutional violations. A fee-paying plaintiff facing identical
circumstances would have had his complaint served immediately, with no pre-
service judicial review or stay of proceedings.
This disparate treatment serves no compelling governmental interest and cannot be
justified under the heightened scrutiny that applies to wealth-based classifications
affecting fundamental rights. As the Supreme Court emphasized in San Antonio
Independent School District v. Rodriguez, 411 U.S. 1, 20 (1973), the judicial
system must be particularly vigilant when classifications based on wealth "tend to
be drawn along lines of political power."
D. HEIGHTENED SCRUTINY MUST APPLY TO THIS CONSTITUTIONAL CHALLENGE
1. Multiple Constitutional Rights Are Implicated
The constitutional challenge to § 1915(e)(2) screening implicates multiple
22
fundamental rights, each of which independently triggers heightened scrutiny:
First, access to courts is protected as a fundamental right under the First
Amendment's Petition Clause. California Motor Transport, 404 U.S. at 510. The
Supreme Court has consistently applied strict scrutiny to burdens on First
Amendment rights. Button, 371 U.S. at 438 ("The decisions of this Court have
consistently held that only a compelling state interest... can justify limiting First
Amendment freedoms.").
Second, court access is protected by due process, particularly when constitutional

Anti-Fascist Refugee Comm., 341 U.S. at 170 (Frankfurter, J., concurring).
Third, wealth-based classifications affecting fundamental rights trigger heightened
equal protection scrutiny. M.L.B., 519 U.S. at 120-24.
The convergence of these multiple constitutional protections "calls for more
exacting judicial scrutiny." Employment Division v. Smith, 494 U.S. 872, 881
(1990) (discussing the heightened scrutiny that applies when multiple
constitutional rights are implicated).
2. Wealth-Based Classifications Affecting Court Access Are Inherently Suspect
23
The Supreme Court has consistently recognized that wealth-based classifications
affecting access to courts are constitutionally suspect. As Justice Harlan observed,
even while dissenting in Griffin, there is a "constitutional rule" that "a man's mere
property status, without more, cannot be used by a state to test his rights to equal
treatment in the face of the law." Griffin, 351 U.S. at 35 (Harlan, J., dissenting).
This principle has deep roots in our constitutional tradition. In Chambers, 207 U.S.
at 148, the Court emphasized that "the right to sue and defend in the courts... lies
at the foundation of orderly government." When this foundational right is burdened
based solely on economic status, the most exacting judicial scrutiny is required.
The Court has specifically held that "lines drawn on the basis of wealth or property,
like those of race, are traditionally disfavored." Harper v. Virginia State Bd. of
Elections, 383 U.S. 663, 668 (1966). The § 1915(e)(2) screening procedure draws
precisely such a line, subjecting indigent plaintiffs to additional procedural hurdles
solely because of their economic status.
3. The § 1915(e)(2) Procedure Fails Heightened Scrutiny
Under heightened scrutiny, the government must demonstrate that the challenged
practice is narrowly tailored to serve a compelling governmental interest.
Washington v. Glucksberg, 521 U.S. 702, 721 (1997).
24
The § 1915(e)(2) screening process fails this test for several reasons:
First, the government cannot demonstrate a compelling interest in treating indigent
plaintiffs differently from fee-paying plaintiffs at the pleading stage. Any
legitimate interest in filtering frivolous claims applies equally to all plaintiffs,
regardless of economic status.
Second, the current screening procedure is not narrowly tailored, as it imposes a
categorical disadvantage on all indigent plaintiffs without regard to the nature or
merit of their claims. This "blunderbuss approach" is antithetical to the narrow
tailoring required by heightened scrutiny. See Ward v. Rock Against Racism, 491
U.S. 781, 800 (1989).
Third, less restrictive alternatives exist that would serve any legitimate
governmental interest without imposing differential burdens based on wealth
status. These include applying the same Rule 12(b)(6) standard to all plaintiffs after
service, with the same procedural protections for all.
As the Court emphasized in Tennessee v. Lane, 541 U.S. 509, 533 (2004), "ordinary
considerations of cost and convenience alone cannot justify a State's failure to
provide individuals with a meaningful right of access to the courts." Similarly,
considerations of judicial efficiency alone cannot justify the federal courts' failure
to provide indigent plaintiffs with the same procedural protections afforded to fee-
25
