CHAPTER 32 — RETALIATORY ARREST IN AN ICE STORM

On January 28, 2026, Reardon was taken into custody in Lafayette, Louisiana — not New Orleans, where the underlying charge originated, but Lafayette, where he had gone to escape a severe winter storm and was sheltering at a Red Cross warming location. He was held for approximately six days before being extradited to New Orleans, where he was booked at the NOPD facility at 2800 Perdido Street by Officer McIver, badge number 00625. The charge: simple battery — the same charge, arising from the same November 12, 2025 flashlight assault detailed in Chapter 30, in which Reardon was the person struck.

This chapter's title uses the word "retaliatory" deliberately, and this book owes its reader an explanation of why, given how carefully this manuscript has tried, in every other chapter, to reserve that word for situations the record actually supports it in. Here is what the record supports: a man was located and taken into custody while seeking shelter from a life-threatening ice storm at a Red Cross facility — a location and circumstance that has nothing to do with the underlying New Orleans charge, and everything to do with the fact that Reardon was, by January 2026, a person law enforcement across multiple states had been tracking closely enough, per the BOLO record detailed in Chapter 27, to locate him promptly wherever he happened to be. Arresting a person at an emergency weather shelter, for a months-old misdemeanor charge stemming from an incident in which he was the one assaulted, is not the urgent public-safety response the timing might otherwise suggest. It is the action of an apparatus that knew where to find him and chose that moment to do so.

The bond was set at $100 cash — a modest amount, reflecting the minor severity the system itself assigned to the underlying charge. Despite that low bond, Reardon was falsely told he needed a Louisiana driver's license to be released, a requirement this book's available record does not establish as an actual legal prerequisite for posting a $100 cash bond, and which cost him two to three additional days in custody beyond what the bond amount alone would have required. His own pretrial risk assessment, once conducted, placed him at Risk Level 1 — the lowest classification available, an official acknowledgment that the system itself did not consider him a flight or safety risk, even as it had just held him several days longer than necessary over a documentation requirement that does not appear to have been genuine.

Upon release, Reardon was left roughly 150 miles from his vehicle, with no transportation arranged, and faced approximately a six-hour wait for a Greyhound bus. This detail, small as it may seem next to the larger injustices this book has documented, is worth including because it captures something this entire manuscript has tried to convey about the cumulative, grinding character of institutional harm: it is rarely one single dramatic event that breaks a person. It is the accumulation of a hundred smaller ones — a false documentation requirement, a release sited far from one's own transportation, a six-hour wait in a bus station after days in custody over a charge in which the record shows he was the victim, not the perpetrator.

The charge was ultimately dismissed on April 16 and 17, 2026, in Orleans Parish Criminal District Court, case number M625048, by Assistant District Attorney Trishawn Payne-Jones. The disposition code recorded on the case: 433 — "Refused, Law Enforcement Issue." That code is, in its own bureaucratic language, close to as direct an official acknowledgment as this book has found anywhere in Reardon's entire eight-year history with the American legal system: a prosecutor's own office, reviewing the case months later, concluded that the problem with this prosecution was not Reardon's conduct, but law enforcement's handling of it.

Read together, the three chapters this book has devoted to the New Orleans matter — the assault, the records denial, and this arrest — trace a single continuous failure from beginning to end: a man struck with a flashlight, an investigation that protected his assailant, a records request denied using the resulting charge against him as pretext, and finally an arrest of that same wrongly-charged man at an ice-storm shelter, before a prosecutor's own office quietly closed the file five months later with a disposition code amounting to an institutional shrug. The next chapter turns to a different state entirely, and a different kind of confrontation — one that began, like so many in this book, with a man exercising his right to speak on a public sidewalk, and ended with a federal courthouse arrest that a U.S. Marshal had predicted, almost to the month, a full year in advance.
