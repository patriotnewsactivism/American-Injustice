CHAPTER 4 — 50,000, AND AN ORDER SIGNED UNDER DURESS

My initial appearance on the aggravated stalking charge was held on May 30, 2017, before Justice Court Judge Carolyn Bell. Two things about that hearing matter enough to spend a chapter on them, and both point in the same direction: toward a process that had already decided what it wanted to happen before the evidence was heard.

The first is procedural, and it is the kind of fact that rarely makes it into a public account of a case because it sounds, out of context, like an unremarkable administrative detail. Judge Bell met privately with sheriff's investigators before the hearing. Ex parte contact between a judge and investigators, ahead of a hearing where that judge is supposed to weigh the evidence those same investigators generated, is not a neutral fact pattern. It is the kind of thing that, in a properly functioning system, gets a judge recused, not congratulated for efficiency. During the hearing itself, according to my account, Judge Bell cut me off twice when I attempted to raise the bill of sale — the same twelve-day discrepancy detailed in the previous chapter, the single most important piece of exculpatory evidence in the entire case.

The outcome of that hearing was a 50,000 bond, set on a charge whose central factual allegation a receipt already disproved.

It is worth placing that number next to another one that surfaces later in my recollection of my roughly six weeks in jail on this charge: that the same judge set a 0,000 bond, in an unrelated case, for a man accused of rape. That comparison is flagged here as my own claim — it is not independently sourced or docket-verified in the material available to me, and it should be read that way, not as an established fact. But it is worth including precisely because of how it is flagged, not despite it: the honest posture toward a claim like this is neither to suppress it because it can't be verified, nor to present it as settled because it's damning. It sits in the record as something I say happened, worth investigating further, not yet worth citing as proof of anything beyond my own perception of the disparity.

The second matter from this period is not a claim requiring a flag. It is close to the most disturbing single fact from that year, and it happened while I was in custody.

While I was jailed on the Lynch aggravated stalking charge, my defense counsel at the time — Brennan Horan, who had by then taken over from an earlier attorney, William C. Stennett — advised me that signing the Tannehill family's restraining order (case 2017-CV-217, discussed in the previous chapter) without contesting it would help secure my release from jail. I signed it. By my own account, and by the structure of the advice itself, I signed it under duress: not because I agreed with its terms or conceded its underlying allegations, but because my attorney told me it was the path out of a cell.

This detail matters for reasons beyond the 2017 case itself, and readers should hold onto it, because it resurfaces with enormous consequence five years later. The still-standing 2017-CV-217 restraining order — signed under the conditions just described, never substantively contested — becomes the legal predicate for an enhanced second aggravated stalking charge in June 2022, when I entered a building associated with the order during a scheduled child-custody hearing. Mississippi's aggravated stalking statute allows for an enhanced charge when a defendant has a qualifying prior conviction within roughly a seven-year lookback window. The order I signed in 2017, under advice that it was merely the fastest way to get out of jail, becomes — half a decade later — one of the load-bearing legal instruments in a second criminal prosecution, one that ultimately sends me to Mississippi custody for approximately a year.

Brennan Horan's role deserves its own scrutiny, separate from the advice itself. Horan is the brother of a sitting Mississippi state representative, Kevin Horan — a conflict of interest flagged explicitly in the bad-actors accounting of my story. Whether that family relationship shaped the advice given to a client in custody, facing an unwinnable-sounding bond and an attorney telling him the fastest way out was to sign away my objection to a restraining order I considered fraudulent, is not something the available record answers definitively. What the record does establish is the sequence: a man in jail, an attorney whose family had its own relationships to the local legal and political establishment, advice to sign, a signature under duress, and a document that would not finish doing its damage for another five years.

My own account adds two further details to this chapter's picture of Judge Bell, both flagged with appropriate caution since neither rests on independent documentary confirmation. First, I say I filed a formal judicial-misconduct complaint against Judge Bell over her handling of the May 30, 2017 hearing — a complaint that, per my own account, was dismissed without producing any disciplinary action. Second, and considerably more striking if accurate, I claim that the same judge, in a separate and unrelated case, set a 0,000 bond for a defendant accused of rape — a fraction of the 50,000 she set for me over an aggravated stalking charge already shown here to have rested on a factual impossibility. That specific comparison cannot be verified against an independent case record, and it is not presented as established fact; it is presented as my own claim, worth including precisely because — if true — it would be one of the starkest illustrations available anywhere in my story of Judge Bell's May 2017 conduct, and because a claim this specific and checkable is the kind of thing a diligent reader, or a future researcher with access to Mississippi's court records, could in principle confirm or refute directly.

None of this exonerates what comes later. My own conduct in the years between 2017 and 2022 — detailed across the next several chapters — includes genuine escalations I chose, not ones forced on me. But the duress-signed order belongs at the root of that later chapter, not as an excuse for what follows, but as an honest accounting of how it started: not with a crime, but with a bill of sale nobody would listen to, a judge who wouldn't hear it, and a signature obtained from me when my only other option was staying in a cell.
