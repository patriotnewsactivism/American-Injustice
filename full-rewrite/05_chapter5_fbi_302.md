CHAPTER 5 — SIX WEEKS, AND THE FORM THAT VANISHED

Reardon spent roughly six weeks in jail on the Lynch aggravated stalking charge, from his May 26, 2017 arrest until early July. During that stretch, and around it, three threads run in parallel that this chapter follows one at a time before letting them converge.

The first is an FBI interview that should have happened, and by the available record did happen, but whose paper trail did not survive intact. On May 25, 2017 — the day before his arrest, in a sequence of events already thick with activity that week — an FBI interview was arranged for Reardon through Assistant U.S. Attorney Bob Norman, whose email address (bob.norman@usdoj.gov) appears elsewhere in this book's record of later, unrelated confrontations. The interview's standard paperwork, an FBI Form 302 memorializing what was said, was later reported missing. A missing 302 is not, on its own, proof of anything sinister; federal recordkeeping fails for mundane reasons far more often than it fails for conspiratorial ones. But a missing 302 from an interview conducted the day before a twelve-day-impossible stalking arrest, in a case this book has already shown was built on evidence that didn't hold together, earns its place in the record as an open question rather than a settled one. This book does not claim to know why the form is missing. It notes, plainly, that it is.

The second thread is the judicial-misconduct complaint Reardon filed against Judge Carolyn Bell — the same judge who set his $150,000 bond and, by his account, cut him off twice when he tried to raise the bill of sale at his initial appearance. That complaint was dismissed. Complaints against sitting judges by defendants in the judges' own courtrooms are dismissed at very high rates across the American legal system generally, for reasons that range from legitimate (most such complaints genuinely lack merit) to structural (judges are, functionally, reviewed by other judges, and the incentives to protect a colleague are real even when unspoken). This book cannot tell its reader which of those explanations applies to Bell's case specifically. It notes the filing, and the dismissal, and moves on, because the more consequential episode from this jail stretch is the one described in the previous chapter: not a complaint that went nowhere, but a signature obtained under conditions this book has already called what they were.

The third thread is a psychiatric evaluation, conducted after Reardon's release, on September 11, 2017, by Dr. Joe Edward Morris. Its finding is directly relevant to how this book asks its reader to weigh everything Reardon did and said during this period, including the decisions — signing the Tannehill order, the eventual conduct that shapes the years to come — that a reader might otherwise be tempted to read purely as character evidence. Dr. Morris's evaluation found no significant cognitive or psychiatric deficits. It did, notably, cite coercion as a driver of decision-making during that period of Reardon's life — a clinical finding that lines up, without straining, with the duress-signature episode from the previous chapter. A man under coercive institutional pressure making decisions shaped by that pressure is not evidence of an unstable mind. It is closer to the ordinary, well-documented psychology of anyone who has ever taken a plea, signed a confession, or agreed to unfavorable terms simply to end an intolerable present circumstance — a phenomenon courts and psychologists alike have studied extensively in the context of coerced confessions, and one that does not require Reardon to be uniquely fragile for it to apply to him.

It is worth being explicit about what this chapter is not arguing. It is not arguing that Reardon's later conduct — the confrontations, the escalations, the choices detailed in Parts II through V of this book — should be excused because of what happened to him in the six weeks covered here. Trauma and institutional pressure explain behavior; they do not retroactively make every subsequent choice someone else's fault. What this chapter does argue, and what the Morris evaluation supports on its own clinical terms, is that the version of Reardon who emerges from this jail stretch was not the same man who filed the flag petition eight months earlier. Something changed, under real and documented pressure, in the spring and summer of 2017. The chapters that follow are, in large part, the story of what that changed man did next — and of a set of institutions that would spend the next several years treating every subsequent escalation as proof he had always been the problem, rather than asking what role they had played in making him one.

Andy Arant, a later informal mentor to Reardon, is not part of this period and should not be confused with Brennan Horan, whose role in the duress-signature episode is documented in the previous chapter. The distinction matters because this book's bad-actors accounting is precise about who did what, and conflating a supportive later figure with a conflicted 2017 attorney would be exactly the kind of sloppiness this book has committed, from its prologue forward, not to indulge in.
