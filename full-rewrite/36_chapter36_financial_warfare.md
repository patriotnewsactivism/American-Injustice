CHAPTER 36 — THE FINANCIAL WARFARE OF CONSTITUTIONAL LITIGATION

This book has spent thirty-five chapters documenting a legal and institutional conflict spanning eight years, two states, and more than a dozen separate courts, agencies, and departments. This chapter steps back from the narrative to ask a question none of the preceding chapters could fully answer while staying inside the story: what did all of this actually cost, and who was built to survive that cost?

Start with the bond amounts alone, because they are the plainest, most quantifiable measure of what this book has documented. The original 2017 Lynch charge carried a $150,000 bond, set by Justice Court Judge Carolyn Bell. The 2023 Galveston felony DWI charge — the one this book's own analysis, corroborated by a federal magistrate's later findings, treats as built on a contradicted affidavit — carried a $100,000 bond, and held Reardon in pretrial custody for approximately eleven months before the felony was quietly downgraded. A person facing $250,000 in cumulative bond exposure across two proceedings, years apart, in two different states, is not a person the system expected to be able to simply post bond and walk. Bond amounts of this size function, whatever their stated purpose, as a form of pretrial punishment for anyone who cannot immediately produce them — and Reardon, a veteran discharged under other-than-honorable conditions, working as an independent journalist without institutional backing, was never going to be able to produce them.

Then count the towing and impoundment fees, the small-dollar charges that recur across this book's account of 2025 and 2026 almost as a background hum beneath the larger legal drama: the June 2026 seizure of Reardon's 2016 Mitsubishi Outlander in Galveston, held twenty-two hours and towed to a lot charging $373.94 for two days, on top of a base impound fee this book has documented Galveston's own municipal code capping — a cap a private towing company appears, per a June 2026 temporary restraining order this book has referenced, to have stacked additional daily storage and surcharge fees on top of, in what that TRO itself characterized as a profit-motive argument. A single tow-and-impound cycle costing several hundred dollars is a rounding error against a $100,000 bond. It is also, for a homeless veteran living out of his car — Reardon's own documented living situation across large stretches of this book's timeline — potentially the difference between having a functioning vehicle and not having one, a distinction that matters enormously to a person whose work requires him to travel between courthouses, file records requests in person, and simply survive.

Now count what this book has already shown, in Chapter 24, is the single most consequential financial variable in this entire eight-year story: the presence or absence of counsel. Reardon's 2022 pro se federal lawsuit against Lafayette County, filed without a lawyer, was dismissed with prejudice. The 2024 post-conviction relief petition that produced the January 2025 full vacatur of his 2022 stalking conviction was filed by Jacob Howard of the MacArthur Justice Center — a nationally recognized civil rights litigation organization with institutional resources no individual pro se litigant can replicate. The difference between those two outcomes is not, primarily, a difference in the strength of Reardon's underlying grievances; the underlying facts supporting both filings were substantially the same facts this book has documented since Chapter 4. The difference is the presence of institutional legal resources in one case and their absence in the other.

This is where this chapter's title earns its place. "Financial warfare" is a strong phrase, and this book does not use it lightly. It is not a claim that any specific official set out, consciously, to bankrupt Reardon into submission — this book has no evidence supporting that specific intent, and it does not allege it. It is a claim about structure and effect, independent of intent: a system in which $150,000 and $100,000 bonds, stacked towing fees, and the sheer multi-year duration of parallel litigation across multiple states functions, in its cumulative effect, as a mechanism that favors institutional defendants — counties, cities, federal agencies, all backed by public budgets and government attorneys who are paid regardless of outcome — over an individual plaintiff who must personally absorb every dollar of bond, every towing fee, every filing cost, and every hour of unpaid labor spent drafting his own pro se motions, while also trying to survive day to day.

Reardon's own pro se litigation sophistication grew considerably across this book's timeline — the nine motions citing thirty-two Supreme Court cases filed in Union County Justice Court in May 2026, detailed in Chapter 33, reflect a considerably more sophisticated litigant than the one who filed the dismissed 2022 federal complaint four years earlier. That growth is itself a data point in this chapter's argument: it took years of forced, unpaid legal self-education, conducted while homeless and while fighting parallel cases in multiple states, for Reardon to develop the litigation skill that institutional defendants could simply purchase from day one, with public money, without having to personally live through a single day of what they were defending against.

This book does not offer a policy solution to this asymmetry in this chapter, because that is not this book's role. What it offers is documentation: a decade-long record of exactly what it costs, in dollars and in years, for one individual to hold a set of local, state, and federal institutions accountable through the American legal system's own designated channels — and a clear-eyed accounting of how much of that cost fell on Reardon personally, rather than on the institutions whose conduct this book has spent thirty-five chapters examining. The epilogue that follows closes this book not with a verdict, because the story this book tells does not yet have one, but with where things stand as of this writing, and what remains unresolved.
