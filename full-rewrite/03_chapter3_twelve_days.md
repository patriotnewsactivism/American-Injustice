CHAPTER 3 — THE SETUP: TWELVE DAYS

On May 24, 2017, two things happened that belong in the same paragraph even though the officers involved never put them there.

First: Reardon underwent a hair-follicle drug test that came back positive for amphetamine and ecstasy. This book is not going to bury that fact in a footnote or wait until a "counterargument" section to raise it. It is a genuinely complicating piece of the record, and any honest account of what follows has to hold it in view rather than pretend the man at the center of this story was, in this period of his life, simply an innocent bystander with clean hands. Whatever else is true about the case Lafayette County built against him, this detail is real, and it is his.

Second, and separately: Investigator Jarrett (also recorded in some documents as Jared) Bundren reviewed Reardon's Facebook page that same day, specifically looking for a threat directed at Todd Lynch. This review is the investigative basis — the paper trail — for what becomes, two days later, an aggravated stalking arrest. It is worth noting what Bundren was doing on May 24: building a case, from social media content, against a man who had criticized a local business owner on a livestream five days earlier. That is a strange, thin foundation for a felony-adjacent stalking charge under ordinary circumstances. It becomes something closer to indefensible once the charge's actual factual allegation comes into view.

Also in this same window — the record places it in this same charged period around May 24-25, 2017 — the Tannehill family (the same family whose name recurs later in this book in connection with Oxford's mayor) filed a restraining order against Reardon. That case carries its own docket number, 2017-CV-217, and Reardon calls it fraudulent. Rhea Tannehill, he says, threatened him in a courthouse hallway during this period, and was physically restrained by Major John Hill — who this book's own bad-actors accounting places firmly in the "not a bad actor" column, since his role here was to prevent an altercation, not cause one. The Tannehill restraining order hearing was held before Judge Glenn Alderson. This book returns to 2017-CV-217 in Chapter 5, because what happens to it — specifically, how Reardon came to sign it — is inseparable from the conditions of his detention on the charge this chapter is actually about.

There is a separate, later thread that bears directly on how the public first came to understand these events, and it belongs here rather than in a later media chapter because it concerns the same week this chapter documents. In a recorded call years afterward, Reardon put the question directly to Rebecca, a publisher at the Oxford Eagle, about the paper's May 2017 coverage of his arrest — coverage he alleges was built on unqualified, inaccurate information fed to the paper by Major Alan Wilburn, one of the four officers who would make the May 26 arrest this chapter is about to detail. On the call, Reardon says, the publisher declined to produce any evidence supporting the article's specific factual claims when pressed. This book cannot independently verify what Wilburn told the Oxford Eagle or what the paper printed as a result, and it records this as an unresolved allegation rather than a proven fact — but it flags the allegation because it fits a pattern this book will document repeatedly in later chapters: officials in this story shaping public narrative through informal channels, well outside of anything that would be subject to cross-examination or documentary scrutiny in a courtroom.

That charge: on May 26, 2017, four senior Lafayette County law enforcement officers — Chief Deputy Scott Mills, Major Alan Wilburn, Lieutenant Jared Bundren, and Sheriff Joey East — arrived and arrested Reardon for aggravated stalking. The allegation, as charged, was that he had threatened Todd and Ashley Lynch with an AR-15 "on or before May 8, 2017."

Reardon did not purchase the AR-15 in question until May 20, 2017. There is a bill of sale, from seller Larry Watkins, found through Armslist, dated May 20. Twelve days after the date the state's own charge alleges he used the weapon to threaten someone.

It is difficult to overstate how clean a piece of exculpatory evidence this is, and how rarely a case like this one produces something this unambiguous. This is not a swearing match between Reardon's memory and an officer's memory. It is not a question of who is more credible, or whose account of an ambiguous encounter should be believed. It is a dated commercial transaction record showing that the instrument of the alleged crime did not exist on the date the alleged crime occurred. A charge built on an impossibility does not become less of an impossibility because four officers, rather than one, showed up to make the arrest. If anything, the show of force — a chief deputy, a major, a lieutenant, and the sheriff himself, descending on one man over an allegation that a piece of paper in a gun-sale record could have disproven in thirty seconds of investigative diligence — raises the more uncomfortable question this book keeps returning to: was this ever really about a threat to Todd and Ashley Lynch, or was the boycott broadcast, and the city hall meeting three days before it, and the flag petition eight months before that, the actual reason four of Lafayette County's senior law enforcement officers decided this particular citizen needed to be taken off the street that week?

The rifle purchase itself deserves one more sentence of scrutiny before this chapter moves on, because a fair reader will have already asked it: why would a man buy a rifle, from a private seller, five days after criticizing a local business owner on Facebook Live, if not in connection to some kind of conflict? The honest answer is that the record does not establish any connection between the rifle purchase and Frank & Marlee's at all, and the state's own charge — built around a date twelve days before the purchase — implicitly concedes it can't establish one either. A man buying a gun in Mississippi in May 2017 is, absent some independent evidentiary link, evidence of nothing beyond a gun purchase. The state needed the rifle to exist on May 8 to make its charge work, and it didn't, and that gap is not a technicality. It's the whole case.

What comes next — Judge Carolyn Bell's initial appearance, the $150,000 bond, and the restraining order Reardon signed under conditions his own attorney would later be accused of engineering — is the story of what a justice system does once it has already committed to an arrest that a bill of sale contradicts. It rarely reverses course cleanly. It usually just keeps going.
