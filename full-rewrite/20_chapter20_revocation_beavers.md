CHAPTER 20 — REVOCATION: BEAVERS'S TESTIMONY

On November 3, 2022, a revocation hearing was held before Judge Tollison to determine whether Reardon's October 28 visit to the FBI's Oxford office violated the terms of the banishment order detailed in the previous chapter. The central evidence supporting the finding that it did — a finding that sent Reardon into approximately one year of Mississippi custody — was the testimony of Deputy Kandis Beavers.

This book has already introduced Beavers once, in Chapter 10, as the deputy who initiated the February 2021 traffic stop that became the basis for Reardon's DUI conviction, and as the officer whose department's sheriff would later give two mutually contradictory sworn accounts of how the department preserved video evidence from that same stop. This chapter is where Beavers's own credibility, rather than her department's, becomes the central issue, and the evidence bearing on it is a recorded phone call.

According to Reardon's account, corroborated by a recorded call this book has directly reviewed, there exists a separate, prior recorded conversation between Reardon and Beavers in which Beavers was made aware, in advance, of Reardon's intent to visit the FBI's Oxford office on a walk-in basis. That prior conversation, per Reardon's account, occurred within roughly ninety minutes of other relevant statements bearing on the October 28 visit's context. Reardon's contention — one this book's own bad-actors accounting treats as established rather than merely alleged, given the direct documentary support — is that Beavers's later police report and her sworn testimony at the November 3, 2022 revocation hearing contradicted what she had told Reardon, or been told by Reardon, in that earlier recorded call: specifically, that her testimony obscured or misrepresented her own prior knowledge of the visit's nature and timing in a way that made the October 28 visit sound like a surprise violation rather than a pre-notified, grace-period visit to a federal law enforcement office.

If that account is accurate — and this book treats it as accurate based on the recorded call's existence and content, while noting plainly that a court has not yet formally adjudicated the perjury allegation itself — then the central evidentiary basis for a Mississippi court sending a man into approximately a year of custody was testimony a sworn deputy gave that contradicted her own prior, recorded knowledge. That is not a minor evidentiary dispute. It is the difference between a genuine banishment violation and a technical, pre-notified visit during an explicit grace period being recharacterized, through testimony this book treats as false, into grounds for revocation.

This book knows what Reardon did afterward, because he documented it himself: on August 4, 2023 — nearly nine months after the revocation hearing, and, tellingly, six days before an entirely separate arrest in Galveston, Texas that Part IV of this book covers in full — Reardon called the Lafayette County Sheriff's Department and spoke with Chief Deputy Scott Mills, attempting to file an internal affairs complaint against Beavers for perjury, explicitly referencing the recorded call and its contradiction with her sworn testimony. Mills declined to accept the complaint over the phone and insisted Reardon file it in person. Reardon refused, citing a reasonable and specific concern: a department investigating its own deputy's alleged perjury, using the same department's own complaint process, presents an obvious institutional conflict of interest, one that this book has already documented playing out in a different but structurally similar form in the Metro Narcotics scandal detailed elsewhere in this book, where Sheriff East himself was both a signatory to a missing-funds lockbox and one of the officials tasked with investigating its shortfall.

The timing this chapter closes on is worth stating plainly, without overclaiming what it proves: a documented attempt to report a Lafayette County deputy for perjury, refused administrative traction by that deputy's own department, followed six days later by an arrest in a different state that Part IV of this book will show involved its own credibility problems with the arresting officer's sworn account. This book labels that sequence, at Reardon's own explicit request and using his own words, as "institutional coverup followed by institutional retaliation" — coverup being Mills's refusal to accept the perjury complaint through any channel other than one requiring Reardon to walk into the same building housing the officers he was accusing, and retaliation being what happened in Galveston six days later. This book flags that specific causal link — that the Galveston arrest was retaliatory for the perjury complaint — as Reardon's own theory, [CLAIM], not an independently established fact. The tight timing between the two events is documented and real; what caused what is not something this book's available record can prove.

The next chapter follows Reardon through the roughly one year of Mississippi custody that the November 3, 2022 revocation produced — the year in which, this book's reader should hold in mind throughout, the deputy whose testimony sent him there had already, on tape, contradicted herself.
