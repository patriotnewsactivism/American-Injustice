CHAPTER 20 — REVOCATION: BEAVERS'S TESTIMONY

On November 3, 2022, a revocation hearing was held before Judge Tollison to determine whether my October 28 visit to the FBI's Oxford office violated the terms of the banishment order detailed in the previous chapter. The written order that came out of that hearing states the formal ground for revocation in language worth quoting exactly: I had violated the terms of my post-release supervision "by appearing in Lafayette County not at his residence or prior approved court business." That phrase is the official, on-paper basis for sending me into a year of custody. It says nothing about the FBI visit specifically, nothing about notice given in advance, and nothing about the grace period discussed in the previous chapter. It is a general finding that I was present in the county outside an approved purpose — a finding broad enough to cover almost any activity I might have undertaken there, and one that depended entirely on whose account of that presence the court believed. The central evidence supporting the finding that it did — a finding that sent me into approximately one year of Mississippi custody — was the testimony of Deputy Kandi Beavers.

Beavers was introduced once already, in Chapter 10, as the deputy who initiated the February 2021 traffic stop that became the basis for my DUI conviction, and as the officer whose department's sheriff would later give two mutually contradictory sworn accounts of how the department preserved video evidence from that same stop. It is worth pausing on the fact that the same deputy appears at two of the most consequential turns in this entire narrative, eighteen months apart: first initiating the traffic stop that produced a DUI conviction I have never disputed, and then, in November 2022, providing the testimony that sent me into a year of state custody. One appearance does not implicate the other on its own. But a pattern of a single officer's account carrying decisive weight at two separate, high-stakes junctures in the same defendant's record is the kind of pattern worth naming rather than treating as coincidence, particularly once the second appearance is shown, in the paragraphs that follow, to rest on testimony a recorded phone call directly contradicts. This chapter is where Beavers's own credibility, rather than her department's, becomes the central issue, and the evidence bearing on it is a recorded phone call.

According to my account, corroborated by a recorded call I have, there exists a separate, prior recorded conversation between Beavers and me in which Beavers was made aware, in advance, of my intent to visit the FBI's Oxford office on a walk-in basis. That prior conversation, per my account, occurred within roughly ninety minutes of other relevant statements bearing on the October 28 visit's context. My contention — treated here as established rather than merely alleged, given the direct documentary support — is that Beavers's later police report and her sworn testimony at the November 3, 2022 revocation hearing contradicted what she had told me, or been told by me, in that earlier recorded call: specifically, that her testimony obscured or misrepresented her own prior knowledge of the visit's nature and timing in a way that made the October 28 visit sound like a surprise violation rather than a pre-notified, grace-period visit to a federal law enforcement office.

If that account is accurate — and it is treated as accurate here based on the recorded call's existence and content, while noting plainly that a court has not yet formally adjudicated the perjury allegation itself — then the central evidentiary basis for a Mississippi court sending me into approximately a year of custody was testimony a sworn deputy gave that contradicted her own prior, recorded knowledge. That is not a minor evidentiary dispute. It is the difference between a genuine banishment violation and a technical, pre-notified visit during an explicit grace period being recharacterized, through testimony treated here as false, into grounds for revocation.

My response is documented because I recorded it myself: on August 4, 2023 — nearly nine months after the revocation hearing, and, tellingly, six days before an entirely separate arrest in Galveston, Texas that Part IV covers in full — I called the Lafayette County Sheriff's Department and spoke with Chief Deputy Scott Mills, attempting to file an internal affairs complaint against Beavers for perjury, explicitly referencing the recorded call and its contradiction with her sworn testimony. Mills declined to accept the complaint over the phone and insisted I file it in person. I refused, citing a reasonable and specific concern: a department investigating its own deputy's alleged perjury, using the same department's own complaint process, presents an obvious institutional conflict of interest — one already documented playing out in a different but structurally similar form in the Metro Narcotics scandal detailed elsewhere, where Sheriff East himself was both a signatory to a missing-funds lockbox and one of the officials tasked with investigating its shortfall.

The timing this chapter closes on is worth stating plainly, without overclaiming what it proves: a documented attempt to report a Lafayette County deputy for perjury, refused administrative traction by that deputy's own department, followed six days later by an arrest in a different state that Part IV will show involved its own credibility problems with the arresting officer's sworn account. That sequence is labeled here, at my own explicit request and using my own words, as "institutional coverup followed by institutional retaliation" — coverup being Mills's refusal to accept the perjury complaint through any channel other than one requiring me to walk into the same building housing the officers I was accusing, and retaliation being what happened in Galveston six days later. The specific causal link — that the Galveston arrest was retaliatory for the perjury complaint — is flagged here as my own theory, a claim rather than an independently established fact. The tight timing between the two events is documented and real; what caused what is not something the available record can prove.

The next chapter follows me through the roughly one year of Mississippi custody that the November 3, 2022 revocation produced — a year that began with the deputy whose testimony sent me there having already, on tape, contradicted herself.
