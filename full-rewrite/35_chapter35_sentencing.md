CHAPTER 35 — SENTENCING

As this account goes to print, Reardon is scheduled to be sentenced on Friday, August 7, 2026, in the federal courthouse case detailed across Chapters 27 through 29 — the case arising from his August 25, 2025 arrest at the John M. Shaw United States Courthouse in Lafayette, Louisiana, and the January 16, 2026 guilty verdict that followed his December 2025 bench trial. The hearing will take place at the Lake Charles Division of the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Louisiana, conducted via secure video link rather than an in-person courtroom appearance.

The government's sentencing recommendation is unsupervised probation, conditioned on a ban restricting Reardon from federal courthouses except when he is actively filing a lawsuit. Reardon's own strategy, decided well before this hearing, is to object to that specific condition directly, and to request instead the full thirty-day statutory maximum sentence available under the underlying regulation.

This choice deserves explanation, because on its face a defendant asking a court to impose more custody rather than less probation is counterintuitive. A thirty-day sentence is finite. It has a beginning and an end, and once served, it is over — Reardon has, by this point, already served approximately thirteen days of custody combined across the Lafayette, New Orleans, and this federal matter, meaning the practical marginal cost of accepting the full thirty days is smaller than the number itself suggests. A courthouse-ban probation condition, by contrast, is not finite in the same way: it is an ongoing restriction on Reardon's ability to enter the very buildings where journalism, First Amendment auditing, and his own ongoing litigation — including Reardon v. Osteen, detailed in the previous chapter — necessarily take him. A probation condition banning a working journalist and pro se litigant from courthouses except when personally filing a lawsuit does not merely restrict conduct; it restricts newsgathering and legal access simultaneously, for the entire probationary period, in a way consistent with the analysis throughout Part V: a prior restraint on protected activity dressed in the more palatable language of a standard probation term.

Whether a reviewing court will ultimately agree with that characterization is a separate question this account does not try to settle in advance. What can be said plainly is that the choice Reardon has made — preferring a short, certain custodial sentence over an open-ended restriction on his ability to do the work documented here, however imperfectly, since Chapter 1's flag petition in 2016 — is consistent with everything this account has shown about him across ten years and two states. Whatever this record has required saying about Reardon's own conduct — the tripod at the Lafayette courthouse, the crude language, the profane sign carried into a Mississippi courtroom, the drone crash with a minor present — it has not shown a man who backs away from consequences when the alternative is surrendering his ability to keep doing the work. That consistency is not, on its own, proof that the work has always been done wisely. It is evidence of the pattern this account has traced throughout: a man choosing to keep pushing against institutions that have, across this entire manuscript, more often responded with escalating containment than with engagement.

Alongside the sentencing itself, Reardon's appellate team has prepared a motion to unseal the trial transcript, to be filed alongside a Notice of Appeal promptly after the hearing — timed to the triggering of the Rule 58(g)(2) appeal clock that begins running once sentence is imposed. That appeal will be built primarily around the spoliation and structural due-process argument detailed in Chapter 29 — the missing courthouse surveillance footage, and the defense's contention that the footage which does not survive would have shown federal marshals directing Reardon toward the exact location that became the basis for his conviction — rather than around the First Amendment claims alone. It is, by most measures, the stronger path: it does not require a reviewing court to resolve the more contested question of how much constitutional protection courthouse-lobby recording deserves, only whether Reardon received a fair trial on a complete evidentiary record — a record the government itself has already conceded, across Part V, is incomplete.

Separately, Reardon has made a deliberate decision to comply fully with a gag order currently in place regarding certain sealed testimony connected to this case, through the date of this sentencing, rather than risk contempt by speaking publicly about it now. His plan, once sentencing has concluded, is to challenge that gag order directly through a post-sentencing motion to unseal the relevant record. This account respects that same order: it has described the spoliation issue only in the terms already made public through the court record, and it declines to go further until the order itself is lifted or successfully challenged — because winning the right fight sometimes requires waiting for the right moment to have it.

This is where Part V, and the federal chapter of Reardon's decade-long conflict with American institutions, stands as of this writing. What comes next — the sentencing itself, the appeal, the motion to unseal, and whatever Reardon v. Osteen ultimately produces — is the subject of the epilogue that follows, and of the updates this ongoing record will continue to track for as long as this story remains unfinished.
