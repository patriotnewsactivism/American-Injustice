CHAPTER 1 — THE FLAG PETITION

In September 2016, Matt Reardon was not yet anyone's idea of a First Amendment auditor. He had left the Marine Corps five years earlier, discharged under conditions the record classifies as General Under Other Than Honorable, alcohol-related — a fact he does not hide from and one this book will not soften, because softening it would cheapen the more important fact sitting next to it: whatever the Corps' internal accounting of him was in 2011, the man who filed a Change.org petition in 2016 filed it the way anyone is supposed to file one. Publicly, under his own name, with a stated purpose, naming the two elected officials with the power to act on it.

The petition was titled "Cut off State Funding to MS City/Municipalities/Schools who refuse to fly MS State Flag." Then-Governor Phil Bryant and then-State Senator Chris McDaniel were named as the decision-makers whose attention the petition sought. By the time it closed, 4,486 people had signed it. Whatever a reader thinks of the underlying position — and reasonable people can and do disagree sharply about what a state flag should or shouldn't depict, and Mississippi's 2020 flag change three-and-a-half years later suggests the country as a whole eventually disagreed with the flag Reardon was defending — the act itself sits squarely inside the tradition the First Amendment exists to protect: petitioning government for redress, in public, by name, through a mechanism designed for exactly that purpose.

This matters for reasons that have nothing to do with the merits of the flag debate. It matters because everything that happens to Reardon from May 2017 onward gets narrated, by the officials who arrest him, commit him, and prosecute him, as the story of a man who is inherently disruptive — a "First Amendment auditor" in the pejorative sense the term picked up online, someone whose entire public identity is provocation for its own sake. The petition is the counter-evidence to that narrative, and it predates the narrative by eight months. Before Reardon ever picked up a camera and walked into a courthouse lobby, before any of the arrests, before the aggravated stalking charge that would define the next five years of his life, he was already doing the single most conventional, unthreatening, historically American thing a citizen can do when he disagrees with a government policy: he asked, loudly and publicly, for it to change, and he got several thousand of his neighbors to ask alongside him.

It is worth sitting with what that petition was not. It was not a courthouse lobby occupation. It was not a livestream confrontation with a federal prosecutor. It was not a drone flown into the side of a building. Those things come later, and they come from a man whose relationship with institutional authority had by then been reshaped, repeatedly and violently, by what those institutions had already done to him. The petition is the baseline — the version of Matt Reardon that existed before Lafayette County Sheriff's deputies decided, in May 2017, that he was a man who needed to be removed from circulation.

The people who would go on to arrest, commit, and prosecute Reardon over the following nine years rarely if ever mention the flag petition. There is a reason for that. A 4,486-signature Change.org petition filed eight months before the first arrest does not fit the narrative that Reardon was, from the outset, an unstable or dangerous man whose subsequent legal troubles were simply the inevitable consequence of who he already was. It fits a very different narrative: that of a civically engaged, occasionally provocative but entirely lawful private citizen who, within a year of exercising his most ordinary First Amendment rights, found himself facing a $150,000 bond on a stalking charge built on an allegation twelve days older than the weapon it accused him of using.

This book is going to ask its reader to hold two things in mind simultaneously for the next three hundred pages, and it is worth stating the discipline here, at the very start, rather than assuming the reader will infer it. The first thing is that governments, sheriff's departments, circuit clerks, and mayors' husbands do sometimes target the people who are most inconvenient to them, and that the machinery for doing so — a delayed bond hearing here, a "lost" piece of surveillance footage there, a psychiatric commitment timed to a custody dispute — is not conspiracy-theory material. It shows up, case by case, court filing by court filing, sworn affidavit contradicting sworn affidavit, throughout this book, and it shows up because it happened, not because Reardon says it happened. The second thing, held at the exact same time and given no less weight, is that Reardon is not a uniformly sympathetic figure, and this book will not pretend otherwise to make its argument land more cleanly. He has, at various points across this timeline, escalated confrontations he could have de-escalated, said things on camera he cannot take back, and made decisions — coaching a young daughter about a legal dispute, flying a drone with a minor present, leaving a tripod in front of an emergency exit — that a fair-minded reader is entitled to find genuinely troubling, independent of anything the government did to him.

Both of those things are true. The flag petition is where the story of the first one begins, cleanly, with no asterisk required. The second story begins eight months later, on a public sidewalk in Oxford, Mississippi, with a camera pointed at a piano bar.
