CHAPTER 1 — LYDIA, AND THE FLAG PETITION

My daughter Lydia Elise Reardon was born on August 19, 2014. She was extremely premature and weighed one pound nine ounces.

Earlier versions of this manuscript described her as both "eleven weeks early" and born at "twenty-five weeks gestational age." Those two statements do not fit together, and I do not have a medical record in the present source archive that resolves the discrepancy. Rather than choose one from memory, I am removing the gestational-week figure until it can be verified. The date of birth is reflected in the Lafayette County Chancery Court custody record; the birth-weight detail comes from my own account.

Her mother is Phyllis Marie Crowder. Whatever later happened between Crowder and me, Lydia's birth belongs before the litigation. We were two parents dealing with a child who entered the world far too early. I am not going to invent a NICU scene or put words into either of our mouths. The fact that matters to the story is simpler: by 2016, our relationship had ended and our disagreement over Lydia had become a court case.

The custody record gives that transition a date and a case file. On August 3, 2016, Crowder and I filed a Joint Complaint to Establish Paternity, Child Custody, and Child Support in Lafayette County Chancery Court. A later agreed order identifies Lydia's date of birth, finds me to be her natural father, and records that both parents were then considered fit and proper to care for her.

The lawyers matter because two names that recur throughout this book were already present before the 2017 flag dispute. Crowder was represented by J. Rhea Tannehill Jr. I was represented by Andy Arant. At that point, there was nothing inherently remarkable about the pairing. Tannehill was opposing counsel in a family case. Arant was my lawyer.

That is where I want the record to begin. I am not going to retroactively turn a 2016 custody representation into proof of a later political or law-enforcement conspiracy. The later intersections involving Rhea Tannehill are documented where they occur. In 2016, the paper says what it says: he represented Crowder; Arant represented me.

By September 2016, another part of my life had become public.

I started a Change.org petition titled "Cut off State Funding to MS City/Municipalities/Schools who refuse to fly MS State Flag." The petition was directed toward then-Governor Phil Bryant and then-State Senator Chris McDaniel. My archived count for the petition is 4,486 signatures.

The position I was advocating is not the position Mississippi ultimately chose. The state later replaced the 1894 flag. I am not rewriting my politics to make them easier to defend now. In 2016, I supported the existing Mississippi flag and believed state and local institutions should display it. I organized around that belief publicly and aggressively.

The important point for this chronology is the method I chose at the beginning: a petition. I put my name on a political demand, identified officials I wanted to influence, and asked other people to sign it. No criminal case arose from the petition itself.

The flag dispute did, however, move me into increasingly public confrontations with local government.

In April 2017, before the first Oxford arrest, I had a confrontation at Five Flags Speedway in Escambia County, Florida, over carrying the Mississippi flag in the grandstand area. I later recorded a call with Sergeant Palmer at the Escambia County Sheriff's Office. Palmer's position on that call was that the dispute involved obstruction of other patrons' view and a request from management rather than the political content of the flag. My position was that I had been threatened with arrest for protected conduct. We disagreed. I include that episode because it is relevant context for my state of mind going into May 2017, not because it proves anything about Lafayette County.

I had also taken the flag argument to public meetings. In Biloxi, I spoke before the city council and invoked my Marine Corps oath and the vote that had retained the Mississippi flag. I later claimed that the advocacy campaign generated roughly ten thousand calls to Biloxi City Hall. I do not have a municipal call log in the current archive proving that number, so it remains my own estimate rather than a verified statistic.

Oxford became the next focal point.

On May 16, 2017, I attended a meeting of the Oxford Board of Aldermen expecting to speak about the state flag. I had not been placed on that meeting's agenda. Mayor Pat Patterson told me that I was not on the agenda and that I could speak at the June 6 meeting if I got on it. I did. The published June 6 agenda later listed "Matt Reardon to address the board regarding the state flag."

That detail is important because it cuts against the easiest version of the story. Patterson did not, in that specific exchange, tell me I could never speak. He told me the procedural requirement and pointed me to the next meeting. I was more confrontational than he was. Outside the meeting, I asked him whether he was a "cultural Marxist." His recorded response remained focused on the agenda requirement.

By then I was also talking publicly about Robyn and Rhea Tannehill. I called Robyn Tannehill a "cultural Marxist" and said on livestream that I intended to publish a phone number so people could contact her. I also voiced a belief that Rhea Tannehill and other local figures were trying to silence me. At that time, the claim of a coordinated effort was my belief, not a fact I could document. That distinction matters now because later chapters contain actual documents involving the Tannehills; those later records should not be used retroactively to prove what I only suspected in advance.

I was also speaking publicly in armed, embattled terms. On a surviving recording from that period, I described having a .45 and a .40 and being ready in response to threats I said I was receiving. A fair account of what followed has to include that language. It does not prove that every later accusation against me was true, but it is part of the environment law enforcement and other people were seeing online.

The same is true of hostile online coverage about me. A YouTube channel called Reardon Watch archived and mocked portions of my public activity. The existence of the channel and its videos can be documented. What I cannot establish merely from the channel's existence is which particular public officials watched it, whether it influenced any individual officer, or whether it caused an official decision. Earlier drafts crossed that line by treating those inferences as facts. I will not do that here.

The baseline going into May 2017 is therefore mixed and fairly easy to state. I was a father already involved in a contentious custody case. Rhea Tannehill had represented the mother of my daughter in that case. I was publicly advocating for the Mississippi state flag. I had gathered thousands of signatures on an online petition. I was confrontational with public officials and increasingly combative on social media. I also believed I was being threatened and targeted, although the extent and source of those threats were not always independently documented.

None of that, by itself, explains the arrest that came next.

On May 1, 2017, before the May 16 board meeting and before the later Lynch dispute, I stood on the Oxford Square holding the Mississippi flag. Deputy Timmy Pruitt approached me. The encounter was recorded. That recording, rather than any retrospective description of my politics, is where the first Mississippi arrest can be judged.

The next chapter stays with that video.
