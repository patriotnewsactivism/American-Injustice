CHAPTER 17 — A SON BORN IN CONFINEMENT

On February 7, 2022, I sent a formal grievance to Communicare's executive director, Dr. Sandy Rogers, and copied the chancery court. I challenged the December commitment, accused Communicare and Lafayette County officials of violating my rights, demanded preservation of the records surrounding that proceeding, and made clear that I did not trust the treatment process that had produced it.

Two days later, Communicare counselor Susan Beard signed an **Affidavit of Non-Compliance with Outpatient Commitment for Mental Illness** in the same Lafayette County commitment case, Cause No. 2021-691(L). The document was electronically signed at 3:10 p.m. on February 9 and filed with the Lafayette County Chancery Clerk on February 10 at 2:32 p.m.

Unlike the first affidavit, this one is in the record clearly enough to read without reconstructing its basis from later allegations.

Beard wrote that I had been scheduled for an intake appointment on January 6; that I called the day before and asked to reschedule; that she left me a voicemail January 6; that I called January 7 and rescheduled for January 12; and that on January 11 I canceled again and requested a day on which she was in all-day training. She wrote that she offered options for January 20 and 21, that I said I would talk with my wife and call back, and that I did not call back to reschedule. She further wrote that on January 31 I called asking for the executive director and declined, at that time, to reschedule the intake. On February 2 she left me a voicemail saying the request to the executive director needed to be specific and in writing. As of the affidavit, she wrote, I still had not contacted her to reschedule.

That account is important because it does not support the simplest version I once told: that Communicare invented a missed appointment I had never addressed. The document records a chain of calls and rescheduling attempts. My position was different. I later argued that I had not abandoned treatment casually; I was withholding further participation while waiting for a response to the formal grievance I had sent on February 7. That explanation appears in my later court filings. Beard's affidavit, however, records the conduct as noncompliance with an existing outpatient order.

The accompanying Uniform Commitment Affidavit marked the treatment-history ground for further commitment and stated: "See attached summary. No treatment due to Mr. Reardon's failure to schedule and attend follow up with Communicare." Beard recommended commitment to a Mississippi state hospital. The court then entered an order finding that I had failed to comply with outpatient treatment ordered December 9, 2021 and directed that I be committed to the Mississippi State Hospital or another Department of Mental Health program for observation, diagnosis, and treatment for no more than ninety days unless terminated earlier.

I believed the timing was retaliatory because the affidavit followed immediately after my grievance. I said so in later filings. The chronology is indisputable; the motive is not. Beard's affidavit gives a facially stated treatment-based reason for the action, supported by a dated sequence of missed or rescheduled intake appointments. My grievance gives the context for why I was refusing to proceed as Communicare wanted. Those two documents sit against each other and the reader should see both.

The Sheriff's Department executed the writ shortly after midnight on February 11.

The contemporaneous sheriff's report is specific. At 12:52 a.m., Deputy Dustin Black notified Sergeant Joe Quarles that my vehicle had returned to the residence. Quarles responded with the writ. According to his report, I was recording and arguing that the order was illegal. He told me it was a court order and that they had to take me in. The report says I told them I was not going, after which Black handcuffed me and allowed my wife to collect my phones and recording equipment. Black transported me to the Lafayette County Detention Center.

There is no reason to clean up what the officers say happened next.

A second sheriff's narrative says detention-center staff would not allow me to keep a fleece jacket. According to the report, I cursed at staff, said they would have to fight me for it, and threatened to "whoop y'all's ass" if they took it. The report says Sergeant Quarles, Deputy Kandi Beavers, correctional officer Jametric Judson, and Deputy Black eventually put me on the ground and removed the jacket after I pushed away, backed up, and tried to pull my arms free. It says I stopped resisting once I was on the floor. The report records no medical treatment.

That is law enforcement's account of the jail incident, not a judicial finding, but it is a contemporaneous primary record and it is adverse to me. It belongs in this book. Whatever I believed about the legality of the commitment, threatening staff and physically resisting the removal of a jacket, if the report is accurate, did nothing to make my position stronger. It gave the same institutions I was accusing of mischaracterizing me a fresh written account of confrontational behavior on the night they took me into custody.

The confinement did not end at the county jail. My later federal complaint states that I remained there for roughly six days and was transferred on February 16 to East Mississippi State Hospital in Meridian. The commitment file itself identifies East Mississippi State Hospital as the treating facility and contains later hospital paperwork, including a March 8 Notice of Need for Continuing Hospitalization that listed the anticipated discharge date as "undetermined" at that time.

I challenged the second commitment on due-process grounds. In a June 2022 motion to vacate, I alleged that I repeatedly requested a hearing after being taken into custody and was told I was not entitled to one, even though I maintained that paperwork later indicated an in-person hearing had occurred. That is my filed allegation. I have not located a transcript or independent record proving that an actual hearing took place or proving the repeated denials I described. I therefore cannot convert that dispute into a settled fact. What the record does show is that the second commitment proceeded under the December outpatient order, Beard's noncompliance affidavit, and the court's recommitment order.

While I was confined, my son was born on February 17, 2022.

I am deliberately keeping his medical details out of this account beyond what is necessary to explain the event. My later filings state that the birth was an emergency and that I was unable to be present because I was confined at the state hospital. That is the human fact that matters here: whatever the legal merits of the commitment, I was in state custody when my son entered the world, and I was not there with my family.

I later alleged that I tried to obtain permission to leave for the family emergency and was denied. I have not located a facility record documenting those requests or the response, so I preserve that point as my account rather than as an institutional record.

There is another theory I have often attached to this commitment that does not belong in the factual narrative unless I can prove it better. I have said that immediately before the February confinement I requested financial information concerning the Lafayette County Law Enforcement Officers Association and that the request explains the retaliation. Earlier drafts went further, reciting an EIN, IRS filing classifications, supposed founders, and theories about what the association's finances might conceal. The materials presently verified for this book do not justify turning that suspicion into a factual corruption narrative. An AI-generated research memorandum in the archive repeats those claims, but an AI memorandum is not a primary source. Unless the underlying incorporation papers, IRS filings, request itself, and responsive records are placed in the evidence file and verified, that theory stays out of the publication narrative.

What does survive source review is already consequential enough.

Within roughly two months, the same commitment cause produced a December inpatient commitment and a February recommitment for outpatient noncompliance. The first began after Communicare was engaged by local law enforcement and community reports; the second followed a documented series of postponed intake appointments and my written grievance attacking Communicare's earlier conduct. I believed both were retaliatory. Communicare's records present them as mental-health and treatment-compliance actions. The sheriff's report from the second arrest records conduct by me that, if accurately described, was hostile and resistant. None of those facts erases the others.

When I eventually came out of the second confinement, I did not abandon the fight. I put the dispute into federal court.

On April 6, 2022, I filed a civil-rights complaint in the United States District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi. The case would become the first full federal test of the theory I had been developing for years: that Lafayette County officials, court actors, and mental-health personnel had not merely made isolated bad decisions, but had violated federal law in the process.

The federal court did not accept that theory. The next chapter has to explain why.
