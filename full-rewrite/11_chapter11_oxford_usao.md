CHAPTER 11 — THE OXFORD U.S. ATTORNEY'S OFFICE CONFRONTATION

Between July 19 and July 21, 2021, Reardon filmed outside the Oxford office of the United States Attorney. This is not, by itself, an unusual thing for him to have done by this point in his life. By mid-2021, roughly four years into the conflict with Lafayette County traced since Chapter 2, Reardon had settled into the practice that would eventually earn him, in official government correspondence, the label "First Amendment auditor" — filming government buildings, security personnel, and officials from public space, testing and documenting how those officials responded to being recorded. Whatever a reader thinks of the practice as a genre — no position is taken here on whether First Amendment auditing is a wise or generous way to spend one's time, only on whether it is lawful, which it generally is — the Oxford USAO confrontation is worth its own chapter for two reasons.

The first is Police Chief Jeff McCutchen's handling of the incident, which belongs in a small but real column of institutional actors who did their jobs correctly even while dealing with a citizen many of them found exhausting. McCutchen provided Reardon a same-day copy of the police report generated from the incident — a level of transparency and speed that stands in sharp contrast to the FOIA stonewalling, missing footage, and "gone forever" claims that recur throughout the Lafayette County side of this timeline. McCutchen's record elsewhere is mixed, not uniformly favorable, and this chapter does not launder that record. But same-day report production to a subject of an active law enforcement encounter, without a fight, is a small, genuine data point in his favor, and it is worth crediting exactly as much as it deserves — no more, no less.

The second reason this chapter earns its place is a recorded remark, caught on Reardon's own footage from the encounter, in which McCutchen referred to Assistant U.S. Attorney Bob Norman as "blowhard Bob." It is a small, almost throwaway line, the kind of comment one local law enforcement official makes about a federal prosecutor when he assumes the exchange is private or that its context won't travel. It traveled. It matters here for a narrow but real reason: Norman is the same federal prosecutor whose office arranged the May 25, 2017 FBI interview referenced in Chapter 5 — the interview whose Form 302 was later reported missing — and Norman's direct, recorded confrontation with Reardon during this same July 2021 encounter is one of the more vivid pieces of tape in this entire archive. A local police chief's private assessment of that same prosecutor as a "blowhard" does not prove anything about Norman's conduct in 2017 or 2021. It does suggest that Norman's approach to Reardon was not universally regarded, even among his own law enforcement colleagues, as measured or professional.

The confrontation itself, captured on the recording reviewed for this account, shows Norman engaging directly with Reardon rather than referring him to a subordinate or declining comment — itself an unusual choice for a sitting Assistant U.S. Attorney confronted by a citizen with a camera outside a federal building. Whatever was said in that exchange, the fact of a federal prosecutor personally engaging a First Amendment auditor on camera, rather than treating the encounter as beneath his notice, tells its own story about how seriously Reardon's activity in and around Oxford's federal institutions was being taken by mid-2021 — seriously enough that the county's top federal prosecutor apparently felt the encounter warranted his personal attention rather than a subordinate's.

This chapter closes with a note about pattern, because pattern is what makes an isolated confrontation worth remembering rather than forgetting: this same dynamic — a federal facility, a camera, an escalating confrontation with federal law enforcement personnel who engage directly rather than de-escalating or ignoring — recurs almost exactly four years later, in Lafayette, Louisiana, in the events detailed in Chapters 27 and 28. The Oxford U.S. Attorney's Office confrontation of July 2021 is not evidence, on its own, of a coordinated multi-state, multi-year campaign against Reardon. It is evidence that the basic shape of what would eventually happen to him in Lafayette in August 2025 — a federal building, a camera, an escalating encounter with federal personnel who chose confrontation over de-escalation — had already played out once before, four years earlier and four hundred miles away, with far lower stakes and no arrest. The next chapter turns to a lower-stakes but more personally troubling episode from the same period: a drone crash near the Lafayette County Justice Court, with Reardon's young stepdaughter present.
