CHAPTER 17 — THE FEDERAL LAWSUIT

On April 6, 2022, I filed my first federal civil rights lawsuit against Lafayette County and the officials who populate everything I've described so far — docketed as Reardon v. Lafayette County MS et al., case 3:22-CV-50-SA-JMV, in the Northern District of Mississippi before Judge Sharion Aycock. I filed it pro se, without an attorney, roughly seven weeks after missing the emergency birth of my son while committed under Susan Beard's February 9 affidavit, and roughly four and a half years after the twelve-day rifle impossibility that opened my conflict with Lafayette County's institutions.

There is a reasonable case to be made that this was the single most important legal decision I made in this entire saga, and there is an equally reasonable case that it was, procedurally, close to a doomed one from the start. Both are true.

The case for its importance: a federal §1983 civil rights complaint is, structurally, the only legal mechanism available to a private citizen seeking to hold state and local officials personally and institutionally accountable for the kind of pattern documented here — retaliatory arrests, spoliated evidence, contradictory sworn testimony, psychiatric commitments timed to inconvenient confrontations. State court, where nearly all of the underlying events in Parts I and II played out, offered me almost nothing in the way of a remedy against the institutions themselves; it offered me, at best, a chance to defend myself against the charges those institutions brought, which is a fundamentally different posture than affirmatively suing them. Filing in federal court, naming the county and its officials directly, was my first real attempt to go on offense after nearly five years of playing defense.

The case for its likely futility is equally straightforward, and it is worth stating plainly rather than glossed over. Pro se federal civil rights complaints against local governments and law enforcement officials face an extraordinarily steep set of doctrinal hurdles even when filed by experienced counsel — qualified immunity for individual officers, Monell liability standards for municipal defendants that require proof of an official policy or custom rather than isolated misconduct, and strict pleading standards under Twombly and Iqbal that require factual specificity a pro se litigant, drafting without formal legal training, often struggles to meet. The outcome, recorded plainly here because honest reporting requires it: the case was dismissed with prejudice on June 28, 2023, roughly fourteen months after it was filed.

A dismissal with prejudice — meaning the case cannot be refiled — is not a small setback. It is, in the ordinary vocabulary of federal litigation, closer to a complete loss. The available record does not establish the specific grounds on which Judge Aycock dismissed the case, and no speculation is offered here about whether the dismissal reflected the genuine legal weakness of a pro se complaint, judicial impatience with a difficult pro se litigant, or some mixture of both. What can be said is that the dismissal did not erase the underlying facts documented across the preceding sixteen chapters. Those facts remain true whether or not a federal court found my 2022 pro se complaint legally sufficient to proceed against them.

What the failed 2022 lawsuit foreshadows, worth carrying forward into the chapters that follow, is the difference between a case built without counsel and a case built with it. Nearly three years later, when the MacArthur Justice Center's Jacob Howard took up a related post-conviction matter on my behalf, the outcome was entirely different: a full vacatur, detailed in Part IV. The lesson embedded in that contrast is not that my underlying grievances were meritless in 2022 and became meritorious later. It is that the gap between a documented injustice and a legally cognizable, procedurally sound claim is often the gap between having a lawyer and not having one — a gap that falls hardest on exactly the kind of pro se, financially strained litigant I've been since the beginning.

Two months after filing this lawsuit, on June 28, 2022, I walked into a building associated with the 2017 Tannehill restraining order — the same duress-signed order from Chapter 4 — for a scheduled child-custody hearing. What happened next is the subject of the next chapter, and it is the moment where the signature I gave under duress in a Lafayette County jail cell in 2017 finally, five years later, came due.
