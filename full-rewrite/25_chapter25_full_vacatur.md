CHAPTER 25 — FULL VACATUR

On January 21, 2025, a Mississippi court fully vacated the 2022 aggravated stalking conviction and sentence — the plea entered September 30, 2022, the five-year suspended probation, and the five-year county-wide banishment from Lafayette County that had, by this point, shaped nearly three years of Reardon's life, detailed across Chapters 18 through 21 of this book.

This chapter is short because the fact at its center does not need padding to carry its weight, and because this book has already done the work, across the preceding nine chapters, of laying out exactly what the vacatur undid. It is worth restating plainly, in one place, what a full vacatur of this conviction actually means for how the rest of this book's account of 2022 should be read: a court, reviewing the same underlying record this book has presented — the 2017 restraining order signed under an attorney's advice that amounted to duress, the aggravated-stalking enhancement that transformed a minor and disputed building-access issue into a felony-adjacent charge, the revocation hearing built on Deputy Kandis Beavers's testimony that a recorded phone call contradicts — concluded that the conviction could not stand.

This is not a technicality or a procedural footnote. A full vacatur, as opposed to a partial modification or a resentencing, is the legal system's most complete available acknowledgment that a conviction should not have happened as it did. It does not, by itself, formally establish bad faith on the part of any individual official named in this book, and this book does not claim it does — vacaturs can rest on procedural defects, ineffective assistance of counsel, newly discovered evidence, or a combination of grounds that fall well short of a finding that any specific person lied or acted maliciously. What a full vacatur does establish, at minimum, is that the process that produced Reardon's 2022 conviction failed to meet the standard the law requires before a person can be branded a felon, banished from his home county, and subjected to a year of custody on a revocation.

Reardon relocated to Layton, Utah on February 1, 2025, roughly ten days after the vacatur — the first time since the events of Chapter 18 that he was not, in some form, under an active banishment, probation term, or pending revocation tied to Lafayette County, Mississippi. This book does not know, from the available record, exactly why Layton specifically, and it does not speculate. What matters for this chapter's purposes is the plain fact of the move: after eight years of continuous entanglement with Mississippi's courts, sheriffs, clerks, and commitment proceedings — an entanglement this book has traced from the flag petition in Chapter 1 through the vacated 2022 conviction in this chapter — Reardon was, for the first time since May 2017, not legally required to remain anywhere near the county where nearly all of it had happened.

The vacatur did not end the broader legal fight. Reardon v. Osteen, the federal civil rights suit against the Galveston officials and the Mississippi officials named alongside them, remained active and would go on to produce the magistrate's findings and the Osteen concession detailed in Chapter 23. And a separate Mississippi appellate matter — Reardon v. State, 2024-KM-00839-COA — was still working its way through the Mississippi Court of Appeals, and would produce, roughly thirteen months after this chapter's vacatur, a reversal this book covers next, one that would sit in direct and pointed contradiction with a sworn federal affidavit Circuit Clerk Jeff Busby had filed just months earlier.
