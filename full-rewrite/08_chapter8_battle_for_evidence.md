CHAPTER 8 — THE BATTLE FOR EVIDENCE

On July 7, 2020, Reardon filed a post-conviction relief motion — docketed as L20-316 — targeting his original 2017 Lynch aggravated stalking conviction, the case built on the twelve-day impossibility detailed in Chapter 3. It was denied without a hearing on July 30, 2020, by Circuit Court Judge John Kelly Luther. A Rule 59 motion for rehearing was denied on October 13, 2020.

The bare procedural facts of a denied PCR motion would not, on their own, merit much space in this book. PCR motions are denied far more often than they are granted, in Mississippi and everywhere else, and a denial without a hearing is not automatically evidence of anything beyond a judge concluding, on the papers alone, that the motion did not clear the legal threshold required to warrant one. What earns this episode its own chapter is what Reardon discovered afterward, and what he did with that discovery.

On August 12, 2020, Reardon filed a motion to recuse Judge Luther. According to Reardon's account — and this book flags it plainly as his account, because the available record does not include independent court administrative documentation establishing precisely when or how the assignment changed — his case had originally been assigned to a different Circuit Court judge, Kent Smith, and was swapped to Luther with no explanation given. Reardon says this is not an isolated, one-time irregularity: it is a swap he has observed happen twice, in matters touching his cases, Smith giving way to Luther both times without a stated reason on the record.

This is a serious allegation, and this book is not in a position to either confirm or dismiss it based on the material available. Judicial assignment changes happen for entirely mundane reasons far more often than they happen for improper ones — scheduling conflicts, recusals, docket rebalancing, judges rotating through specialized dockets. A change from Smith to Luther, even a repeated one, is not on its own evidence of anything improper; Mississippi circuit courts reassign cases among sitting judges routinely, and a two-judge swap pattern could just as easily reflect nothing more than how a small circuit's docket happens to be balanced between its members. What makes Reardon's suspicion at least worth recording, rather than dismissing outright, is the surrounding context this book has already established: a $150,000 bond set by a judge who allegedly met privately with investigators before the hearing; a restraining order signed under an attorney's advice that amounted to duress; an FBI form that vanished. None of that proves either Smith-to-Luther swap was improper. It does mean that, by August 2020, Reardon had accumulated enough documented reasons to distrust the ordinary functioning of Lafayette County's courts that an unexplained docket reassignment — twice, by his count — read to him as one more instance of a pattern, rather than as the routine administrative event it most likely was.

The recusal motion did not succeed. Luther's July 30 denial stood, and the October 13 rehearing denial closed that avenue.

It was in the weeks surrounding this fight — specifically a text exchange dated June 25 through 27, 2020 — that Reardon laid out to Christi McCoy, Crowder's own attorney, a theory this book treats with considerably more caution than anything else in this chapter, and flags accordingly: the notary-fraud theory. As Reardon has described it, Crowder's attorney at the time, Mona Pittman, filed a false or materially incomplete Chancery Court custody petition on August 26, 2017 — fast-filed, without proper summons, specifically timed to reduce Crowder's sentencing exposure in the then-pending Olive Branch false-reporting case — and later filed a corrected, "true" version of the same petition while reusing the same notary stamp from the original filing.

This book cannot verify that theory against an independent notarial record, a state bar complaint, or a court finding, and no such verification exists in the material available for this manuscript. It is included here, clearly labeled as [CLAIM], for a specific reason connected to something raised in the introduction of this book: theories that cannot be verified are not, for that reason alone, worthless, and an honest book does not simply omit everything it cannot independently confirm. What can be said in the notary theory's favor is that it is, by a wide margin, the single most internally consistent claim Reardon makes across the entire period covered by this manuscript — the dates line up with the Olive Branch sentencing timeline, the incentive structure (reducing a client's exposure in a pending criminal matter by hastily correcting a custody filing) makes logical sense, and it does not require positing an implausible level of coordination between unrelated actors. It could also, just as plausibly, be an ordinary correction to an imperfectly filed petition, of the kind family law attorneys make routinely, misread by a man who had by 2020 developed a well-earned and not-unreasonable suspicion of every procedural irregularity touching his cases.

The reader is entitled to weigh that theory for themselves, with the caveat attached plainly rather than buried. What is not in dispute is the broader pattern this chapter and the one before it establish: a false-reporting conviction against Crowder that produced no real consequence, a PCR motion against Reardon's own conviction that went nowhere, an unexplained Circuit Court judge swap he says he watched happen twice, and a growing, two-directional distrust between a man and the county court system that would only deepen over the following year. The next chapter turns to a different front in that same conflict — not the courts, but Child Protective Services, and a pattern of recurring accusations that Reardon and Madelyn would describe, in their own words during a July 2020 home visit, as happening "three years in a row."
