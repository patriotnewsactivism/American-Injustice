CHAPTER 9 — THREE YEARS RUNNING

On July 7, 2020, a Child Protective Services caseworker arrived at Reardon and Madelyn's home to investigate an anonymous hotline report. The visit was recorded, and the recording is one of the more valuable pieces of evidence drawn on for this period of the timeline — not because it is independent or neutral (it plainly is not; both Reardon and Madelyn are speaking in their own defense, to an investigator whose job is precisely to weigh what they say against everything else in the file) but because it is contemporaneous. It captures what Reardon and Madelyn were saying to a state investigator in real time, in 2020, years before any of this became material for a book, which makes it meaningfully different in evidentiary weight from a retrospective account assembled after the fact to serve a narrative.

On that recording, Reardon and Madelyn describe a pattern: that Phyllis Crowder had made CPS reports against them recurring three years in a row, each timed within roughly a three-month window, and each — in their telling — clustered around a pregnancy or a miscarriage in Crowder's own life. They describe Crowder, in the same conversation, as someone they believe has "multiple personality disorder," a characterization recorded here as their own clinical-sounding but non-clinical assessment, not as a diagnosis with any medical standing.

This chapter treats that recording exactly the way the previous two chapters treated the notary-fraud theory: as valuable, worth including, and clearly bounded by its own limits. A self-serving statement to an investigator is not proof of anything beyond the fact that it was said. But it is not nothing, either, and dismissing it entirely because it comes from an interested party would be its own kind of distortion — the same distortion this account has refused to apply to Crowder's own account of the November 2018 encounter, or to Joey East's sworn testimony, or to Clerk Busby's letters. Interested-party statements are evidence. They are weaker evidence than a bill of sale or a docket entry, and they are treated accordingly, but demanding this account ignore every statement that comes from someone with something at stake would leave almost nothing to read, because nearly everyone whose voice appears in this story had something at stake.

What can be said with somewhat more confidence, because it does not depend solely on Reardon and Madelyn's own framing, is the raw fact of repetition: multiple CPS referrals, from the same reporting source, recurring across multiple years, none of which is documented in the available record as having produced a substantiated finding against Reardon or Madelyn. A single unsubstantiated CPS report proves very little on its own — hotline systems are built to receive and investigate reports cheaply precisely because false positives are considered an acceptable cost of catching real abuse, and an unsubstantiated finding does not mean a report was made in bad faith. A pattern of multiple unsubstantiated reports from the same source, recurring on a roughly annual cycle, timed to identifiable events in that source's own life, is a different kind of evidence — not proof of bad faith, but the kind of pattern that a functioning child-welfare system ought to notice and account for when weighing the credibility of the next report from the same source, rather than treating each new report as a fresh, unweighted allegation.

A separate recording, made roughly around the same period — a heated conversation in a car, in which Reardon can be heard speaking with Christi McCoy, Crowder's own attorney in the custody matter and a figure consistently placed in the advocate-only, not-a-bad-actor category — adds a specific evidentiary claim to this picture, though it deserves the same care applied to every claim that cannot be independently verified. Reardon tells McCoy directly that he has "two police reports where Crowder told deputies CPS said I couldn't see Lydia," and separately, "two recordings speaking to CPS where they flat out say she is lying and the reports have been closed out as lies." If those two specific recordings exist in the form Reardon describes them — recordings in which CPS caseworkers themselves state, on tape, that Crowder's reports were false and closed as such — they would be considerably stronger evidence than the pattern-based inference this chapter has built from the July 2020 home-visit recording alone. Those two specific recordings have not been located or independently confirmed within the available archive, a gap named plainly here rather than treated as equivalent to having reviewed them directly.

There is no indication in the available record that Mississippi's CPS system, or DeSoto County's caseworkers specifically, ever formally flagged this pattern or adjusted their handling of subsequent reports because of it. That is not necessarily evidence of misconduct on CPS's part; it may simply reflect how these systems are structurally designed to operate, treating each report as its own discrete event regardless of reporting history, a design choice that has obvious child-protection justifications (nobody wants a system that dismisses a genuine abuse report because the reporter has cried wolf before) and equally obvious costs to a parent on the receiving end of a recurring, unsubstantiated pattern.

This chapter does not resolve whether Crowder's CPS reports were made in good faith, made in bad faith, or made in the genuinely difficult psychological space that sometimes produces both at once from the same person over time. What it establishes, from a contemporaneous 2020 recording rather than from anything written in hindsight, is that Reardon and Madelyn were describing this pattern to a state investigator years before it became a chapter in a book, and that the pattern they described — three years running, three-month windows, timed to pregnancies and miscarriages — is specific enough, and consistent enough across the recording, that it belongs in this record as more than an unsupported later assertion.

The next several chapters leave the Crowder custody conflict largely behind and turn to a different, escalating front: Reardon's growing confrontations with Lafayette County law enforcement itself, beginning with a February 2021 traffic stop by Deputy Kandis Beavers that would eventually become one of the most consequential episodes in this entire account.
