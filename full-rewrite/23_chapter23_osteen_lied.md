CHAPTER 23 — OSTEEN LIED UNDER OATH

This chapter's title is not editorializing. It is, at this point in the record, a conclusion a federal magistrate judge's own findings support, and the government's own subsequent filing conceded.

Galveston Police Officer William Osteen's sworn affidavit, detailed in the previous chapter, alleged a "moderate odor of alcohol" as grounds for Reardon's August 11, 2023 felony DWI arrest. That affidavit supported roughly eleven months of pretrial detention on a $100,000 bond. A forced blood draw came back at 0.00 — a result that, on its own, would already be difficult for an affidavit alleging a "moderate odor of alcohol" to survive, since alcohol odor typically correlates with the presence of alcohol in a person's system, and this test found none.

What moves this chapter beyond a simple test-result contradiction is a specific admission buried in the government's own June 10, 2026 Reply brief in the resulting federal civil rights litigation, Reardon v. Osteen. That filing — Docket 79, paragraph 14 — concedes that Osteen himself stated on camera, at the scene, that he smelled no alcohol on Reardon. This is not Reardon's account of what happened at the traffic stop, filtered through his own memory or his own recording. It is the defendants' own attorneys, in their own brief, acknowledging what their client said into a camera at the time of the arrest: that the odor his sworn affidavit would later claim as the basis for a felony arrest was not, by his own contemporaneous on-camera statement, actually present.

An officer who says on camera, at the scene, that he smells no alcohol, and then swears in an affidavit supporting an arrest that he smelled a "moderate odor of alcohol," has given two directly contradictory accounts of the same sensory observation, made minutes apart. One of those two accounts is false. The blood test's 0.00 result makes clear which one.

The felony charge against Reardon was dismissed on May 24, 2024, nearly a year after his arrest, and recharged as a misdemeanor. A dismissal-and-recharge of this kind can happen for entirely ordinary prosecutorial reasons — evidentiary reassessment, docket management, a plea negotiation that never materialized — and not every downgraded charge is evidence of misconduct. What makes this particular dismissal worth its own scrutiny is timing relative to a specific legal mechanism: a Franks hearing, named for the Supreme Court's 1978 decision in Franks v. Delaware, is the procedural vehicle by which a defendant can challenge the truthfulness of a sworn affidavit underlying an arrest or warrant, and force the officer who signed it to defend its accuracy under direct examination. Dismissing the felony charge before that hearing could be held — and recharging a lesser offense that would not carry the same incentive to litigate the affidavit's truthfulness as aggressively — has the practical effect of allowing a false or misleading affidavit to avoid the one procedural mechanism specifically designed to test it.

It cannot be stated with certainty that avoiding a Franks hearing was the prosecution's actual motive in timing the May 2024 dismissal. What can be stated is that the effect was exactly what avoiding that hearing would produce: Osteen's affidavit was never formally tested, under oath, against his own on-camera contradiction, in the forum built for precisely that purpose. It took a separate federal civil rights lawsuit — Reardon v. Osteen, filed later, detailed fully in the next two chapters — and the government's own June 2026 concession in that separate case, to put the contradiction on the record at all.

The full Galveston police dispatch and bodycam archive for August 11, 2023 — a 46-file zip reviewed in full for this account — adds detail that sharpens this chapter's central claim rather than complicating it. A bystander captured on the recordings confirms that Reardon himself offered to take a breathalyzer test at the scene — an offer inconsistent with a man trying to conceal intoxication, and one Osteen's affidavit does not appear to have acknowledged. Separately, Galveston police personnel can be heard warning hospital staff, ahead of Reardon's arrival for the forced blood draw, that "an auditor" was coming — language that frames Reardon's identity as a First Amendment auditor and independent journalist as the operative fact driving the department's handling of him, months before the BOLO pattern detailed in Chapter 27 would formalize that same framing at the federal level. A Galveston police sergeant, identified in the available record as Hoby James, was brought into the incident specifically after watching Reardon's own livestream of the arrest — again suggesting Reardon's media activity, not any independent safety assessment, was shaping how officers on scene were responding to him.

The same dispatch audio captures Mississippi authorities, contacted during the arrest, unable to confirm on the spot whether Reardon's prior conviction status was what Galveston officers believed it to be — a detail worth flagging because it shows the arrest and detention proceeding on an unverified assumption about Reardon's record, layered on top of an affidavit already shown to be independently unreliable. An arrest built on an officer's contradicted claim of alcohol odor is one problem; an arrest additionally proceeding on cross-state record information nobody on scene could actually confirm in real time compounds it.

The recordings also include a specific rebuttal to any suggestion that Reardon was a sudden, first-time complainer whose grievance should be discounted as isolated griping: they reference Reardon having filed seventeen separate police reports over the preceding five years — a volume of documented, formal engagement with law enforcement that is difficult to square with a narrative of an erratic or unreliable complainant, whatever else one makes of the broader pattern traced across those same five years. The blood draw itself was performed at UTMB Health by a hospital employee identified in the available record as Defendant McDougle.

Sergeant Jack Doraty, present at the forced blood draw itself, occupies an uneasy position in this account, listed under "needs more context" rather than in the confirmed column, because the available record does not establish that Doraty knew, at the time, what Osteen had said on camera minutes earlier, or that Doraty had any independent duty to intervene in a blood draw a court had already authorized. What can be said is that he was present, that the draw he witnessed produced a result flatly inconsistent with the affidavit that authorized Reardon's arrest, and that nothing in the available record shows he raised that inconsistency at the time.
