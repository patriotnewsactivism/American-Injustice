CHAPTER 12 — THE DRONE AND GROWING TENSIONS

On September 29, 2021, Reardon flew a drone near the Lafayette County Justice Court. It crashed into the building. His young stepdaughter was present at the time.

This book has, up to this point, tried to reserve its harshest editorial judgment for institutional actors — sheriffs, clerks, judges, prosecutors — precisely because they hold power a private citizen does not, and because the entire argument of this book depends on holding power accountable to a higher standard than it holds itself. This chapter is one of the places where that same standard has to be applied to Reardon himself, without softening, because the alternative — quietly minimizing an episode that reflects poorly on him while dwelling at length on every institutional failure in this book — would be exactly the kind of one-sided advocacy this book has promised, from its prologue forward, not to produce.

There is no institutional misconduct to weigh against this one. A grown man flew a drone in the vicinity of a government building, with a minor present, and it crashed into that building. Whatever his intent — and Reardon's own later framing of the incident, consistent with his broader practice of documenting government facilities and monitoring the institutions he had spent years litigating against, suggests the drone was being used for the same kind of surveillance-and-accountability filming detailed elsewhere in this book — the outcome was a crash, at a courthouse, with a child nearby. That is a legitimate safety concern independent of any legal consequence that did or didn't follow, and a fair reader is entitled to weigh it as evidence that Reardon's judgment, by this point in the conflict, was not always sound when it came to balancing his own escalating campaign of documentation against the presence and safety of the children in his life.

Chief Deputy Scott Mills and Oxford Police Department personnel arrived at the scene. This book does not have independent record of what citation, if any, resulted from the incident, and it does not speculate. What is documented is the response itself — a multi-agency arrival at a drone crash near a courthouse — which is, on its face, a proportionate law enforcement reaction to an aircraft striking a government building, regardless of who was piloting it or why.

It would be easy to read this chapter as a minor episode, a single bad afternoon sandwiched between the more consequential events on either side of it in this book's timeline — the July 2021 Oxford U.S. Attorney's Office confrontation before it, the November 2021 trial after it. It earns its own chapter anyway, because of what it represents rather than what it produced: a pattern, evident by the fall of 2021, of Reardon's activities increasingly intersecting with risk to the people closest to him, not just to himself. The Facebook Live boycott in Chapter 2 risked nothing but a business relationship. The rifle purchase in Chapter 3 risked nothing on its own; it became dangerous only in the state's retroactive framing of it. By September 2021, though, the pattern has shifted. A minor stepdaughter is present at a drone crash into a courthouse. Earlier chapters in this book document Reardon coaching his young daughter Lydia about custody matters during recorded conversations at her mother's home — behavior this book's honest-reporting obligation requires it to name plainly, not bury in a footnote, alongside everything documented here.

None of this cancels out the institutional misconduct detailed in the surrounding chapters. A sheriff's department that loses exculpatory footage, a judge who won't hear a bill of sale, a prosecutor confronted on camera by a citizen he apparently took seriously enough to engage personally — none of that becomes less real or less documented because the man on the other end of those encounters also made choices, involving the children in his life, that a careful and honest reader is entitled to find troubling. Both things are true, and this chapter's job is only to make sure both are said, clearly, in the same book, rather than one being emphasized at the expense of the other.

The next chapter turns to the reckoning that these several years of escalating conflict were building toward: the November 4, 2021 trial, in which Reardon faced nine charges arising from three separate events — and in which the outcome, when it finally came, split so cleanly along institutional lines that it is difficult for this book to read as coincidence.
