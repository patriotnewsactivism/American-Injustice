CHAPTER 36 — THE UNION COUNTY THREAD

On April 15, 2026, I was recording on a public sidewalk in Union County, Mississippi, when I had a confrontation with Board of Supervisors President C. J. Bright. My account and the video record I have described from that day include Bright acknowledging that he could not lawfully stop the activity I was engaged in. That encounter became the opening event in a new series of disputes with Union County officials.

The next day, April 16, a separate case from another state reached an important disposition. The materials I later assembled for the New Orleans litigation record that the Orleans Parish District Attorney's Office refused the simple-battery charge arising from the November 12, 2025 City Hall incident under disposition code 433, described in those materials as "REFUSED — LAW ENFORCEMENT ISSUE." I have not located the underlying prosecutor-screening sheet itself in the presently assembled source set, so I am not assigning the code a broader legal meaning than the words recorded in my case materials. What matters chronologically is that the New Orleans prosecution that had produced my January arrest was no longer moving forward by mid-April.

Four days later, on April 20, the Union County Board of Supervisors matter escalated.

My account is that, shortly before public comment, a warning was announced against obscenity or vulgarity and that I was then arrested after using a profane word during my own remarks. The charge was described in my records as public profanity under Mississippi Code section 97-29-47. That sequence is central to my First Amendment objection: a speech restriction was announced at the meeting and then applied to my speech moments later.

I have also described an Mississippi Department of Corrections hold being placed on me after that arrest even though I believed no valid MDOC basis existed for it. The source set I have in front of me does not presently contain the hold document itself. More importantly, my own materials conflict on the date the hold was cleared and I was released. One account places the relevant court action on April 21; another places it on April 24. Until the actual release order or custody record settles that point, this book leaves the date unresolved rather than choosing the version that reads better.

On May 5, I appeared pro se in Union County Justice Court on the profanity matter. In my account of that hearing, when I raised the Supremacy Clause and the federal constitutional issue, the judge responded, "I don't give a rat's ass about that." I do not presently have a certified transcript of that hearing in the assembled evidence. The quotation therefore remains my recorded/reported account of the exchange, not a certified court-reporter quotation. I also recorded that I filed nine motions that day and later sought the judge's recusal on May 10. Those filings should ultimately be tied to their stamped copies in the evidence index before publication.

A second Union County arrest occurred on June 1.

That incident did not arise from the same conduct as the April 20 public-comment arrest. I carried a sign reading "FUCK CITY HALL" into the county courthouse and into the Justice Court area. My materials identify the resulting charge as disturbing the peace under Mississippi Code section 97-35-13. I had escalated the protest from speech at a board meeting to carrying an explicitly profane sign into a working courthouse. That distinction belongs in the record whether or not the charge was constitutional.

My recordings and later summaries attribute several statements during that incident to Sheriff Jimmy Edwards, including "You wanted it, you gonna get it" and "I'm gonna keep arresting you." They also record my invoking Cohen v. California and Edwards responding that he did not know the case. Until the underlying recording is indexed directly alongside this chapter, those quotations should be treated as recording-derived claims rather than certified transcript testimony.

The affidavit associated with the June 1 matter also raises documentary questions in my copy. My records describe the charge line as "Disturbance of a Business" while the narrative alleges disturbing the peace, and I have not identified a case number, docket number, notary stamp, or clerk stamp on the copy available to me. That is a description of the document I possess, not a conclusion that no properly executed charging instrument exists elsewhere.

The affidavit also describes courthouse employees as fearful of what I might be capable of doing. The version in my possession does not identify a specific threat by me. That discrepancy is fair to report. It is not fair to convert it automatically into proof that the affiant fabricated the fear, because the current record does not establish the witness interviews behind that language.

My phone was seized during the June 1 encounter. My materials also place Mark McClinton at the courthouse that day. I have not established from primary records whether this is the same Mark McClinton who appears in a separate 2022 call, and I am not merging those identities without proof. The name "Chad Mood" also appears in my materials from this period, but I cannot yet place his role with enough confidence to narrate it as fact.

Three days later, on June 4, I went to the Thomas G. Abernethy Federal Building in Aberdeen, Mississippi. I had prepared a federal civil-rights complaint and related papers concerning the Union County events and intended to file them there.

My account of the entrance encounter is that security demanded photo identification, that I resisted the demand, and that the interaction lasted roughly twenty minutes while personnel waited on an individual identified to me only as "Mr. Lipshutz." I then asked whether refusing to provide identification would send me to jail. My records attribute the response "Probably" to an officer outside. I ultimately identified myself and was arrested by Aberdeen Police Chief D. Shumpert after building security reported that I had caused a disturbance.

The arrest, vehicle search, towing, and phone seizure are events I have described consistently in my materials. The resulting matter proceeded in municipal court rather than as a federal prosecution. That jurisdictional fact is worth recording, but the earlier draft went too far in treating it as evidence of a deliberate plan to keep the case out of federal court. I do not have proof of that motive.

I also need to be exact about the civil complaint I had intended to file. The June 4 arrest prevented me from filing it at the Abernethy building that day. As of the source set supporting this chapter, I have not identified a later stamped federal filing of that particular complaint. I therefore describe it as a prepared complaint whose filing was interrupted, not as a complaint the federal court rejected.

On June 6, I published my account of the Aberdeen incident. My records then reflect that on June 7 an Aberdeen Police captain identified as "SRQ" Lee forwarded the article to colleagues with the message, "Watch the video with us in it. Lol." That text is potentially significant because it is contemporaneous documentary evidence of how at least one officer reacted to the published account. It does not, standing alone, establish the culture or motive of an entire department.

By early June, three distinct proceedings were moving at once: the remanded Lafayette County misdemeanor appeal, the Union County cases, and the Aberdeen municipal matter. The next chapter returns to the Texas federal civil case against Officer William Osteen, where the federal magistrate's July 2026 recommendation contained one of the most important adverse findings about Osteen anywhere in the record—and, at the same time, recommended dismissal of my case.
