CHAPTER 22 — RELEASE, AND GALVESTON

I was released from Mississippi custody on August 1, 2023, after the roughly year-long detention detailed in the previous chapter — a detention grounded in testimony from Deputy Kandis Beavers that a recorded phone call contradicts. Three days later, on August 4, 2023, I called the Lafayette County Sheriff's Department and tried to do something about it: I spoke with Chief Deputy Scott Mills, attempting to file an internal affairs complaint against Beavers for perjury, explicitly citing the recorded call in which she had prior knowledge of my FBI visit that her later testimony contradicted. Mills declined to accept the complaint over the phone and insisted I file it in person — a requirement I refused, for the plain and reasonable reason that a department investigating its own deputy's alleged perjury through its own walk-in complaint process is not a neutral process, a concern already documented playing out in a structurally similar form in the Metro Narcotics scandal, where Sheriff East was both a signatory to a missing-funds lockbox and one of the officials charged with investigating its own shortfall.

Six days after that call, on August 11, 2023, I was arrested in Galveston, Texas, on a felony DWI charge.

This chapter requires the same discipline applied throughout, whenever my own theories about coordination between distant officials and jurisdictions meet the documentary record — separating what the record actually supports from what I believe it supports. The temptation to read a six-day gap between a stonewalled perjury complaint and an out-of-state arrest as proof of retaliation is exactly the kind of inferential leap I've repeatedly declined to make on thinner evidence elsewhere. The plain, verifiable facts are these: I tried to report a Lafayette County deputy for perjury on August 4; that attempt went nowhere because the department that employed the deputy would not accept the complaint by any means other than an in-person visit I had specific, articulable reasons to distrust; and six days later, in a different state, on unrelated facts, I was arrested by a different agency on a felony charge. There is no documented evidence of communication between Lafayette County, Mississippi and Galveston, Texas law enforcement connecting these two events. I believe there is a connection — I have said so explicitly, and that belief is recorded plainly here, labeled as my own theory, because being honest means not silently omitting what I believe even when I can't verify it. The label applied, at my own request and in my own words, is "institutional coverup followed by institutional retaliation" — coverup meaning Mills's refusal to accept the Beavers complaint through any channel that didn't require me to walk back into the building housing the officers I was accusing, and retaliation meaning what followed six days later. That causal claim is flagged explicitly here: the tight timing between the two events is a documented, verifiable fact; that one caused the other is not something the available record can establish.

What can be established, independent of any theory about coordination, is what happened in Galveston itself, and it is serious enough to stand entirely on its own. Galveston Police Officer William Osteen's affidavit in support of my arrest alleged a "moderate odor of alcohol." On the strength of that affidavit, I was held for approximately eleven months on a $100,000 bond. A forced blood draw, conducted with Sergeant Jack Doraty present and performed by UTMB Health staff including a technician named McDougle, came back at 0.00 blood alcohol content.

Zero. Not below the legal limit — zero, the number a person with no alcohol whatsoever in their system produces. An affidavit alleging a "moderate odor of alcohol" supported an arrest and eleven months of pretrial detention on a $100,000 bond, and the actual physiological test the state itself administered found nothing. That is not inference or theory. That is the documented outcome of a state-administered forensic test, measured against a sworn affidavit that formed the basis for nearly a year of my confinement.

The felony charge was ultimately dismissed on May 24, 2024, and recharged as a misdemeanor — a downgrade the following chapters will show was not a routine prosecutorial mercy but, according to a federal magistrate's own later findings, a deliberate maneuver to avoid a Franks hearing that would have directly tested Osteen's affidavit's truthfulness under oath. That finding, and the on-camera admission buried in the government's own later court filings that would eventually confirm what the blood test had already suggested, is the subject of the next chapter.
