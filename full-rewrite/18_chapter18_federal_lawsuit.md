CHAPTER 18 — THE FEDERAL LAWSUIT

On April 6, 2022, I filed a federal civil-rights action in the United States District Court for the Northern District of Mississippi: **Reardon v. Lafayette County, Mississippi, et al., No. 3:22-cv-00050-SA-JMV.** I filed it pro se.

The lawsuit was the first time I tried to place the December and February commitment proceedings, the courthouse dispute, the Sheriff's Department, Communicare, and the associated county officials into one federal case. It was also the first time a federal judge would have to decide not merely whether I believed the events were connected, but whether my pleading stated claims federal law would recognize against the people and entities I had sued.

On June 13, 2022, I filed a First Amended Complaint. It was seventy-four pages long.

The caption named the State of Mississippi; Lafayette County; the Lafayette County Chancery Court; Communicare; the Mississippi Department of Mental Health; the Lafayette County Sheriff's Department; Sheriff Joseph B. East; Dr. Sandy Rogers; Rachel Alcorn; Susan Beard; Chancery Clerk Sherry Wall; Circuit Clerk Jeff Busby; and county attorney David O'Donnell. I invoked 42 U.S.C. §§ 1983, 1985, 1986 and 1988, along with state-law claims. I alleged violations of the First, Fourth, Fifth, and Fourteenth Amendments and argued that Mississippi's mental-health commitment framework had been exploited to deprive me of liberty and retaliate against protected speech and petitioning activity.

The amended complaint is useful evidence of what I believed and what I was alleging in June 2022. It is not evidence that a court had found those allegations true. That distinction became important when the court later summarized the case. Judge Sharion Aycock's memorandum opinion repeatedly framed the factual background as what was alleged **"according to his Amended Complaint."** The court was recounting my pleading, not certifying my narrative as judicial fact.

The complaint also shows a weakness that would become central to the outcome: I tried to litigate almost the entire institutional history at once.

I had years of events, different legal actors, multiple courts, two commitment proceedings, several constitutional theories, state-law tort claims, and an attack on the statutory commitment framework itself. Instead of separating those claims into the narrowest possible legal units, I put them together in a sprawling pro se pleading. The district court later described the seventy-four-page complaint as **"undeniably convoluted and difficult to follow."** That is the court's language, and there is no value in hiding it.

The court also noted that I repeated substantially the same constitutional counts against groups of defendants rather than tying every theory cleanly to the conduct and legal status of each person. What felt to me like documenting a pattern looked, in federal pleading terms, like an overbroad complaint that made it difficult to determine which claim belonged to which defendant and why.

The defendants then raised the defenses federal civil-rights plaintiffs routinely have to overcome, and the court ruled against me on them.

The State of Mississippi and the Mississippi Department of Mental Health obtained dismissal under the Eleventh Amendment. That meant the federal claims against those state defendants could not proceed in the form I had pleaded them.

The individual defendants asserted qualified immunity. Judge Aycock's opinion explained that once they did so, the burden shifted to me to identify clearly established law showing that the particular conduct I alleged violated a constitutional right under sufficiently established precedent. The court concluded I had not carried that burden.

That ruling matters because it is more precise than saying the court simply decided none of the underlying events happened. It did not. The qualified-immunity analysis asked whether my complaint and legal authorities were sufficient to overcome immunity as to the individual defendants. The court said they were not.

The opinion also addressed my theory that the commitment itself constituted an unconstitutional seizure. In analyzing that claim, the court pointed to something my own amended complaint acknowledged: I had been taken into custody pursuant to a writ signed through the chancery commitment process. The existence of that judicial process mattered to the Fourth Amendment analysis. The court did not accept my position that the surrounding allegations were enough to turn the resulting custody into a cognizable federal false-arrest claim against the individual defendants I had named.

The court was equally direct about the larger theory behind the case. Near the end of the opinion, Judge Aycock wrote that my grievance appeared to rest, in significant part, on my belief that Mississippi's statutory involuntary-commitment scheme was unconstitutional. I had indeed made that argument. The First Amended Complaint expressly asked the federal court to treat the state's mental-health laws as constitutionally defective and to overhaul or invalidate them. The district court did not accept that theory as I presented it.

My state-law claims failed for a separate procedural reason. The opinion held that I had not satisfied the Mississippi Tort Claims Act's pre-suit notice requirement. Whatever factual merit I believed those tort theories had, the federal court concluded I had not completed a statutory prerequisite to bringing them against the governmental defendants.

The result was a decisive loss. On June 28, 2023, Judge Aycock entered the memorandum opinion and order disposing of the case against me. The federal action ended without a trial on the factual theory I had wanted a jury to hear.

That outcome has to be stated without qualification before I say what I think it means.

I lost the case.

I did not lose because a jury heard testimony from Alcorn, Beard, East, Busby, the deputies, and me and decided whose account was true. I lost at the legal stage where the court evaluated immunity, pleading sufficiency, the existence of the chancery writ, sovereign immunity, the structure of the constitutional claims, and state-law notice requirements. That distinction does not make the judgment less real. It defines what the judgment actually decided.

The opinion also forces an admission about my own lawyering. The complaint was too broad. It mixed allegations with legal conclusions. It treated repeated events as though identifying a pattern automatically solved defendant-specific pleading problems. It assumed that if I could show enough troubling facts, a federal court would necessarily let me conduct discovery into all of them. Federal civil-rights litigation does not work that way, and Judge Aycock's description of the complaint as convoluted was not invented by an adversary; it was the assessment of the judge deciding the case.

None of that means the source documents underlying the lawsuit cease to exist. Alcorn's recorded statement that local law enforcement engaged Communicare still exists. Beard's February noncompliance affidavit still exists. The December commitment file still exists. The recorded December 6 encounter with East still exists. But the federal judgment means I cannot responsibly describe the 2022 lawsuit as a judicial vindication of my interpretation of those documents. It was the opposite: an adverse judgment rejecting the federal case I built from them.

The timing of the lawsuit also matters to the chronology that follows. The federal case was still new when another Mississippi criminal investigation began.

On **June 20, 2022**, I went into Oxford City Hall with a camera. The video later relied upon by investigators showed me entering the building, going upstairs, approaching the mayor's office, and attempting the office doorknob.

On **June 23**, Oxford Police referred the matter to the Mississippi Bureau of Investigation.

On **June 27**, MBI Lieutenant Joey Mauney prepared an aggravated-stalking warrant, swore to it by FaceTime, and Circuit Judge Kent Smith signed it.

On **June 28**, deputies served that already-existing MBI warrant when I arrived at Lafayette County Chancery Court for a family-court matter.

That is the order. The Chancery Court visit was where I was arrested. It was not the conduct that generated the warrant.

The federal lawsuit and the new criminal case were now moving at the same time. The next chapter turns to the second aggravated-stalking prosecution and the evidence MBI actually used to obtain the warrant.
