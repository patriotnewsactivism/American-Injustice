CHAPTER 16 — A SON BORN IN CONFINEMENT

On February 9, 2022, a second involuntary commitment affidavit was signed against Reardon, this one by Susan Beard rather than Sheriff Joey East. Eight days later, on February 17, 2022, Reardon's son was born by emergency cesarean section — while Reardon himself remained confined under that commitment.

This chapter is short, and it should be, because some facts do not need three thousand words of context to land with their full weight. A man was not present for the emergency birth of his son because the state had him committed to a psychiatric facility at the time. Whatever the medical or legal justification for that commitment was — Susan Beard's underlying affidavit is not in the available record, so its clinical basis cannot be evaluated directly — the practical result was that Reardon missed the birth of his own child under emergency surgical circumstances, confined by the same state apparatus that, by this point in the timeline, had already committed him once before, in December 2021, within twenty-four hours of a confrontation over a judicial irregularity.

Two commitments in three months, both preceding major family or legal events, is the kind of pattern that should not be overread. Commitments happen for real reasons, and Reardon's own documented conduct in this period — the confrontations, the escalating filings, the drone incident with a minor present, the pattern traced honestly across Part II — is not the conduct of a man in a settled or entirely stable place in his life. A reader sympathetic to the institutions in this story could reasonably argue that a second commitment within three months of the first is exactly what one would expect if the underlying concern behind the first commitment had never actually been resolved.

A reader sympathetic to Reardon could just as reasonably note that the second commitment landed, again, at a moment of maximum consequence — this time missing the birth of a son — and ask why an apparatus this attentive to Reardon's mental state showed no comparable urgency about the accumulating record of institutional irregularities he had spent years trying to get anyone in a position of authority to take seriously: the twelve-day rifle impossibility, the duress-signed order, the missing FBI form, the unexplained judge swap, the sheriff's own contradictory sworn testimony about video preservation.

Both readings are available from this record, and no attempt is made here to resolve which one is correct. What can be said plainly is the human cost, independent of who was right about the underlying clinical judgment: a son born by emergency C-section, and a father who was not there.

The next chapter turns to what Reardon did with the time and legal standing he had once he was no longer confined: on April 6, 2022, he filed his first federal civil rights lawsuit, pro se, against Lafayette County and the officials who had spent the previous five years building the case documented chapter by chapter.
