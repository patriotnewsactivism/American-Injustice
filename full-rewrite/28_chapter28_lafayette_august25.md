CHAPTER 28 — LAFAYETTE: AUGUST 25

On August 25, 2025, Reardon was arrested at the John M. Shaw United States Courthouse in Lafayette, Louisiana — the same building where the two-month surveillance pattern detailed in the previous chapter had begun. He was charged with violating 41 CFR § 102-74.390(b), a federal regulation governing conduct on General Services Administration property, arrested by Deputy U.S. Marshal Hayden Nugent. It was his fourth documented visit to that courthouse.

This chapter requires the same discipline this book has applied to every other episode in Reardon's life where institutional overreach and his own escalating conduct sit side by side, and nowhere in this manuscript is that discipline more necessary than here, because the trial record — detailed fully in the next chapter — establishes plainly that Reardon's own actions that day went beyond simply recording in a public lobby. The trial record shows Reardon left a tripod positioned in a way that blocked an emergency exit. It shows crude, confrontational language directed at Deputy Marshal Nugent. And it shows Reardon's own livestream, captured in his own words, stating "now they just asked for it" — a phrase this book cannot read charitably. Whatever grievance Reardon believed he was documenting or provoking that day, a livestream in which the subject himself frames an escalating confrontation as something the government "asked for" is not the language of a passive documentarian caught up in an overreaching arrest. It is the language of a man who understood, in the moment, that he was pushing toward a confrontation, and who said so on tape.

This book will not soften that record to make its broader argument about selective enforcement land more cleanly. Both things are true at once, and this chapter's job, like so many before it in this book, is to hold them together rather than pick one. A tripod blocking an emergency exit at a federal courthouse is a legitimate security concern, independent of any question about the government's prior surveillance of Reardon. Crude language directed at a federal officer is not itself a crime, but it is not admirable conduct, and this book does not pretend otherwise. And a defendant's own recorded words framing a confrontation as something he believed the government had earned — "asked for" — undercuts any simple narrative in which Reardon is purely a victim of a government campaign, with no agency of his own in how the August 25 encounter unfolded.

What those facts do not undercut, and cannot undercut, is the two-month documentary record established in the previous chapter. A defendant's poor judgment in the moment of an arrest — a badly placed tripod, ugly language, an ill-advised livestream caption — does not retroactively justify, or even meaningfully address, the question of why federal law enforcement across two states had already spent two months circulating his photograph, labeling him a "First Amendment auditor," and predicting his next move before this specific incident ever occurred. Those are two separate questions, resolved by two separate bodies of evidence, and this book's honest-reporting commitment requires answering both rather than letting one crowd out the other: Reardon's own conduct that day was not blameless, and the government's response to him was shaped by a surveillance apparatus built and deployed against him well before August 25 gave it a specific incident to act on.

It is worth noting one more parallel this book flagged much earlier, in Chapter 11: the basic shape of what happened at this Lafayette courthouse in August 2025 — a federal building, a camera, an escalating confrontation with federal personnel who chose engagement over de-escalation — had already played out once before, at the Oxford U.S. Attorney's Office in July 2021, four years earlier and four hundred miles away, with lower stakes and no arrest. That earlier parallel does not prove a coordinated multi-year campaign against Reardon specifically. It does establish that this pattern of confrontation — one this book has now traced across two states and eight years — was not new to either side of these encounters by 2025.

Reardon was found guilty following a bench trial roughly four months later. The next chapter covers that trial, the verdict, and the spoliation issue this book's own analysis treats as the strongest available ground for appeal.
