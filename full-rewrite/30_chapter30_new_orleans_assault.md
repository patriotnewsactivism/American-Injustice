CHAPTER 30 — THE NEW ORLEANS ASSAULT

On November 12, 2025, while the federal courthouse case detailed in the previous two chapters was still pending, Reardon was struck in the head with a metal flashlight by Allied Universal security guard Jerome Ard, at New Orleans City Hall. The blow split his head open. He was transported to Tulane Medical Center. His phone was destroyed in the encounter — a detail worth flagging without editorializing, given how central Reardon's own recordings have been to nearly every other chapter of vindication in this manuscript.

What happened afterward is where this chapter's real weight sits, because it establishes a pattern now visible, in different forms, across multiple states and multiple years: an official account constructed to protect the person who inflicted harm, rather than the person who suffered it. NOPD Officer Junious Grady swore out the affidavit supporting an arrest — not of Ard, the man who struck Reardon with a flashlight hard enough to split his scalp, but of Reardon himself. That affidavit relied on statements from Ard's own Allied Universal coworkers, identified in the available record as Vidal and Savage — colleagues of the man who inflicted the injury, not independent witnesses. A separate NOPD officer, Victor Paz, badge number 26091, declared in his own report that Reardon was "the aggressor." The warrant against Reardon was signed the same day, at 3:34 PM, by Judge Juana Marine Lombard.

The city's own Real Time Crime Center cameras — the network of surveillance infrastructure New Orleans maintains specifically to monitor incidents like this one — did not capture the actual altercation. This book does not know why. It does not have evidence establishing that this gap in coverage was deliberate, and it will not speculate that it was. What it can say is that the same city that operates an extensive real-time camera network happened not to have footage of the specific moment a security guard struck a man hard enough to require hospital transport, and that the absence of that footage left the incident to be adjudicated almost entirely on the word of the guard's own coworkers and the officers who took their statements at face value.

Jerome Ard, the man who wielded the flashlight, was never charged with anything.

This book has, across twenty-nine prior chapters, tried consistently to hold institutional misconduct to a standard independent of whether Reardon is a sympathetic figure in any given episode — and this chapter is one of the clearest examples in the entire manuscript of why that standard matters. Whatever a reader thinks of Reardon's broader pattern of confrontational First Amendment auditing, documented honestly across the account of Oxford, Lafayette, and Aberdeen, nothing in that pattern justifies a security guard striking him hard enough to split his head open with a metal object, and nothing in that pattern explains why the resulting legal process treated the injured party as the aggressor while the man who caused the injury walked away with no charges at all. A split scalp, a hospital transport, a destroyed phone, and an arrest warrant for the victim rather than the assailant — sworn out by an officer relying on the assailant's own coworkers as witnesses — is, on its face, one of the starkest examples of institutional failure documented here, independent of anything else book says about Reardon's own conduct elsewhere.
