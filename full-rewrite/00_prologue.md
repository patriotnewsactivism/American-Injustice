PROLOGUE — THE FIRST DOMINO

Before Oxford. Before the flag. Before any of the names in this book had reason to know mine, there was the Marine Corps.

I entered the Corps in 2008 and went through the East Coast training pipeline from Parris Island to Marine Combat Training at Camp Lejeune, North Carolina. I was twenty years old. Whatever happened to me later, I have never stopped being proud that I earned the title Marine.

In May 2008, while I was at Marine Combat Training, a young Marine named Bradley Foust died at School of Infantry East.

I did not know Foust personally. That matters. It would be easy to make the opening of this book more intimate by turning him into a friend, but he was not. We were Marines in the same training environment, moving through a place built to teach people quickly and send them onward.

My own account, repeated years later on recordings, is that I was on roving post that night and experienced the incident as a suicide involving an M16 fired on three-round burst. I have described it publicly as a gruesome suicide and as the event that stayed with me afterward. I am identifying that as my recollection because the official records I later obtained do not themselves use the word "suicide."

There is independent corroboration for the core event from another Marine who was there. In a recorded 2025 conversation with me, Jay Andrews described hearing a three-round burst at Camp Devil Dog that night. Andrews later provided a written account stating that he was questioned by NCIS and that, the following morning, a Marine sergeant showed him the scene and told him that Foust had put the rifle on three-round burst and killed himself. Andrews's account is his own eyewitness account. It does not turn every detail of my memory into an official finding, but it matters because it exists independently of me.

The Marine Corps casualty material I later obtained identifies Foust as an active-duty, nonhostile death at School of Infantry East. Public accounts I found over the years described his death as a training accident. That mismatch — between what I experienced, what Andrews separately described, and the way the death was publicly characterized — is what kept pulling me back to the records.

I need to correct one claim from an earlier draft of this book. I previously wrote that a suicide classification would necessarily have cost Foust's family death benefits and that an accident classification preserved them. That was a theory I held and discussed on recordings; I do not have a source in this record sufficient to state it as fact. It is not necessary to the point I can document, so I am not going to use it as an explanation for why the death was characterized the way it was.

What I can document is a change in Marine Corps sentry procedure after Foust's death.

The preexisting School of Infantry East order dated December 14, 2007 allowed students to serve as armed guards or sentries at the ammunition-storage area and Camp Devil Dog. It required the armed sentry to carry an M16A2 or M9 with security ammunition and prescribed how that ammunition was to be controlled.

A later version of that same order, dated August 25, 2008, changed the rule for entry-level students. It states that student guards or sentries "may not be issued security ammunition" and requires armed permanent personnel to support the post in the immediate vicinity.

An earlier draft of this book said that change was made in May 2008, the same month Foust died. The document in my possession is dated August 25, 2008. That is the date I can prove, and that is the date this book will use.

The policy change does not contain a sentence saying, "This was changed because Bradley Foust committed suicide." I infer a connection because the change followed his death at that training command and directly removed live security ammunition from entry-level student sentries. Jay Andrews independently drew the same inference in our recorded conversation. But an inference, even a strong one, is not the same thing as an official admission. I will not write it as one.

The incident nevertheless became part of my own life. I have repeatedly said that I began using alcohol to manage what I experienced and that the Marine Corps later discharged me early after alcohol-rehabilitation failure. I have also said, repeatedly and publicly, that I stopped drinking in 2018. Those are facts about my own conduct and my own understanding of it. I am not asking a military record to diagnose the reason I drank; I am telling the reader how I understood the connection.

Years later, in 2017, I went back to the Marine Corps through the Freedom of Information Act. One of my requests sought the School of Infantry/MCT standard operating procedures. The records I obtained gave me something memory could not: a before-and-after paper trail showing that entry-level student sentries had been allowed to carry security ammunition before Foust's death and, by August 25, 2008, were expressly prohibited from being issued it.

That paper trail is why the episode belongs at the front of this book.

It taught me a habit that would later become obsessive: compare what people say happened with what the documents actually say; compare the first version of a record with the later version; and pay attention to the places where a rule changes after an event even when nobody explains the connection in writing.

I have reached out to Foust's family over the years. I have tried not to treat their loss as material that belongs to me. I do not know what the Marine Corps told them privately, and I will not claim that I do. I also do not know whether they agree with my interpretation of the records. Their grief is theirs. The part that belongs in this memoir is what I experienced, what another Marine later described independently, and what the documents I obtained show.

I also cannot prove a formal chain connecting my 2017 Marine Corps records requests to the Mississippi events that followed. I once spent more time entertaining that possibility than the evidence justified. I am not going to turn timing into coordination without proof. The fact that my conflict with Mississippi institutions intensified in the same period is chronology. It is not, by itself, evidence that the Marine Corps, the FBI, and local Mississippi officials were acting together.

That distinction — between chronology and causation — will matter again and again in the pages ahead.

The chapters that follow contain records that are much more concrete: a bill of sale with a date; police narratives; court orders; recorded calls; sworn testimony; an MBI warrant file; a federal magistrate judge's written statement that a Galveston officer "lied to Judge Tollison"; and appellate rulings that sometimes went against me and sometimes corrected what had happened below.

Those later records are stronger because they do not require the reader to accept my interpretation of a policy change from 2008. They can be read directly.

But this is where I first learned why I would spend years asking for them.

Not on the Oxford Square. Not in a Mississippi courtroom. At Marine Combat Training, after the death of a Marine I did not know personally, when what I remembered and what the surviving public record appeared to say did not line up.

What follows begins years later, with my family, a custody case, a state flag, and the first arrest that put my own name into a government file I would spend the next decade reading back to them.
