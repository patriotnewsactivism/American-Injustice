CHAPTER 23 — RELEASE, AND GALVESTON

I walked out of Mississippi custody on August 1, 2023. I had been jailed since November 1, 2022 — roughly nine months — after the first revocation of my 2022 suspended sentence. The written revocation sentence was one year, but I did not serve a full calendar year before release. That distinction matters because this book cannot turn a sentence into time actually served when the dates say otherwise.

Three days after my release, on August 4, I called the Lafayette County Sheriff's Department. I asked to make a complaint about Deputy Kandi Beavers's testimony at the November 2022 revocation hearing. I had the October 28 recording by then, and I believed it showed that the Sheriff's Department had been told in advance that I intended to go to the FBI office — contrary to the characterization later placed before Judge Tollison that I had lied about having an appointment.

The call with Chief Deputy Scott Mills is recorded. Four sentences into my explanation he interrupted me: *"Hold up, hold up, hold up."* He then said, *"I'm not sitting here listening to you,"* and told me that if I wanted to make a complaint I could come to the Sheriff's Department and file it in person.

I refused. By that point my dealings with the department had included arrests, prosecution, civil-commitment involvement, and the revocation that had just kept me incarcerated for months. I was not willing to walk back into the building simply to hand over evidence concerning one of its own deputies. That was my decision and my assessment of the risk. I do not need to convert that judgment into a claim about what the department intended.

I also published the recording. I put the October 28 call beside the revocation testimony so that viewers could hear what had actually been said and compare it with the later sworn record. I have described the audience at different times as roughly forty to fifty thousand views within about a week. I do not presently have the platform analytics in the evidentiary record, so I am not fixing an exact number here. What I can establish is the sequence: release on August 1; complaint call on August 4; publication of the recording; then Texas.

Seven days after the call with Mills, I drove to Galveston. My purpose, as I have consistently described it, was journalistic. I had received information concerning allegations of racial profiling there and was going to meet other journalists and look into it. I had been on the road most of August 10 and reached Galveston in the early morning hours of August 11.

The first correction is basic but important: **Officer William Osteen did not initiate the traffic stop. Officer Larry Murph did.** The Galveston records place the stop at approximately 1:52 a.m. Osteen arrived afterward to assist.

That matters because Osteen later wrote the affidavit used to obtain the warrant for my blood even though Murph was the officer who had observed the driving that preceded the stop.

The second correction is about the interstate calls.

At approximately **2:41 a.m.**, a caller identified himself to Galveston as Hoby James of the Lafayette County Sheriff's Office in Mississippi. He said he had watched my arrest unfold on a livestream. A few minutes later he called again and identified himself as Sergeant James.

At approximately **3:44 a.m.**, Galveston personnel were recorded discussing the prior convictions that would determine whether the new DWI allegation could be charged as a felony. One officer said that if there was a qualifying prior conviction and Lafayette County could provide the conviction date, cause number, and court information, *"this will be a felony."* The same recording reflects difficulty locating the Mississippi conviction in the information they were reviewing.

At approximately **3:46 a.m.**, Galveston called Lafayette County seeking that information. James said he did not have access to it on his shift and could leave a note for someone to respond later that morning. The Galveston dispatcher stated that the information was being sought so they could *"call the DA."*

At approximately **4:04 a.m.**, another caller identified herself as Deputy Beavers of the Lafayette County Sheriff's Department and asked to be connected with the Galveston officer who had been dealing with me. The dispatcher asked whether she meant Officer Osteen. Beavers said yes. When the dispatcher reached Osteen and asked whether he had just been speaking with a woman from Mississippi, Osteen answered, *"Yes, I was."*

Those recordings establish communication. They do not establish what Beavers said to Osteen during the conversation that followed, and I do not know what she told him. I am not going to write content into a call I do not possess.

What the recordings do establish is narrower and still important: within hours of my arrival in Galveston, Lafayette County personnel were communicating with Galveston Police while Galveston personnel were working to identify the prior convictions necessary to determine the level of the DWI charge.

The traffic-stop and warrant records then create a separate evidentiary problem.

Osteen's search-warrant affidavit described signs he associated with intoxication. Among them, he marked a **moderate odor of an alcoholic beverage on my breath**, described my speech as **slurred and thick-tongued**, and described my eyes as **watery and dilated**. He also treated my refusal of the requested blood specimen as evidence supporting the warrant.

I had repeatedly asked for a breath test. The recordings and later defense filings became important precisely because my willingness to take a breath test does not fit neatly with an affidavit framed around refusal evidence.

A magistrate signed the blood warrant at approximately **2:49 a.m.** I was then taken to UTMB, where officers restrained me while medical personnel drew my blood. I have consistently described the blood draw as forced. The records identify UTMB personnel involved in the procedure, and later litigation named a medical technician identified as McDougle.

The toxicology results require precision because earlier drafts of this book overstated what they proved.

The Texas laboratory report states **"No alcohol detected."** That is directly relevant to Osteen's sworn assertion that he detected a moderate odor of an alcoholic beverage on my breath. It does not, however, end the entire toxicology inquiry.

A separate confirmation analysis reported **methamphetamine at 0.13 milligrams per liter**. The report did not separately state an amphetamine concentration. The broader amphetamines screen had been referred for further analysis, and the confirmation result identified methamphetamine at 0.13 mg/L.

Campagna and I disputed that isolated result. From the beginning, our position was that the trace result was consistent with a false positive rather than proof that I was impaired. I continue to maintain that position. But I am labeling it correctly here: it is our asserted interpretation of the laboratory evidence, not a scientific finding made by the laboratory or a court.

There is a second, independently documented limitation that does not depend on accepting my interpretation. When Mississippi later used the 0.13 mg/L result at my July 2024 revocation hearing, the State presented **no expert testimony or other evidence establishing that 0.13 mg/L would impair my ability to operate a motor vehicle.** That point appears expressly in the later post-conviction filing prepared by the MacArthur Justice Center.

So the toxicology record says three things that must be kept separate.

First, **no alcohol was detected**.

Second, **0.13 mg/L of methamphetamine was reported** in the confirmation analysis.

Third, **the revocation record contained no evidence establishing that this concentration caused impairment**.

The manuscript previously collapsed those three propositions into a much simpler claim: that the laboratory proved I was completely sober. The record does not support that wording. The laboratory conclusively undercuts an alcohol-based theory, but the methamphetamine result existed and must be confronted rather than omitted.

The contradictions in Osteen's descriptions became more pronounced later. In the warrant affidavit he described my pupils as dilated and my speech as slurred and thick-tongued. At the July 2024 Mississippi revocation hearing he described my pupils as very small or pinpoint and described me as talking extremely fast. He also testified that once I was outside the vehicle he could not detect an alcoholic odor coming from me.

Those are not my paraphrases of what I think he meant. They are materially different descriptions in records created at different points in the case.

The criminal case did not end when the laboratory report returned. The felony DWI prosecution remained pending until **May 24, 2024**, when the felony charge was dismissed. The later Mississippi record reflects that Osteen understood a misdemeanor matter to remain pending, but the precise Texas procedural path after the felony dismissal belongs in the next chapter and must be described from the actual Texas docket rather than inferred from shorthand testimony.

The bond history also requires care. Osteen's report reflects a recommended bond of **$10,000** on the August 11 arrest. I bonded out several days later. I was then rearrested on August 17 after Lafayette County moved on the probation-revocation matter. The Texas bond was later set at **$100,000**, while the Mississippi hold operated independently and prevented release regardless of the Texas amount.

That means the approximately eleven months I ultimately spent confined after the Galveston arrest cannot truthfully be attributed to one affidavit, one bond, or one jurisdiction. The Texas prosecution, the Mississippi revocation warrant, interstate rendition, and the later Mississippi proceedings overlapped. The chronology is damaging enough without pretending all of that custody had a single legal cause.

What I can say about August 11 without qualification is this: I arrived in Galveston in the early morning; Murph stopped me; Osteen took over the DWI investigation; Lafayette County officers communicated with Galveston during the same early-morning window; Osteen obtained a blood warrant containing specific sworn observations; the laboratory later reported no alcohol and 0.13 mg/L methamphetamine; and those records would eventually be placed beside Osteen's later sworn testimony.

That comparison is the subject of the next chapter.
