CHAPTER 29 — TRIAL AND VERDICT

Reardon's bench trial on the August 25, 2025 courthouse charge was held December 15 and 16, 2025, before Judge LeBlanc. The verdict came down January 16, 2026: guilty. On July 16, 2026, a First Amendment motion to dismiss was denied, the court characterizing the courthouse lobby as a "nonpublic forum" — a legal classification that substantially narrows the constitutional protection available to speech and recording activity conducted there, regardless of how that activity might be protected on a public sidewalk or in a traditional public forum.

This book does not dispute that a federal courthouse lobby can, as a matter of settled First Amendment doctrine, be treated as a nonpublic forum, subject to reasonable, content-neutral restrictions the government would not be permitted to impose on a public street. That doctrine exists for legitimate reasons — courthouses are working government facilities with security concerns a public park does not share — and this book is not equipped to say, as a matter of pure legal analysis, that Judge LeBlanc's classification was wrong. What this book can do is lay out the strongest available challenge to the verdict that follows from that classification, a challenge grounded not in the strength of Reardon's First Amendment claim on its own terms, but in a due-process and evidentiary problem that would matter regardless of how the forum question is resolved.

Two events in the run-up to trial deserve their own place in this record, because both bear directly on how seriously the pre-arrest surveillance detailed in Chapter 27 should be taken by anyone evaluating this case. On October 15, 2025, the government filed a protective order motion seeking to restrict communications between U.S. Marshals and court staff regarding Reardon going forward. This book flags the framing of that motion carefully: it did not deny that such communications had occurred — the BOLO record and the June 2025 emails detailed in Chapter 27 make any such denial untenable — it sought only to restrict them prospectively. A motion to restrict future marshal-judge communications about a defendant, filed by the same government that had already generated the surveillance record this book has documented, reads less like a denial that a problem existed and more like an implicit acknowledgment that the existing pattern of communication needed to stop.

Then, on November 12, 2025, Magistrate Judge Carol B. Whitehurst recused herself from the case, citing an appearance of bias. This book does not have, in the available record, the specific factual basis Judge Whitehurst cited for that recusal, and it will not speculate about what she knew or observed that led to it. What it can say is that a sitting federal magistrate judge, presiding over this exact case, concluded independently that the appearance of bias was serious enough to warrant stepping aside — a judicial determination that arrived entirely independent of anything this book argues, and one that sits uncomfortably close in time to the protective-order motion the government itself had just filed a month earlier. Two separate signals, a month apart, both suggesting that whatever had happened between marshals and court staff regarding Reardon was serious enough to require formal institutional correction — arriving well before the bench trial itself, detailed below, had even begun.

The sealed trial record — portions of which this book can discuss and portions of which remain under a gag order Reardon is complying with through his August 7, 2026 sentencing, per his own explicit decision to challenge that order through a post-sentencing motion to unseal rather than risk contempt by speaking now — establishes that the government admitted it failed to preserve all of the courthouse lobby's surveillance footage from August 25, 2025. The defense's argument, based on the footage that does survive and on testimony given at trial that this book will describe only in the terms already made public through the court record, is that the missing footage would have shown federal marshals actively waving Reardon toward the specific door he was later convicted of obstructing — footage that, if it exists in the form the defense contends, would transform the entire character of the encounter from a defendant obstructing a court facility into a defendant who was directed toward the exact location that became the basis for his conviction, by the same officials who then arrested him for being there.

This is, in the vocabulary of federal constitutional law, potentially both a Brady violation — the suppression of evidence favorable to a defendant — and a claim under California v. Trombetta and Arizona v. Youngblood, the line of Supreme Court cases governing a defendant's due process rights when the government fails to preserve potentially exculpatory evidence. This book's own analysis, informed by the appellate strategy Reardon and his advisors have developed, treats this spoliation argument as considerably stronger than a pure First Amendment retaliation claim standing alone — not because the First Amendment issues are meritless, but because a structural due-process argument grounded in missing evidence does not require a reviewing court to resolve the harder, more doctrinally contested question of how much First Amendment protection courthouse-lobby recording deserves. It only requires the court to determine whether the government's failure to preserve potentially exculpatory footage, footage the defense contends would show the very obstruction charge being manufactured by the government's own personnel, deprived Reardon of a fair trial.

This chapter closes on the same note this book has struck consistently since Chapter 27: two things are true simultaneously. Reardon's own conduct on August 25, 2025 — the tripod, the language, the livestream caption — was not, on this book's own honest accounting, blameless. And the trial that convicted him rests on a foundation this book cannot verify is complete, because the government itself has admitted the foundation is missing pieces, pieces the defense contends would have changed the entire narrative of what happened that day. A conviction obtained on an incomplete evidentiary record, against a defendant federal law enforcement had already been tracking for two months before the incident that produced the charge, is not automatically an unjust conviction — but it is a conviction this book cannot call clean, and it is the conviction Reardon will be sentenced on August 7, 2026, the event that closes this Part and this book's account of the federal case.

Before reaching that sentencing, though, this book turns to two other fronts that opened while the federal case was still working its way toward trial: a brutal assault at New Orleans City Hall in November 2025, and a separate, ongoing confrontation with officials in Union County, Mississippi that would not begin until the following spring.
