CHAPTER 37 — REARDON V. OSTEEN: THE OBJECTIONS

On July 14, 2026, a federal magistrate judge entered a Report and Recommendation in Reardon v. Osteen — the consolidated federal civil rights lawsuit, filed in the Southern District of Texas before Judge Jeffrey V. Brown and referred to Magistrate Judge Andrew M. Edison, that names the Galveston officials from Chapter 24's affidavit contradiction alongside Circuit Clerk Jeff Busby and Deputy Kandi Beavers from Mississippi as co-defendants in a single pro se action. That R&R resolved seven separate motions at once, and it marks the moment where a federal court, for the first time in this entire eight-year saga, formally engaged with — and in one specific instance, validated — the credibility findings built chapter by chapter since Part I.

The R&R recommended granting the motions to dismiss filed by Busby, by the Galveston defendants (the City, Officer Osteen, and Sergeant Doraty), and by Beavers, largely on jurisdictional and pleading grounds. It also recommended denying my motion for a temporary restraining order connected to a June 22, 2026 vehicle seizure — the towing of a 2016 Mitsubishi Outlander after I was cop-watching at a Galveston Walgreens, held twenty-two hours, and towed to a lot where a supervisor identified as "Ray" told me: "Stand in line, that's what happens to people like you."

What makes this R&R more than another procedural setback, however, is Magistrate Judge Edison's own language comparing Osteen's sworn blood-warrant affidavit against Osteen's later testimony before Judge Tollison — the same contradiction examined in Chapter 24. After discussing the affidavit's "moderate odor of alcohol" and "dilated" pupils alongside later testimony that he could not smell alcohol and that my pupils were "pinpoint," Edison wrote: "I take no pleasure in this result. The record shows that Osteen lied to Judge Tollison. That is disturbing." A federal magistrate judge did not merely note an inconsistency. He used the word "lied" in a written federal recommendation about Osteen's testimony.

Edison also allowed an important part of my vehicle-seizure theory to clear the pleading threshold. He wrote: "Because it was unreasonable to impound Reardon's vehicle, Reardon has stated a constitutional claim for unreasonable seizure." I previously described that sentence too broadly as a final finding, as a matter of law, that my Fourth Amendment rights had been violated. That is not the procedural posture of an R&R deciding motions to dismiss. The precise point is that Edison concluded the allegations were sufficient to state an unreasonable-seizure claim. He did not enter a final merits judgment imposing liability on the officers.

The same distinction explains the result that followed. On the Osteen testimony issue, Edison still recommended dismissal of the malicious-prosecution claim because he concluded that independent probable cause existed for the DWI arrest regardless of the disputed alcohol observations. On the impoundment claim, even after concluding that I had stated a constitutional claim, he recommended qualified immunity because I had not identified sufficiently analogous preexisting authority clearly establishing the right in the particular circumstances he analyzed. The R&R cited Degenhardt v. Bintliff, a Fifth Circuit decision that postdated my August 2023 arrest, as part of that discussion.

That combination is legally important precisely because it is not the simple victory I once wanted it to be. The R&R contains an express judicial statement that Osteen lied to Judge Tollison, yet recommends dismissal of the claim for which that lie was relevant because of Edison's independent-probable-cause analysis. It also says I stated a constitutional unreasonable-seizure claim, yet recommends immunity for the officers. A judicial recognition of troubling conduct and a plaintiff's loss can coexist in the same decision. In this case, they did.

I, still pro se, filed nine objections to this R&R on July 28, 2026 — on the fourteen-day deadline, file-stamped that same day by Clerk of Court Nathan Ochsner. Six of those objections, plus a ninth, proceeded under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 72(b), which calls for de novo review of properly objected-to portions of a dispositive recommendation. One of my central qualified-immunity arguments relied on Hope v. Pelzer and the "obviousness" principle — the argument that clearly established law does not always require a prior case with identical facts where the unlawfulness would have been apparent to a reasonable official.

That framing mattered to me because Degenhardt was decided after my August 2023 arrest. A later decision cannot itself have supplied notice to officers before the conduct occurred. My objection therefore argued that the court should evaluate the seizure under the body of law that existed at the time, including the obviousness principle, rather than treat the later Degenhardt decision as though it had been available to the officers in 2023.

My objections also addressed my First Amendment retaliation, excessive-force, Doraty-related, Busby, sanctions, and supplemental-pleading issues. Some requests sought leave to amend rather than an immediate merits judgment. Two objections proceeded under Rule 72(a)'s more deferential review standard for nondispositive rulings. The standards mattered because not every ruling in the magistrate proceedings reached the district judge under the same level of review.

The objection I considered most important was the third. I asked the district court to preserve in the appellate record Edison's statement — "the record shows that Osteen lied to Judge Tollison. That is disturbing" — together with the defendants' paragraph-14 concession discussed in Chapter 24. I also requested a narrow Franks hearing directed to materiality. In that objection I expressly acknowledged that the R&R's independent-probable-cause analysis was substantive; I was not pretending Edison had ruled the entire malicious-prosecution claim in my favor.

How the district court would rule was not known when an earlier version of this chapter was drafted. It is known now. I obtained the Order Adopting Magistrate Judge's Memorandum and Recommendation (Dkt. 93) and the Final Judgment (Dkt. 94), both signed by Judge Jeffrey Vincent Brown and entered August 10, 2026. Judge Brown wrote that the court had carefully considered the objections, the memorandum and recommendation, the pleadings, and the record, and that it accepted Judge Edison's recommendation and adopted it as the opinion of the court.

The order granted the motions to dismiss filed by Busby, the Galveston defendants, and Beavers; dismissed Hoby James, Galveston County, and Doe defendants for failure to state a claim; declined supplemental jurisdiction over state-law claims; and denied the injunctive-relief request connected to the June 22 vehicle seizure. The Final Judgment dismissed the matter.

Judge Brown's order also addressed my request to amend again. It noted that Magistrate Judge Edison had already granted leave for a second amended complaint in April 2026 and concluded: "The court will not permit Reardon to file a third amended complaint." Beyond adopting the R&R, the district order did not separately walk through each of my nine objections.

That absence of individualized discussion is not proof that the court ignored the objections, and I am not presenting it as such. District judges may adopt a magistrate judge's recommendation without writing a separate essay on every objection. What the order establishes is narrower: the R&R was adopted, the objections did not change the result, and the case ended at the district-court level with a final judgment against me.

The result leaves both sides of the record in place. I lost the case at that stage. At the same time, the adopted R&R contains Edison's explicit statement that Osteen lied to Judge Tollison and his conclusion that my allegations stated an unreasonable-seizure claim before qualified immunity was applied. Neither fact should be inflated into something it is not, and neither should be omitted because the final judgment went against me.
