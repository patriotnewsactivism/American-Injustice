CHAPTER 37 — REARDON V. OSTEEN: THE OBJECTIONS

On July 14, 2026, Magistrate Judge Andrew M. Edison entered a Memorandum Opinion, Order, and Recommendation in Reardon v. Osteen, Civil Action No. 3:25-CV-00203 in the Southern District of Texas.

The case brought together several strands already documented in this book: my claims arising from the August 2023 Galveston DWI arrest; claims against Galveston defendants including Officer William Osteen; and claims against Mississippi defendants including Lafayette County Circuit Clerk Jeff Busby and Deputy Kandis Beavers.

The July 14 decision was mostly adverse to me. Edison recommended granting the dispositive motions to dismiss, recommended dismissal of additional defendants, and recommended denial of the temporary restraining order connected to my June 2026 vehicle-seizure dispute. He also entered rulings on nondispositive motions.

But inside that adverse ruling was a sentence that cannot be summarized away.

In discussing the conflict between Osteen's warrant affidavit and his later testimony before Judge Gray Tollison, Edison wrote: "The record shows that Osteen lied to Judge Tollison. That is disturbing."

That is the language of the federal magistrate judge. It is not my characterization of Osteen's credibility, and it is not a criminal perjury adjudication. The court said Osteen lied to Judge Tollison. It did not convict him of perjury or make the additional findings a perjury prosecution would require.

The R&R nevertheless recommended dismissal of the search-related claim. Edison concluded that, even after setting aside the disputed alcohol-related statements, other allegations supplied independent probable cause for the blood-draw warrant. My later objections identified the remaining facts as failure to maintain a single lane, watery eyes, and refusal of a field-sobriety test. I disputed that analysis and requested a Franks hearing directed to materiality, but I did not pretend the R&R had resolved the claim in my favor.

The vehicle-impoundment analysis produced a different combination of findings.

My July 28 objections quote the R&R as concluding that I had stated a viable Fourth Amendment claim for unreasonable seizure and as rejecting the community-caretaking justification on the record before the court. The R&R then recommended qualified immunity because I had not adequately addressed the clearly-established-law component of the defense or supplied controlling authority establishing the right at the required level of specificity.

That procedural distinction is essential. Saying that a complaint states a constitutional claim is not the same thing as entering judgment that a defendant violated the Constitution. And a qualified-immunity ruling can end a damages claim even after the court concludes that the pleaded conduct is constitutionally problematic.

I filed my objections on July 28, 2026. The document separated dispositive recommendations, reviewed under Rule 72(b), from nondispositive rulings reviewed under Rule 72(a). My first objection challenged the qualified-immunity recommendation on the vehicle seizure and invoked Hope v. Pelzer's notice principle. I argued that a later appellate decision could not itself have given officers notice before the conduct occurred and asked the district court either to reject qualified immunity or permit supplemental briefing.

My third objection preserved the Osteen credibility finding and challenged the R&R's materiality analysis. I quoted Edison's sentence that Osteen lied to Judge Tollison and also cited the defendants' own reply brief as conceding that Osteen had stated on camera that he could not smell alcohol. I asked for a Franks hearing while expressly acknowledging that Edison's independent-probable-cause reasoning was a substantive obstacle.

The objections also exposed a correction that belongs in this manuscript's treatment of Jeff Busby.

I had previously argued that Busby's March 25, 2024 letter concerning the Mississippi appeal and his later federal affidavit were directly contradictory. The R&R did not accept that theory. My own July 28 Objection No. 6 states that Edison's personal-jurisdiction analysis relied on affidavits establishing that Assistant District Attorney Steven Jubera—not Busby—personally transmitted the March 25 letter to Texas authorities. Objection No. 7 further records that Edison denied my motion to strike Busby's affidavit and for sanctions because the record showed Jubera personally contacted Texas, meaning Busby's affidavit was not literally false on that point.

That is now the controlling way this book treats the issue. The March 2024 letter remains relevant to the history of the Mississippi appeal, but the present record does not support saying that Busby personally sent it to Galveston or committed perjury by denying that he did. A prior draft said otherwise. The court record corrected it.

The remaining objections addressed First Amendment retaliation, excessive force, claims against Sergeant Doraty, the Busby jurisdiction issue, the motion to strike, the attempted supplemental complaint, and the temporary restraining order. In several places I asked for dismissal without prejudice and leave to amend rather than claiming entitlement to immediate judgment.

The district court did not accept the objections. On August 10, 2026, Judge Jeffrey Vincent Brown entered an order adopting the magistrate judge's recommendation and a final judgment dismissing the case at the district-court level. The dismissal meant I lost the civil action in that court.

That result does not erase the findings embedded in the adopted R&R. It also does not transform those findings into judgments they were not.

The durable record is more complicated: the court adopted a recommendation that ended my case; the same recommendation expressly said Osteen lied to Judge Tollison; and it concluded that my vehicle-seizure allegations stated a constitutional claim before qualified immunity defeated the damages claim.

Those propositions can coexist. In fact, preserving all of them is the only accurate way to report the decision.

Three days before Judge Brown entered final judgment, I had been sentenced in the separate federal misdemeanor case arising from the Lafayette courthouse arrest.

That August 7 sentencing is the next chapter.
