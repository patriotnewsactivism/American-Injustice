CHAPTER 11 — THE FEBRUARY 2021 DUI SETUP

On February 21, 2021, at approximately 1:54 a.m., Deputies Jacob Williford and Kandis Beavers saw my 2013 Kia traveling east on Jackson Avenue with one headlight illuminated.

That is the reason for the stop in Williford's report. He did not write that I was weaving, speeding, or driving erratically. He turned around, followed me onto Hathorn Road near the Ole Miss softball fields, and initiated the traffic stop because of the headlight.

My wife was in the vehicle with me.

Williford wrote that when he approached the driver's side, he smelled what he believed, based on training and experience, to be burned marijuana. He asked whether there was anything illegal inside. I said there was not. After I stepped out, he had my wife step out as well and searched the vehicle. His report says he found no contraband.

That part of the officer's report is important because my own recording of the stop substantially tracks it. I did not deny that I had smoked marijuana earlier in the evening. Beavers's report says I told her I had smoked before 11:00 p.m., that my wife had contacted me because she had left medication at home, and that I had taken "one hit out of a one hitter" before leaving to bring the medication to her.

I also told the deputies I did not believe I was impaired.

Beavers wrote that she could smell marijuana from the vehicle but not from me once we were standing away from it. Because I admitted smoking earlier, she told me she wanted to administer tests to determine whether I was safe to drive.

The weather complicated that plan. Both reports describe below-freezing temperatures and snow or ice on the ground. Rather than conduct the tests roadside, Beavers said I would be transported to the Lafayette County Detention Center's garage, commonly called the sallyport, for the testing.

I objected. Beavers's own report says I became upset and told her I did not consent to being transported for the tests. I asked for a supervisor. Lieutenant John Dukes arrived. According to Beavers, Dukes told me I would have to go to the detention center to perform the tests. Beavers then told me that, if she concluded I was able to drive after the tests, I would be returned to my vehicle. Her report says I agreed to go after that explanation.

I was handcuffed for transport. My wife was allowed to leave with the Kia.

At the sallyport, Beavers removed the handcuffs and administered field-sobriety testing. Her report is more detailed than the simplified version I told for years.

On horizontal gaze nystagmus, she recorded no clues.

On a modified Romberg balance test, she wrote that I estimated thirty seconds in thirty-five seconds and displayed eyelid and body tremors and swaying.

On lack of convergence, she wrote that my left eye did not converge as expected.

On the walk-and-turn, she recorded four of eight clues, including difficulty maintaining balance during instructions, missing heel-to-toe, stepping off line, and making an improper turn.

On the one-leg stand, she recorded two clues: using my arms for balance and swaying.

After the tests, Beavers asked me to rate myself from zero to ten, with zero sober and ten the highest I had ever been. Her report says I answered zero.

She concluded I was not safe to drive and arrested me for DUI first offense. Williford also cited me for the expired tag and improper equipment.

The Sheriff's report lists no physical evidence recovered from the vehicle. Williford expressly wrote that he found no contraband. Beavers's report lists "EVIDENCE: n/a." I was not charged with possession of marijuana or paraphernalia from this stop.

That is the factual core of the case. It is more complicated than saying the deputies simply invented an odor, and it is also more complicated than saying the officers had objective proof of impairment. I admitted recent marijuana use. No contraband was found. Beavers recorded some clues on some tests and none on HGN. I rated myself sober. The central question became whether the observations were enough to prove DUI beyond a reasonable doubt.

The reason the word "setup" remains in the title is not because the report itself proves the stop was staged. It does not. The title reflects how I came to view the case after the evidence-preservation dispute that followed. That distinction needs to stay visible.

Neither deputy had a dash camera or body camera recording the roadside encounter. The actual field-sobriety tests were performed inside the detention-center sallyport, an area covered by the Sheriff's Department's fixed camera system. When the case reached trial, that footage had not been preserved.

I requested video evidence before trial. In later testimony, Sheriff Joey East described the detention-center system as ordinarily retaining footage for about seven days unless it was preserved. He also acknowledged that other Sheriff's Department footage from a different incident involving me had been affirmatively preserved. Chapter 14 deals with that testimony in detail, because earlier versions of this book incorrectly put the phrase "gone forever" in East's mouth. He did not use those exact words in the trial audio I later reviewed.

What the missing sallyport video would have shown cannot now be known. I believe it would have supported my account of how I performed on the tests. Beavers's written report says otherwise on several components. The absence of the recording leaves the disagreement unresolved rather than automatically proving either side.

The Justice Court ultimately convicted me of DUI first offense, improper equipment, and expired/no tag. The certified abstracts identify the offense date as February 21, 2021, the trial date as November 4, 2021, and the judgment date as November 24, 2021. The DUI abstract lists a $400 fine plus $404 in assessments. The improper-equipment abstract lists a $30 fine plus $116 in assessments.

I appealed those convictions.

The DUI stop was only one thread in what became a difficult 2021.

On March 23, 2021, Crowder and I entered another agreed order in the Chancery custody case. The order adjusted the litigation between us, maintained child support at $400 per month, dismissed the guardian ad litem, and contained restrictions intended to reduce the cycle of public and CPS accusations. Whatever the order was intended to accomplish, the relationship deteriorated again. In later Chancery pleadings I stated that communication and visitation with Lydia stopped around June 5. A June 19 Sheriff's report records me going to Crowder's property around Father's Day seeking my daughter, being told to leave, and telling Crowder to "fuck off." I told the deputies I had recorded the encounter.

That is another fact I will not clean up for publication. I was angry and profane. The custody conflict was escalating again.

There was also a separate crisis in my household during 2021. After an argument with Madelyn, police became involved and I was arrested on a disturbance matter. During that episode officers reported finding a marijuana pipe in a vehicle. I was later sent for a short stay at the Tupelo Crisis Center.

I have previously placed that incident confidently in July 2021 and described the stay as roughly two weeks. The event itself is real, but the exact date has not yet been verified from an arrest report, docket entry, or discharge record in the source set. I am therefore not fixing a specific date to it in this edition until the underlying record is located. I also remember the disturbance and paraphernalia matters being retired to the file rather than prosecuted to conviction, but that procedural status should be tied to the Calhoun County record before publication.

The Calhoun County matter later entered the Chancery case anyway.

On September 14, 2021, Chancellor Robert Q. Whitwell entered an order requiring me to submit to a nail-bed drug test and suspending visitation pending further order. The written order acknowledged that the paraphernalia charge had been "retired to the file pending Mr. Reardon's good behavior in Calhoun County" and stated that the drug test was not being ordered to prove or incriminate me on that charge.

I objected on constitutional grounds and sought recusal and appellate review. I argued that the court was using an inactive criminal matter as a basis to compel testing in the custody case. Whitwell rejected my recusal position. The Mississippi Supreme Court later denied the mandamus relief I sought and denied my request to proceed in forma pauperis. My underlying Fourth and Fifth Amendment theory about the drug test did not produce a merits ruling in my favor.

Earlier versions of this chapter described the Supreme Court episode too selectively. Part of my appeal ultimately failed over costs, but the recusal issue was actually addressed and decided against me. That belongs in the record just as plainly as the issues I believe the courts never reached.

By September 2021 I was litigating simultaneously against criminal convictions, custody restrictions, and evidence-preservation problems. I had also begun identifying myself publicly as a journalist and recording government encounters with increasing frequency.

The next confrontation moved to a federal building in Oxford, where I went looking for a federal audience for the complaints I believed local institutions would not address.
