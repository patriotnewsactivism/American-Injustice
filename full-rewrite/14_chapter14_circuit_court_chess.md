CHAPTER 14 — CIRCUIT COURT CHESS GAME

On December 6, 2021, I confronted Circuit Clerk Jeff Busby over the Smith-to-Luther judge swap first raised in Chapter 8 — the unexplained substitution of Judge John Kelly Luther for Judge Kent Smith on my 2020 post-conviction relief matter, a swap I say I watched happen not once but twice, with no stated reason on the record either time.

I filmed both halves of that morning. For a long time I did not realize I still had the footage; it turned up in a batch of my own recordings nobody had gone through. It changes this chapter, and not entirely in my favor, so I am going to give you the clerk's office first and the sheriff second, in the order they happened.

**Inside the clerk's office.** I walk in with the camera running and greet the deputy clerk by name. Before I ask anything, I say this, twice, unprompted:

> *"It's nothing with y'all. Just to preface that... I don't believe it's anything with y'all."*

I ask them to pull up my newest case — cause number L214964 — and tell me if anything stands out about it. And then I put the question that this whole chapter is about, in front of the person who had answered it four days earlier:

> *"You told me on Friday that it was assigned to Kent Smith. And I said I wanted to make sure that it wasn't gonna get switched to Luther again, because it was switched in the system to Luther last year on my post conviction relief."*

That is the fact. It is on tape, said to the clerk who had said it to me, and not contradicted by anyone in the room.

Now, in fairness, what they said back — because a book that quotes only its own side has no standing to complain when the State does it. They told me, repeatedly and not unreasonably, that this was not theirs to answer: *"We can't do anything about that... that's out of our scope."* *"Only thing we can do is file what you bring in here. We don't have any influence with any judges whatsoever. We're not attorneys."* And, in the middle of it: *"I'm not trying to be rude to you."*

They were not being rude. They were telling me the truth about the limits of a clerk's office, and I said so on camera: *"I wasn't trying to pass the blame to y'all... I tried to be as cordial as possible."*

Two things did happen in that room, though. When I asked whether they had ever seen the same reassignment happen to anybody else, the answer was: *"I'm not gonna answer you."* And somewhere in that exchange comes the instruction that ends the conversation — *"Don't answer."* The audio interleaves voices badly enough that I will not tell you with certainty which man said which sentence, and I would rather admit that than assign a line to Jeff Busby that might not be his.

What I can tell you, because the camera was on my own face and hands the whole time, is the temperature of that encounter. I did not raise my voice. I did not refuse to leave. The worst thing I said in that office was that I was sick of the bullshit and that it was my life on the line. Then I asked whether Judge Luther happened to be in the building that day, was told he was not, and I left.

Hold that, because of what happened in the parking lot.

**Outside, at my car.** Sheriff Joey East pulled in behind me, blocking me, then parked alongside. That conversation is on video too, and East's own account of why he was there is the reason this footage matters:

> *"You just gotta call me up here harassing people..."*
>
> *"Filing paperwork and all that's fine... Don't go back in there harassing."*
>
> *"You're disturbing their business."*
>
> *"You came here looking for trouble."*

I said I had been petitioning the government, and that I had told them plainly I did not think the problem was theirs. When I said the words *"to ask a question,"* East's answer, as best the audio renders it, was that they don't answer questions about this stuff.

Sit with the line East drew, because he drew it himself and he drew it clearly: **filing paperwork is fine; asking a question is harassment.** A sheriff told a citizen, in a public parking lot outside a public courthouse, that he was welcome to hand documents across a counter but not to ask what had happened to his case. There is no version of the First Amendment in which that is the correct division.

And then compare his stated reason to the other tape. East said I was harassing people, disturbing their business, and came looking for trouble. The recording of what happened in that office minutes earlier has me telling the staff three separate times that my complaint was not with them, and has a staff member telling me he was not trying to be rude. Both recordings are of the same morning. They do not describe the same event. I cannot rule out that East was told something about earlier visits that is not on this tape — but on the morning in question, the sheriff's account and the video do not match.

One more sentence of his, which I am going to quote and then not build anything on top of:

> *"I would suggest you that you calm down before something happens."*

He said that on December 6. On December 7 he signed an affidavit seeking my involuntary commitment. I am putting those two facts next to each other because they are both true and both dated, and I am stopping there, because the next chapter explains at length why I am not going to claim the first sentence caused the second.

**And now the part I would leave out if I were writing propaganda.** I did not conduct myself well in that parking lot. I called Joey East a tyrant — not once, but over and over, eight or nine times, talking over him. I demanded he comment on what I called his fake investigation. I told him I was glad he had nothing better to do than mess with me. And I ended the encounter by telling the sheriff of Lafayette County to go f*** himself, and then saying it again.

That is on my tape, filmed by me, and it is in this book because I filmed it. It does not make East's line about paperwork and questions any more constitutional. It does mean that when he wrote, the following day, that this man needed to be evaluated, he had spent the previous afternoon being sworn at in a parking lot. A reader is entitled to weigh that. I would rather hand it to you than have it found.

What is captured as well, on a phone call I placed later that same day to Judge Luther's own court administrator, Kathy, is a version of events close enough in time to the confrontation to serve as a real-time account rather than a reconstructed one. On that call, I raised the judge-swap issue directly, and pressed the point that a Rule 59 rehearing motion I had filed just days earlier, on December 3, had been assigned to Judge Kent Smith and then silently reassigned to Judge Luther over the following weekend — the exact substitution pattern first raised in Chapter 8, now confirmed happening a second time, with no explanation entered on the record either time. Two things Kathy said on that call are worth quoting exactly, because they are a court official's own description of how the system is supposed to work. Asked how my case had moved from one judge to another, she said assignment is handled by the clerk's office and is "a computer generated, random assignment on civil matters." She did not dispute that the case had been assigned to Smith and then sat with Luther. She simply could not tell me how. Pressed further, she gave me the most honest sentence anyone in that building gave me that year: *"I'm just a dead end for you."*

I want to be fair to her and to the deputy clerk, because neither of them did anything to me. Kathy apologized twice, told me she was "not a Juris doctor," and stayed on a call she had no obligation to take while her phones rang off the hook. The deputy clerk — identified in the available record only by the first name China — had, by my account, told me plainly on the Friday that the case was assigned to Judge Kent Smith, which is the correct and helpful thing for a clerk to do. Neither woman is a bad actor in this story. They are the two people in the courthouse who answered me.

What changed was the instruction they were given. Busby, per my account and per what the recording captures me saying contemporaneously, told the deputy clerk *"don't answer any questions"* — I described the moment to Kathy that morning as "very, very sketchy," and told her I had said to Busby, in the office, that I was not trying to point the finger at anyone and only wanted answers. Then, in my words on that same call, Busby "got on his cell phone. Apparently, he called the Lafayette County sheriff."

I record Busby's conduct as I experienced it and no further. One recording of one morning is not a pattern, and this book does not convert a single bad encounter into a character. What is documented after it, because it left its own physical trace in the timeline, is what came next: East physically blocked my car from leaving the courthouse premises, and barred me from Circuit Court.

This is worth pausing on as its own discrete fact, separate from whatever was said in the Busby confrontation that preceded it. A sheriff physically blocking a private citizen's vehicle, and barring that citizen from a public courthouse's circuit court, is an extraordinary escalation — the kind of action that ordinarily requires either an active safety threat or a specific court order restricting the person's access, neither of which the available record establishes as having existed at that moment. Whatever I said to Busby about the judge swap, and whatever Busby's response was, the sheriff's response — physical obstruction of a vehicle and an exclusion from a public court — is a response to a citizen's question about judicial assignment irregularities that goes well beyond what any legitimate law-enforcement interest in the exchange could plausibly justify.

This is also the moment in the timeline where two threads from earlier chapters need to be held together, because the following day's events, detailed in the next chapter, cannot be understood without both of them. The first thread is the years-long pattern of conflict traced since Chapter 3 — a pattern in which nearly every significant escalation between Lafayette County's institutions and me traces back, in one way or another, to my raising a documented irregularity the institution in question had no clean answer for, this judge swap now among them. The second thread is the pattern, developing in real time by December 2021, of those same institutions responding to inconvenient questions not with answers but with escalation — a pattern that reaches, in this chapter, its most physically direct form yet: a car blocked, a courthouse barred, on the strength of a clerk confrontation nobody outside the two men involved actually witnessed.

None of this is offered as proof that Busby's own explanation, or lack of one, for an unexplained judicial substitution on my case should be dismissed outright — only as context for how seriously to weigh it.

It would be irresponsible to assert, without more direct evidence than currently exists, that East's December 7 decision to seek my commitment (detailed fully in the next chapter) was retaliation for the December 6 Busby confrontation rather than a good-faith response to a genuine mental-health concern East believed he was observing. Sheriffs do have a legitimate, sometimes life-saving role in initiating psychiatric holds for people in genuine crisis, and nothing here suggests, glibly, that every commitment sought by a law enforcement official in this story was necessarily pretextual. What can be said, based on the documented sequence alone, is that the timing is close enough — a confrontation over a judicial irregularity on December 6, followed within twenty-four hours by the same sheriff who had just blocked my car seeking my commitment — that a reasonable reader is entitled to ask the question, even if the available record cannot answer it with certainty.

The next chapter follows that commitment in full: Judge Lawrence Little's signed affidavit, the roughly two-week detention that followed, and what it meant for me as someone who, by December 2021, had spent four and a half years accumulating documented reasons to distrust nearly every official who now held the power to decide whether I was well.
