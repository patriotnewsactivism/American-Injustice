## DEFENDANT'S MOTION TO UNSEAL TRIAL TRANSCRIPT AND LIFT PROTECTIVE ORDER RESTRICTING DISCLOSURE OF MARSHAL COMMUNICATIONS AND SURVEILLANCE-PRESERVATION TESTIMONY

NOW COMES Defendant Matthew Oliver Reardon, appearing pro se, and respectfully moves this Court to (1) unseal the portions of the trial transcript addressing U.S. Marshal Hayden Nugent communications and the preservation status of courthouse lobby surveillance footage, and (2) lift or narrow the Court's protective order of October 15, 2025, to the extent it continues to restrict public disclosure of that testimony. In support, Defendant states as follows:

### I. PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

1. Defendant was convicted after a bench trial on January 16, 2026, of violating 41 C.F.R. § 102-74.390(b) arising from conduct at the John M. Shaw United States Courthouse in Lafayette, Louisiana, on August 25, 2025.

2. On July 16, 2026, the Court denied Defendant's First Amendment motion to dismiss, characterizing the courthouse lobby as a nonpublic forum.

3. Sentencing occurred on August 7, 2026. The Court sentenced Defendant to time served (approximately 13 days) plus two years of unsupervised probation, with a special condition barring physical presence at the John M. Shaw courthouse and any other federal courthouse in the State of Louisiana absent "legitimate business" and two business days' advance notice to the U.S. Marshals Service. No fine was imposed and the special assessment was waived based on the Court's finding of indigency. Defense counsel objected on the record that the "legitimate business" condition is unconstitutionally vague; the objection was overruled.

4. On October 15, 2025, the government moved for — and the Court entered — a protective order restricting communications regarding Deputy U.S. Marshal contacts with court staff. That order was sought and framed as a measure to protect the *integrity of pretrial and trial proceedings*, not as a permanent seal on the underlying facts.

5. During the sealed proceedings, testimony was elicited establishing that the government did not preserve the entirety of the courthouse lobby's surveillance footage from the events at issue — footage Defendant contends would show marshals directing him toward the very door he was later convicted of obstructing.

### II. ARGUMENT

**A. The presumption of public access attaches to criminal trial records, and no compelling interest survives sentencing.**

The public and press enjoy both a common-law and First Amendment right of access to criminal trial proceedings and the records generated by them. *Nixon v. Warner Communications, Inc.*, 435 U.S. 589 (1978); *Press-Enterprise Co. v. Superior Court*, 478 U.S. 1 (1986). Sealing is permissible only where a compelling interest requires it and the seal is narrowly tailored to that interest. The interest the government originally identified — protecting the pretrial and trial process from prejudicial pre-verdict disclosure — necessarily expires once trial concludes and sentencing is imposed. No continuing prosecutorial interest is served by shielding from public view sworn testimony about a federal agency's own evidence-preservation practices.

**B. The spoliation testimony is a matter of significant public interest, not simply personal grievance.**

Defendant is a working journalist and First Amendment auditor whose coverage of law-enforcement and court-security practices is itself the subject matter underlying this prosecution. Testimony bearing on whether federal marshals failed to preserve surveillance footage of the very encounter giving rise to a criminal conviction implicates the integrity of the judicial process generally, not merely Defendant's individual case. Continued sealing under these circumstances would insulate from public scrutiny the government's own conduct in preserving (or failing to preserve) evidence in a federal prosecution — the paradigm case for public access.

**C. Unsealing is necessary to permit Defendant to prepare and support his appeal.**

Under Fed. R. Crim. P. 58(g)(2), Defendant's appeal clock runs from the entry of judgment following sentencing. Defendant's principal appellate ground is a structural due-process/spoliation argument under *Arizona v. Youngblood*, 488 U.S. 51 (1988), and *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963) — namely, that the government's failure to preserve exculpatory lobby footage deprived him of evidence material to his defense that marshals directed him toward the door he was convicted of obstructing. Full public access to the sealed testimony is necessary both to permit effective appellate briefing and to allow public verification of the factual basis for that argument.

**D. Alternative, narrower relief.**

Should the Court decline full unsealing, Defendant alternatively requests that the Court unseal only those specific transcript pages addressing (1) the preservation status of the lobby footage, and (2) the ex parte marshal-to-court-staff communications of June 24 and June 26, 2025, while leaving any other sealed matter undisturbed.

### III. CONCLUSION

For the foregoing reasons, Defendant respectfully requests that this Court unseal the trial transcript testimony concerning surveillance-footage preservation and marshal communications, or in the alternative grant the narrower relief described above, and grant such other relief as the Court deems just.
